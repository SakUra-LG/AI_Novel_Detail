The Eight Immortals Cross the Sea

By the time the Eight Immortals reached the shore of the East Sea, the wind was driving row after row of waves onto the beach. Tieguai Li stopped first and planted his iron crutch in the sand. “That's the end of the road.”

Zhang Guolao twisted around and looked backward over his shoulder. “There is a road. It just happens to be made entirely of water.”

“That means there isn't a road.”

“Only if you insist on looking at it right-side up. Turn yourself around and there are roads everywhere under heaven.”

Lü Dongbin had heard enough. “Work out which way up *you* go before you start giving directions to the rest of the world.”

An old fisherman had been hauling in his net nearby. Hearing the argument, he came over and asked where they meant to go. Han Zhongli pointed across the water and said they were crossing the East Sea. The fisherman looked at the eight travelers, then at his little boat. His face took on the expression of a man whose livelihood had just won an enormous piece of business and might sink under it on the spot.

“The boat will carry you,” he said. “It'll just be a tight fit.”

Lan Caihe hugged the flower basket to his chest. “I don't take up much room. My flowers do.”

Han Xiangzi said, “Neither does my flute.”

Cao Guojiu straightened his robes and hat. “People may be squeezed. Propriety may not.”

He Xiangu laughed. “At this rate, propriety will get the boat and all eight of us will stay here to wave it off.”

The old fisherman could not tell whether they were making plans or ruining them, so he tried again. “The East Sea is rough today. If you truly mean to cross, a boat is still the safest way.”

Tieguai Li shook his head. It was not that they looked down on a mortal boat. They simply refused to cram eight people aboard the tiny craft on which the fisherman depended for his living, then make him risk his life ferrying them across. Han Zhongli opened his palm-leaf fan against the wind. Zhang Guolao patted his fish drum; Lü Dongbin rested a hand on his sword; He Xiangu held her lotus; Lan Caihe lifted the flower basket; Han Xiangzi brought out his jade flute; and Cao Guojiu unfastened his jade tablet. Each of them carried a skill of their own. If they still made the fisherman's boat groan under their weight, even its planks would laugh when the tale got around.

“No boat,” Lü Dongbin said. “Each of us will cross by our own art. No one is going to borrow a mortal's livelihood just to show off an immortal's power.”

Zhang Guolao asked, “May I borrow another immortal's power?”

“Not yet. Wait until yours runs out.”

“I'll remember you said that. It may become useful when you pretend you didn't.”

They all agreed. The fisherman climbed to higher ground, still uneasy, and told them to come back if the waves turned dangerous. The Eight Immortals did not make fun of his concern. They thanked him in earnest before taking their places at the tide line.

Water washed over the sand and erased the footprints they had just made. None of the eight retreated, but no one rushed to fling a treasure into the sea either. If displaying their powers meant nothing more than racing to go first, seven immortals would be left with nothing to do but watch. So they settled on an order, giving each traveler room to make a path of their own.

Aman crouched beside a reef with his green bamboo slips in his arms. He wrote down every name from Tieguai Li to Cao Guojiu, then counted them all again. Lan Caihe leaned over. “Have you got eight?”

“I have. But if you keep wandering back and forth, this story may end up being called *Sixteen Immortals Cross the Sea*.”

He Xiangu pulled Lan Caihe back into place. “Stand still. Don't inflate the population for the historian.”

Aman spread a green slip across the damp rock. His brush picked up a little salt from the sea mist, and he had barely written the words “No Boat” before he rolled the slip up again and edged backward, afraid a wave might snatch it. His toe struck a smooth shell in the fine sand. He nearly slipped, caught himself, and froze, as though even a stumble might disturb the eight immortals before him.

Out at sea, the waves rose one after another like curious children craning for a better look. White foam rolled silver in the sunset. Every swell seemed to be studying the eight immortals who had refused a mortal boat, wondering what treasures they could possibly use to force a way across.

Tieguai Li took the gourd from his shoulder and weighed it in his palm. “If everyone is going to stand here waiting, someone may as well go first.”

“Easy now,” Han Zhongli said. “I haven't worked out what mood my fan is in today.”

Tieguai Li gave a grunt and moved to the front. The other seven did not help; they simply cleared a space for him. The first treasure of the Eight Immortals was about to leave the shore.

Tieguai Li pulled the stopper from his gourd and sniffed it first, making quite sure it did not contain the remains of last night's wine. Only then did he toss it onto the sea.

The gourd swelled in the wind and settled firmly on a bright patch of water. Leaning on his iron crutch, Tieguai Li stepped aboard. He had just turned to say, “Watch carefully,” when the gourd revolved beneath him and left him facing the shore.

Zhang Guolao called from behind, “Are you setting out or coming back?”

Tieguai Li ignored him. He planted the tip of his crutch in the water and tried to steer. The gourd obediently turned another half circle. Now it faced the right way, but the next wave pushed rider and vessel straight back to where they had begun.

Lan Caihe observed, “Very steady. Not going anywhere, but extremely steady.”

“Quiet.” Tieguai Li braced the crutch across his body. When the next wave arrived, he struck the water and borrowed its force. At last the gourd cleared the breakers and slid out to sea. A crosswind caught it at once, however, and its course began to wriggle like a snake that had suddenly remembered it had left its brain at home.

Han Zhongli stepped forward and opened his palm-leaf fan. “I'll give you a push.”

He meant to send the wind neatly behind the gourd. His first stroke was far too strong. Sand exploded along the shore; waves flattened across the sea; and Tieguai Li shot away with the gourd, only to spin like a top at the far edge of the gust.

“Are you sending me across the sea,” Tieguai Li shouted from the distance, “or looking for a spare corner to put me in?”

Han Zhongli hurriedly lowered the fan. Zhang Guolao offered a thoughtful assessment. “A little harder and you could send him back to yesterday.”

Tieguai Li pressed his crutch against one side of the gourd and finally stopped the spinning. He did not put on a brave face and claim he needed no help. Instead, he waved the crutch toward shore. “Less wind. Aim it at my back.”

Han Zhongli tried a thread of breeze. The gourd moved forward, then a wave knocked it sideways. Tieguai Li did not tell him to push harder; he used the crutch to nudge the gourd back on course. One adjusted the wind while the other corrected the heading. After several attempts, they found a method that did not require either of them to spend all his time undoing the other's work.

Han Zhongli stopped using the fan like a broom. He studied the wind over the water and moved it gently. The breeze came up behind the gourd, supporting it without stealing its direction or driving it too fast. Tieguai Li traveled more steadily now. An occasional tap of the crutch was enough to avoid an oncoming wave.

Once the ride became smooth, Tieguai Li decided the sea had grown too quiet. He looked back. “If you'd used the fan like that from the beginning, I could have saved myself several turns.”

“If you hadn't taken those turns, I wouldn't have known where to aim.”

“So the credit is mine after all.”

“Take all the credit you like. Just don't bring the circles back with you.”

When Tieguai Li reached the deeper water, Han Zhongli stepped forward. His palm-leaf fan opened wide. The wind rising beneath it supported his robes and his feet alike, and he walked on the current he had made, clearing one wave with every stroke of the fan.

Tieguai Li glanced back. “That looks comfortable.”

“Of course it is. I tested it on you first.”

“You admit I was your fan-testing dummy?”

“I admit only that your spinning proved extremely useful.”

Their voices blew back to shore, where the other six immortals burst out laughing. Yet while the jokes flew, Tieguai Li's gourd never stopped and Han Zhongli's fan never lost control. One kept his course with an iron crutch; the other rode forward on a gentle wind. Each made it safely beyond the roughest water near the beach.

Only now did those on shore fully understand. “Each showing their own powers” did not mean standing in a row and flashing their treasures. It meant trusting those treasures enough to walk straight into the East Sea. Zhang Guolao wandered to the tide line. “The first two have tested the sea. Time to let the sea test me.”

He drew a paper donkey from inside his robe. The moment it touched the ground, it grew to the size of a living donkey, flicked its ears, and stopped at the edge of the waves with no intention of taking another step.

Zhang Guolao mounted backward as usual, facing the shore, and patted its back. “Off we go.”

The donkey stayed exactly where it was.

“Don't be afraid. There's nothing ahead but water.”

The paper donkey took one step backward.

“An excellent bit of reassurance,” Lü Dongbin said.

Zhang Guolao had no choice but to beat his fish drum. Its note spread across the sea, and the waves eased with it. Only then did the paper donkey test the water with one hoof. There was no solid ground beneath it, yet it seemed to find an invisible road and began walking out to sea one careful step at a time.

The trouble was that Zhang Guolao, facing the way he had come, could see everyone on shore but none of the waves ahead. When they shouted “Left!” he patted the donkey toward the right. When they shouted “Right!” he insisted he had heard them perfectly well. Under such guidance the donkey hesitated at every turn. Its route was even more polite than Tieguai Li's, as though it meant to pay a formal visit to every wave before passing.

At last Zhang Guolao stopped giving directions and used the drum to listen instead. A sharp wave ahead sent back a heavy echo; smoother water answered softly. The paper donkey adjusted its steps to the rhythm, and little by little its course grew steady.

Once Zhang Guolao had cleared the breakers, Lü Dongbin drew his sword. The blade flew out over the sea, and a streak of light split the oncoming wave down the middle, opening a short passage through the water.

He stepped onto the sword and rose into the air, robes streaming behind him. His departure was far neater than either of the first two. Lan Caihe had just opened his mouth to praise it when the divided wave closed again from behind. Spray chased Lü Dongbin down and soaked the hem of his robe.

Lan Caihe swallowed the compliment and replaced it with another. “Lovely technique. Even the sea is running after you to say goodbye.”

Lü Dongbin looked back. “If you're jealous, you can let it wash your flower basket later.”

He abandoned the notion of splitting the East Sea from shore to shore with a single stroke. Instead, he opened only as much water as he needed immediately ahead. Each time the sea closed, he struck again. Every flash of the sword dealt with the obstacle before him and no more, leaving the gourd and the fan farther ahead undisturbed.

Whenever the paper donkey wandered into the sword's path, Lü Dongbin held his blade and let it pass rather than tearing open a wider channel. When Zhang Guolao heard the drumbeat echo through empty water, he too guided the donkey away from the sword light. Their tongues continued to spar, but neither treated the other's skill as an inconvenience underfoot.

Hearing the water behind him, Zhang Guolao tapped his drum. “Who's caught up?”

“Someone sensible enough to travel facing forward,” Lü Dongbin replied.

“A pity. You can see only the places you haven't reached, never how far you've already come.”

“And you can see everything except the next wave.”

They crossed on, one behind the other. The paper donkey trod the waves while the fish drum sounded their depth; the sword divided the water while Lü Dongbin rode its blade. Neither copied the pair ahead, and neither tried to travel the other's road for him.

Four immortals remained on the shore. He Xiangu watched the two new paths spread across the sea and slowly turned the lotus in her hand. When Lan Caihe saw her step forward, he lifted his flower basket. “Surely it must be the flowers' turn now.”

He Xiangu set her lotus on the water. Its petals opened into a light, steady platform. She stepped onto the flower, held the stem, and glided away on the first wave.

Lan Caihe set the flower basket afloat as well. It rode the water beautifully; the branches inside it were another matter. At the first touch of sea wind, they reached in every direction. Lan Caihe balanced on the rim and waved proudly toward shore. “My road comes with flowers.”

At once, a branch hooked itself around one of He Xiangu's lotus leaves. The lotus jerked backward while the basket kept drifting forward. The two treasures tugged against each other and made miserable progress.

He Xiangu looked over her shoulder. “Are you crossing the sea or picking my flowers?”

Lan Caihe hurried to free the branch. “Just being friendly. My flowers may be friendlier than I am.”

“Then kindly put their friendliness back in the basket.”

Lan Caihe gathered every overenthusiastic branch. Once free of the tangle, the basket moved with the waves again. He Xiangu did not merely wait for calm water. She turned the lotus stem gently and let the flower change direction with each swell. When a wave came from the side, a leaf tilted and let it pass. When a wave rose ahead, the petals lifted her over the crest and set her down softly beyond it.

“If I'd known,” Lan Caihe said, watching her smooth progress, “I'd have brought a lotus too.”

“Manage the basket you have. Add one more flower and the East Sea will have to open a florist's shop.”

A fast current caught the basket and dragged it outward. Lan Caihe reached for the lotus stem, remembered the earlier tangle, and pulled the hand back. Instead, he used the basket handle as a paddle. The basket turned slowly but surely until it ran beside the lotus once more.

He Xiangu did not tow it for him. She only called out what lay ahead. “Broken waves on the left, long swells on the right. Which will you take?”

“Left. If the little waves mean to bully me, at least they'll have to queue.”

They traveled on, one to either side. Lotus and flower basket seemed much alike as vessels, but their riders could not have been more different. He Xiangu moved with the waves instead of wrestling the East Sea. Lan Caihe spent the journey negotiating with the flowers and forbidding them from making friends with anyone else's treasure without permission.

A patch of confused water suddenly thrust the lotus and the basket apart. He Xiangu did not chase after Lan Caihe. She called for him to stop fighting the current, then followed the swell around and turned back from calmer water. Lan Caihe copied her. The detour was longer, but his basket did not catch the lotus again.

“So going around can still count as going forward,” Lan Caihe said.

“Provided you don't go all the way around to the beach.”

“That was Tieguai Li's route. I wouldn't steal it.”

Far ahead, Tieguai Li looked back and shouted, “Is everyone behind us keeping up?”

“We're keeping up,” He Xiangu called. “Lan Caihe's flowers are busier than their owner, that's all.”

Lan Caihe immediately defended the basket. “It's their first sea crossing. They haven't seen much of the world.”

With his back still turned to them, Zhang Guolao laughed. “Don't worry. After this trip, they'll even be able to boast about it backward.”

At last He Xiangu and Lan Caihe caught up with the four ahead. Six immortals had now entered the East Sea by their own abilities. He Xiangu had not been left behind, and no one else had crossed her share of the water for her.

When Han Xiangzi raised the jade flute to his lips, the wind along the shore paused.

The melody traveled over the sea, and the disorderly waves began to settle into a rhythm. Fish gathered beneath the sound, forming a flickering school before him. As he advanced with the music, they swam along either side, revealing the calmer spaces between one swell and the next.

Cao Guojiu nodded. “A fine tune.”

“Praise them any more,” Han Xiangzi said, “and the fish may demand a tip.”

The words had barely left his mouth when the entire school crowded in front of him. A few petals had fallen from Lan Caihe's passing basket. The fish had no ear for refinement and thought the petals sounded far more like lunch than the flute did. In an instant, Han Xiangzi's path dissolved into fins and little choppy waves.

Lan Caihe shouted from the distance, “My flowers provide fragrance, not fish food!”

Han Xiangzi changed to a quick, playful tune. Hearing the melody on both sides, the fish split into two groups and followed it away instead of blocking his path. He advanced through the opening, turning the jade flute until the wind followed the music and lifted his robes. The fish did not carry him; they simply made every change beneath the surface easy to see.

One fish still dreamed of petals and kept circling him. Han Xiangzi flicked the final note upward, and it finally returned to the school. Far ahead, Lan Caihe started to wave. He Xiangu pushed the hand down. “Call them again and they'll surround the basket—with you still in it.”

Cao Guojiu waited for every fish to pass before setting a jade tablet on the water. Wherever it rested, the nearby waves sank into a firm place to stand. He yielded to one fish after another and never quite took his turn. Before long, he was still on the shore.

Han Xiangzi broke off halfway through a phrase and looked back. “Are you crossing the sea or greeting arrivals at the ferry?”

“There are many fish. Even fish deserve their proper order.”

“You let the fish go first, and the fish let the petals go first. At this rate, the flowers will be the only ones across by nightfall.”

Cao Guojiu considered this and saw the sense in it. He stopped waiting for the entire sea to clear. Instead, he cast a tablet onto the wave directly ahead. When the water lowered, he stepped onto it, moved another tablet forward, and calmed the next patch. If fish crossed his course, he neither chased them nor stood aside. He kept to his path, and they naturally swam around him.

At first he still cupped his hands and bowed to the sea before every step. Each time he did, Han Xiangzi deliberately stopped playing. Cao Guojiu asked why. “I'm waiting for you to finish exchanging courtesies with the East Sea.” Cao Guojiu finally lowered his hands. “Very well. I'll thank it when we reach the other side.”

The flute began again.

“Now *that* looks like crossing the sea,” said Han Xiangzi before returning the flute to his lips.

“It looked like crossing before.”

“Before, it looked like you were seeing off a guest.”

Flute and tablets carried them gradually away from shore. Han Xiangzi's music drew wind and fish and exposed the openings in rough water. Cao Guojiu's jade tablets calmed one patch after another, allowing him to catch up at a measured pace.

The six immortals already at sea heard the flute and looked back. Seeing the last two underway, He Xiangu said, “All eight of us are finally on the water.”

Still facing the shore, Zhang Guolao corrected her. “I've been watching behind us the whole time. I knew that already.”

“And can you see what's ahead?” Lü Dongbin asked.

“No.”

“Then perhaps say less about what you can see.”

By the time Han Xiangzi and Cao Guojiu joined the others, all eight treasures and all eight arts had appeared over the East Sea: gourd, palm-leaf fan, paper donkey, sword, lotus, flower basket, jade flute, and jade tablets. Each had made a separate path. Not one immortal had been dismissed with a lazy “and the rest followed.”

The far shore was still distant, though the beach behind them had dwindled away. Then a muffled boom rose from the depths. Waves that had been traveling in different directions turned toward them all at once.

At first, the creatures of the East Sea had taken them for a handful of travelers. Then a sword flashed, a fan raised the wind, and flowers and flute music came spilling over the water. The sea patrol watched with growing suspicion. If these people were merely passing through, they had chosen a remarkably effective way to keep the Dragon Palace from getting any sleep.

A squad of shrimp soldiers surfaced at a distance and shouted for them to state their business. Unfortunately, Han Xiangzi was still playing, while Lan Caihe and Cao Guojiu were arguing over whether flower petals counted as luggage. No one heard the question properly.

The shrimp soldiers caught only “Never mind,” “Keep going,” and “Bring up another tablet.” They rushed off to report: “The intruders refuse to answer—and they say they're bringing another weapon!”

Cao Guojiu held up a jade tablet, deeply aggrieved. “I meant *this* tablet.”

Then the patrol saw Lü Dongbin's sword flash across the surface and became even more certain that the visitors meant trouble. Lü Dongbin was about to explain that he was only parting the waves when Zhang Guolao struck his fish drum. At the sound, the shrimp soldiers abandoned their last remaining doubt. “Swords and war drums—and you claim you haven't come to challenge us?”

Zhang Guolao lowered the drumstick. “This drum does not usually take part in battles.”

“Today it sounds extremely involved.”

The explanation came too late. Crosscurrents rose around the Eight Immortals and closed in from every side. Han Zhongli threw his fan against the wave in front, but the gust drove Lan Caihe's basket toward Lü Dongbin. Lü Dongbin dodged the basket, and the channel cut by his sword opened directly before the paper donkey. Seeing a sudden gap where the water ought to be, the donkey stopped dead. It refused to believe that *that* could possibly count as a road.

Zhang Guolao beat the fish drum to urge it on. “There's no water ahead. Perfect for walking.”

The donkey backed away.

“It finds the lack of water even more suspicious,” He Xiangu observed.

Tieguai Li rode over on his gourd to help, but Han Zhongli's wind pushed him too far. He reached out with the iron crutch to hook the flower basket. Lan Caihe immediately clutched its handle. “This is a magical treasure, not a dock.”

“If hooking it straightens you out, it's a dock.”

Han Xiangzi tried to steady the waves with his flute, but the sudden current scattered the fish and the sea drowned his melody. Cao Guojiu calmed one patch with a jade tablet, only for another wave to rise beside it. He pressed down the left and the right broke loose; he turned right and a wave shoved the lotus away behind him.

All eight powers remained, but each pulled in a different direction. No one was driven back to shore, yet no one could move forward. Forced inward from their separate routes, they nearly crowded their treasures into a single tangled heap.

Shielding her lotus, He Xiangu told everyone to stop trying to prove they were not at fault. Han Zhongli lowered his fan. Tieguai Li stopped reaching for other people's treasures with his crutch. Little by little, the uproar faded until the sea patrol could finally hear a complete sentence.

Lü Dongbin sheathed his sword first. “Stop fighting for a path. The more we flail about, the more we look like an invading army.”

Han Xiangzi let the music fall silent as well. With the noise reduced, Lü Dongbin called across to the sea folk. They were only passing through, he explained. They had no intention of entering the Dragon Palace or harming anyone.

The patrol did not believe him at once. A shrimp soldier asked, “If you're merely crossing the sea, why is every one of you carrying a weapon?”

“Because we didn't bring a boat,” Lan Caihe replied.

“No boat, so you brought a sword?”

Lü Dongbin turned the blade toward the water. “I admit it sounds unhelpful when you put it that way.”

He Xiangu told everyone to steady their own treasures and stop racing. Tieguai Li held the gourd firm, Han Zhongli reduced the wind from his fan, Zhang Guolao halted the paper donkey, and Lan Caihe tucked every branch back inside the basket. The rough water still barred their way, and the Dragon Palace had not entirely abandoned its suspicion.

For the first time, the Eight Immortals saw the matter clearly. Their separate powers had been enough to carry them away from shore. But if all eight thought only of themselves in the same wild sea, none of them would get very far.

The waves gave them no leisure for a long discussion. He Xiangu had barely steadied her lotus when a cross-swell struck from the side. Cao Guojiu pressed a jade tablet against the nearby water and lowered it just in time to save her footing.

She nodded to him without ceremony. At present, an extra bow would not persuade the sea to send one less wave.

Han Xiangzi raised his flute again. This time he did not summon fish or try to master the waves alone. He played a short, unmistakable phrase: move when it rose, stop when it fell. All eight could hear it.

Han Zhongli rocked his palm-leaf fan in time with the tune, sending a gentle wind behind the group. Tieguai Li guarded the slowest side, touching his crutch to the water so the gourd would not spin again. Because Zhang Guolao rode backward, he had the best view of the waves chasing them. One strike of his fish drum warned everyone whenever another was coming.

Lü Dongbin no longer tried to carve out a long road with one stroke. He cut through only the fiercest wave immediately ahead. The instant a channel appeared, Cao Guojiu steadied its entrance with a jade tablet. He Xiangu's lotus passed through first, followed by Lan Caihe's basket. If a side wave pushed someone off course, lotus leaves supported one flank and the basket guarded the other, keeping anyone from drifting away alone.

A returning wave suddenly curled in from behind, straight toward Tieguai Li's gourd at the rear. Facing backward, Zhang Guolao saw it first and hammered an urgent warning on the drum. Han Zhongli turned his fan and sent a crosswind; Tieguai Li struck the water with his crutch at the same moment. The gourd skimmed the edge of the wave and returned to the group. Had they still been traveling separately, it would have swept him far away on his own.

“Earlier you told my basket not to hook the lotus,” Lan Caihe said. “Now the lotus has come to catch me.”

“It may catch you,” He Xiangu replied. “You still have to control your flowers.”

“They've improved enormously today. Now they hook only people we know.”

“That is not as reassuring as you think.”

Behind the waves, the sea patrol watched for a long while. If the Eight Immortals had truly meant to invade the Dragon Palace, they could have followed Lü Dongbin's open channel down into the depths. Instead, every move carried them toward the opposite shore, and they took care not to injure the creatures in the water. At last the shrimp soldiers understood. They waved their flags, and the sea folk withdrew the waves they had raised against the travelers.

Once those waves vanished, the natural wind pressed in from ahead. The Eight Immortals did not scatter and race. They kept moving to the flute's rhythm until every treasure had cleared the last violent stretch. Only then did Han Xiangzi sound one long note, telling them they could make for shore in their own way.

The water opened before them. Han Xiangzi kept playing, and all eight followed the same rhythm through the final distance. Tieguai Li's gourd reached the shallows first, but he did not hurry onto dry land. He braced one side with his crutch and waited for the paper donkey, which the wind had slowed.

“Why are you waiting for me?” Zhang Guolao asked. “I can see the way we came perfectly well.”

“Exactly,” said Tieguai Li. “Since that's all you can see, someone has to tell you when we've arrived.”

The paper donkey stepped onto wet sand, and Zhang Guolao finally turned around. The others came ashore one after another. Han Zhongli closed his fan; Lü Dongbin sheathed his sword; He Xiangu lifted her lotus; Lan Caihe picked up the flower basket; Han Xiangzi lowered the jade flute; and Cao Guojiu put away his tablets.

Eight immortals. Not one missing.

They had crossed the sea, but none could claim to have managed the final stretch alone. Showing their individual powers had taken them into the East Sea. Watching over one another had brought them out together.

On the far shore, they first inspected and packed away their treasures. Tieguai Li checked whether the gourd had taken on water. Lan Caihe stuffed wandering branches back into the basket. Zhang Guolao patted the paper donkey's neck and praised it for knowing the right direction all along.

“If it knew the direction,” Lü Dongbin asked, “why did someone have to tell it we'd reached land?”

“The shore arrived very suddenly.”

He Xiangu pointed behind them. “The shore has been here the whole time.”

“Which proves how patiently it waited.”

Cao Guojiu began to thank everyone, but Han Zhongli stopped him. “Better not. If you start with Tieguai Li, it'll be dark by the time you thank yourself.”

“Why start with me?” Tieguai Li demanded.

“Your gourd made the most turns today. It paid attention to every direction.”

“That was your fan's doing.”

Han Zhongli immediately became interested in the horizon. “The sea wind was strong. We cannot blame everything on the fan.”

Han Xiangzi played a short, laughing note, giving the argument a conclusion in which nobody admitted anything. They chose no winner, nor did they pretend the final stretch had been eight separate journeys. The lotus had caught the drifting flower basket; jade tablets had steadied the waves ahead of the lotus; the sword had opened a road; the palm-leaf fan had supplied the wind; the fish drum had warned of waves behind; the gourd had guarded the slowest place; and the jade flute had joined every movement into one. Take away any of them, and eight immortals would not now be standing on the same shore.

After the sea calmed, the old fisherman brought Aman across in his boat, following the safer water near the coast. Aman stepped out clutching his bamboo slips and paid the fare properly. Lan Caihe stared at him. “We each displayed our magic. Why did *you* take a boat?”

“My job is to record magic, not pretend I possess any.”

The fisherman nodded. “This young fellow's special power was causing me no trouble all the way across.”

Aman wrote that down too, then decided it did not make him look especially grand. He drew a small circle around the line so he could later claim it was an ink blot.

He asked which immortal had helped the others most during the final stretch. All eight pointed around the circle, and not one finger pointed toward its owner. Aman counted twice, gave up on ranking them, and wrote the truth on his green slip: *The Eight Immortals crossed the sea, each displaying a power of their own. When the storm rose, each lent a different strength; together they succeeded, and no one was left behind.*

When he finished, he did not ask the Eight Immortals to line up and cross again, nor did he add himself as the ninth. As the bamboo slips closed, Tieguai Li had already slung the gourd over his shoulder, Han Zhongli was stirring the air with his fan, and Zhang Guolao sat backward on the paper donkey. Lü Dongbin, He Xiangu, Lan Caihe, Han Xiangzi, and Cao Guojiu had gathered their things as well.

Lan Caihe looked back at the fisherman. “Could your boat truly have held eight people?”

“Not anymore.”

“Why not?”

“After listening to all of you, even my boat feels its powers are inadequate.”

Everyone laughed. The Eight Immortals did not cross the sea again to prove anything. They simply continued together along the mountain road on the far shore. Behind them, the East Sea still rolled and broke, while the saying “The Eight Immortals cross the sea, each displaying their own powers” came to mean more than eight treasures making eight kinds of commotion. It remembered, too, that final stretch of the journey, when no one traveled alone.
