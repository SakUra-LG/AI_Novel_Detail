People by the East Sea eat by looking at the sky, and they also watch the sky to see when the meal is ready. When the weather’s fine, fish swim in the nets; when it’s bad, the nets just hang under the eaves, looking like a listless face.

The little girl loved running to the sea. She didn’t think the sea was scary, just that it had a bit of a temper: when it was happy, it would bring shells to shore; when it was grumpy, it’d haul the shells back with the sand, like a forgetful neighbor who can’t keep anything but is very possessive.

One day she heard that a new kind of fish had appeared near the shore, so she begged her father to let her go aboard. He was patching a net and didn’t look up: “Can you row?”

“I can.”

“Can you read the wind?”

“I can read the east wind and the west wind.”

Only then did he lift his eyes: “Today the wind is blowing from the north.”

The girl fell silent for a moment, then pointed outside the yard and said, “Then I already know one more kind.”

Her father almost laughed at her, but still refused. She went to a seasoned fisherman who’d been sailing for years and asked him to explain the rules of the sea. He didn’t try to scare her; he simply had her stand on a reef and watch the waves: close waves were breaking, far ones were darkening, and the seabirds returning to shore were lower than usual—none of these were good signs.

Listening carefully, the girl’s earlier dismissive “the sea is just a big body of water” attitude faded. She asked, “If we only go out near the shore and come back when the wind changes, is that okay?”

The old fisherman stroked his beard: “If the sea wants to negotiate with you, it won’t call itself the sea.”

“Then we won’t wait for its permission. We’ll head back ourselves.”

He looked at her father. The father finally relented, but set three rules: only use a familiar small boat, stay near the shore, and never try to outdo the sea. The girl agreed to each one and even took the initiative to re‑tighten the loose knots on the boat. She stopped bragging about everything she could do and instead asked about each thing she didn’t understand.

The old fisherman laid a paddle flat on the sand and told her how to pull it back when the boat’s side was turning. The girl tried to yank it with all her strength, carving a deep groove in the sand and nearly toppling over. The fisherman said, “The sea loves people who think they’re stronger than it. It’s big enough to lose a few battles.”

She got up, shook the sand off her clothes, and tried again, following his hand signals. She learned to let the current push her first, then pull the boat back from the other side. It wasn’t flashy, but it saved effort. Her father watched and handed her an old rope: “Remember, when you promise to turn back, turn back. Don’t wait until you’re hurt to say you just wanted to see one more.”

Before boarding, the father tied a strip of red rope to the bow so people on shore could recognize their boat; the old fisherman put a water pouch in a corner of the cabin. The girl first held the rope, shook it lightly as if doing a little exercise, and the red rope flashed fine light patterns in the morning glow. She lowered her head to check the water pouch, saw its mouth puffed slightly, as if it had just inhaled a breath of sea air, and pinched it gently with her fingertips to make sure it wasn’t leaking. Then she carried the pouch onto the wooden board in the cabin corner; her fingers brushed a protruding nail, making a “tada” sound. The old fisherman laughed, “Watch out—don’t treat the boat like a spring mattress; you’ll bounce off.” The girl laughed and pointed at the nail, “If the boat really bounced, I’d like to try a dance move first.” The father frowned a little but still handed her the old rope, which whispered softly against his fingers.

She checked each item, making sure nothing was missed, then sat down. First she tied one end of the rope to a wooden post on the side of the hull, then she threw the other end gently to the bow; the red rope drew a thin arc in the air, like a bird’s tail feather. Next she tightened the water pouch’s lid, turning the metal clasp three times with a crisp “clack.” The old fisherman nodded in approval: “That grip would be perfect for picking up a sea snail; you’d never fear its shell.” The girl teased her father, “It’s just a short trip near the shore, not a long voyage.” As she said it, a smile danced at the corner of her eyes, like waves on the sea, and she even tugged the freshly tied red rope lightly, as if teasing it.

The father replied, “Return safely, and you decide how far.” After saying that, he flicked the old rope toward the girl; it spun in the air like a mischievous fish. When she caught it, her fingers accidentally brushed the knot at the end, loosening a small section and revealing a thin strand. She looked up at her father in surprise, and he laughed, shaking his head: “If that strand runs away, the boat turns into a ‘driftwood.’ Don’t let the seagulls snatch it.” The girl giggled and pointed at the strand, “Then I’ll let it be the ‘seagull’s tail’ and let it fly back on its own.”

She tied the strand back into a knot, strong as a sea snail’s spiral, then hung the rope securely at the bow. All checks finished, the boat bobbed gently on the rippling sea, the red rope trembling in the wind like a rosy halo around the departing vessel. Aman sat on the shore clutching a green bamboo slip; hearing this, she whispered, “Do we have to remember all three things?” She looked up at the distant sea, her eyes shining with curiosity and a hint of unease, yet holding firmly to her father’s lessons.

Aman sat on the shore clutching a green bamboo slip; hearing this, she whispered, “Do we have to remember all three things?”

The father said, “All of them.”

Aman looked at the sea, then at the narrow bamboo slip in her hand, sighed, “The sea hasn’t spoken yet, but the rules have already taken up all the space.”

The girl laughed once, corrected a crooked character he’d written, and then boarded the small boat with the old fisherman. When the boat left the shore, the village’s cooking smoke was still rising straight up; after the boat passed the reef, the smoke suddenly fell to one side. The old fisherman immediately turned his gaze to the sky, and the girl followed his look.

The clouds to the east were closing, like someone slowly shutting a heavy door. The girl didn’t panic; she gripped the paddle and reminded the old fisherman, “We promised that when the wind changes, we’ll turn back.”

The old fisherman nodded, and the small boat immediately turned its bow. But the familiar shoreline had already faded behind the rapidly rising fog.

When the wind caught the boat, the houses on shore were still visible, but the cooking smoke had already dispersed. The old fisherman stowed the sail and relied only on the paddle to push the boat back to shore. The girl gathered the loose items inside as he had said, then handed the rope to him without asking another question.

The first wave slammed against the side of the boat, jolting it off course. The old fisherman braced the stern and shouted, “Don’t stand up, girl.” The girl crouched behind the rail, saw the reef that had been on the left front now shift to the right, and realized the sea had pushed them off the return path.

The fog thickened. Someone on shore lit a fire pit and struck a copper bowl to guide them. The sound drifted through the wind and waves, intermittent. The girl listened for a moment, then pointed to a faint reddish glow in the fog: “That’s the village entrance.”

The old fisherman didn’t turn right away. He first looked at the water, then at the waves, then shook his head: “There’s a sudden tide in the middle; if we push through, we’ll capsize.”

They followed the wave’s flow, trying to drift toward the gentler side. Halfway through, a higher wave slammed into them, plunging the bow deep. The girl threw an empty water jar overboard and pushed aside the soaked old mat to lighten the boat. The boat steadied for a moment but still drifted outward.

The old fisherman told her to hold on alone while he untangled the rope caught in the wind. She didn’t follow. Instead, she lowered herself to the stern and, using the technique she’d learned on the sand, pulled the paddle in line with the wave’s direction. The boat didn’t immediately return to shore, but it stopped being tossed sideways by the waves. After he untied the rope, he glanced at her and said, “You did it right.” Those three words fell to the wind and dispersed.

The shore fire flickered. The girl remembered the old fisherman’s lesson that in fog you can’t just chase one light; she turned back to look for familiar mountain silhouettes. But the sea and sky had merged, and the tall mountain behind the village was gone.

People on shore tied a long rope to the reef, and several fishermen took turns moving forward. Every time a wave came, they lowered themselves; when the wave receded, they moved forward again. The rope finally was thrown out, but it landed behind the boat. The girl tried to reach it with her paddle, but as she almost touched it, the water pushed the boat farther away. She didn’t cry; she simply returned the paddle to the old fisherman, ready for the next chance.

The shore crew immediately hauled the long rope back in. The wet rope had become twice as heavy as when they’d set out, and a handful of people stood shoulder‑to‑shoulder as they backed away, their heels digging deep troughs into the sand. Once the rope’s end was neatly coiled, they tied a thin wooden sliver to its tip so it could float on the waves; they also added a short section of rope behind it, hoping to push the distance farther. The father stood at the front, repeatedly checking the gaps where the waves would recede, his arm already scarred from the rough rope.

On the second throw, the rope landed on the side of the boat. The little girl bent low, slipped her oar into the loop, and was about to pull it back when a sudden side‑wave slammed in. The oar shaft bent dangerously against the gunwale, and the old fisherman shouted, “Let go!” She didn’t try to hold on any longer and released it at once. The oar was swept away by the wave, and the loop slid off. Those on the shore watched the rope’s end being pushed back again, but no one blamed her; if she’d held on too hard, the boat would have tipped even faster.

For the third attempt, the shore crew threw from the windward side. By then the little boat had already been shoved past the reef’s corner, and fog had split the two sides so that only shouting could be heard. Aman, clutching the green bamboo slip, stood on a high stone and helped pass along words that were hard to hear. He didn’t direct the boat’s course; he simply relayed the girl’s shout of “turn left again” exactly as she said it. The sea breeze blew his first word away, so he and the others shouted together, and dozens of voices barely made it over the crest of the wave.

The rope’s end finally landed within the little girl’s reach. She first grabbed the floating wood, then wound the rope around the boat’s deck, while the old fisherman used his body to steady the stern. The shore crew began to haul in slowly; the bow of the boat turned toward the village entrance, close enough to see the silhouettes of the shore‑keepers. A child, excited, ran forward, only to be pulled back by his mother’s grip on his collar, his feet still kicking in place. No one laughed at him; everyone stared at the taut long rope.

At that moment the knot hit a submerged reef edge. The next wave lifted the little boat high, and the long rope snapped straight, emitting a dull crack. The shore crew stopped pulling immediately, but the rope that had been worn thin by the reef had already broken. The father dove into waist‑deep water to grab it, his fingertips barely brushing the frayed strands. The remaining rope slipped from the boat’s side, and the distance that had been painstakingly shortened was pulled back out by the sea.

The little girl didn’t dare re‑tie the broken rope to herself. She handed the usable portion to the old fisherman to secure to the deck, and she held the last oar, continuing to steer with the waves. The shore crew didn’t stop either; they began to connect a spare short rope. Everyone was doing what they could, but no one could guarantee that the next attempt would succeed. The distant sea surface rose slowly between each try, finally forming the dark silhouette that blocked the daylight.

The old fisherman said, “Hold on tight. When the next wave comes, I’ll steer you toward the shore.”

The little girl, however, wrapped the rope around the deck and handed it to him first, saying, “Both of us should go back.”

As soon as she finished speaking, the wind’s howl fell silent for a moment. A dark shape rose on the distant sea, as if the whole eastern sea had stood up. The old fisherman fought to turn the boat, and the shore crew dragged the long rope into the shallows, yet they were still too far apart.

A giant wave crashed down, and the little boat was swallowed by a white, misty spray. When the shore crew could see the sea again, they saw a broken oar bobbing up, only to be taken away by the next wave. Neither the little girl nor the old fisherman surfaced again.

People stayed until the wind died. The father searched along the shore, the mother lit the lamp at the door again and again, and no one dared say, “We’re not getting back.” The next day the sea pushed a piece of the boat back onto the beach; another day later, an empty water jug was stuck in a reef crevice. Apart from that, the eastern sea returned nothing.

The old fisherman’s family was also waiting. His usual low stool still sat at the door, and the half‑finished net was left uncollected. The villagers put that net together with the boat board the little girl’s father had found, but no one could claim a lucky return. The storm had taken more than a legend; it had taken the empty space that two families had been looking for every day.

The tide rose and fell as usual, as if the little boat had never appeared on the water. The village no longer heard the little girl asking which way the wind came from, and no one ran past the courtyard door laughing that they now knew another kind of wind. At nightfall the father still kept the lamp lit in front of the door, its glow shining toward the sea all night.

When dawn came, the eastern sea still rose as before. The waves pushed broken wood to the shore, then retreated slowly, as if the night had never swallowed a boat. Those guarding the shore found a broken oar, an empty water jug, and a piece of red rope tied to the boat’s bow, but they never saw the girl return. The mother clutched the red rope in her palm, the father stood staring at the sea for a long time. No one in the village lit a fire for breakfast; only the sound of the tide filled the silent spaces.

At that moment a clear bird call came from behind the reef. A bird the villagers had never seen rose from the spray. Its body resembled a blackbird, with fine patterns on its head, a white beak, red feet, and feathers that never flickered between white and green. It was a fledgling; a gust of sea wind pushed it off course; it landed on a slick stone, steadied itself, and flapped its wings again. It was not a bird that had wandered from the forest. When it saw the red rope, it stopped abruptly, recognizing every face waiting for the girl on the shore.

The little girl’s life had been left in the deep water of last night, but her memory had not faded. She remembered the great wave that hit when the boat tilted, the last fire she saw on the shore, and how the eastern sea calmed after dawn. She wanted to shout to her parents, but only two short cries came from her white beak: “Jingwei, Jingwei.”

The mother looked up toward the sound. The bird flew closer, then paused mid‑air. A few steps separated the people and the bird; no one could retrace last night. Seeing the bird’s gaze fixed on the red rope, the mother’s fingers trembled, but she did not reach out rashly. Jingwei circled the parents once, memorizing their shapes, then turned toward the eastern sea. In that moment it was no longer just searching for a way home; it was the first time it truly saw how vast the sea that had taken the girl’s life really was.

The eastern sea did not reply. Small waves over the reef carried a piece of boat board that had just been picked up back into the water. Jingwei chased after it, flew over the crest, then the sea wind pushed it back. It landed high, gasped, waited for the wind to ease, and tried again. This time it flew at an angle with the wind, crossed the water where the broken boat had sunk, and safely returned to the reef. It learned that, though its body was small, it could make many trips; the sea, however vast, could not decide where it would fly next.

Jingwei looked toward the forest behind the shore. There were fallen branches, stones washed out of the soil by rain, and things it could now carry. A thought grew clearer with each trial flight: the eastern sea could take the girl, but it could not erase the event like a wave’s mark. It would bring wood and stone from the mountains into the sea; if one attempt failed, it would try again, and if today was not enough, it would wait until tomorrow.

After deciding to act, Jingwei did not immediately pretend it had already moved the coast. It first flew a loop at the foot of the mountain, spotting where it could land and where the wind was gentler, then turned back to confirm the sea’s direction. The parents still stood on the shore, watching the white‑beaked, red‑footed bird rise slowly from low ground. It gave one last cry of its new name, then flew over the village toward the deep forest. When it returned, its mouth would no longer only sing; it would bring the first thing that could be thrown into the eastern sea.

The next morning, Jingwei flew back from the forest to the eastern sea, clutching a small stone in its beak. The stone’s weight kept it from flying high; a gust of wind pushed it sideways. Jingwei landed on a reef to rest, steadied the stone, and after a breeze passed, flew to the near shore. It let go of the stone, which fell straight into the water, creating a tiny whirl in the waves before disappearing.

Jingwei circled the spot where the stone landed. The sea looked exactly as before, as if the stone had never arrived. It did not return to the shore to wait for results; instead it turned and flew back into the forest. On the second trip it carried a light branch. When the branch fell, it floated on the waves, swayed with the tide, then was pushed back onto the sand, lying beside Jingwei’s feet like a lost‑direction lazy man who had walked half a day and returned to the same spot.

Aman, holding the green bamboo slip, sat on a high rock, intending to count how many times Jingwei had flown back and forth. He wrote a stroke on the slip; wind blew a layer of fine sand, burying half of the stroke; he wrote another, and the splash of waves wet the corner of the slip. A nearby fisher boy asked, “Did you count?” Aman stared at the two marks—one dry, one wet—and answered earnestly, “Very clear. One trip back to the wind, one trip back to the sea. The one that comes back to me hasn’t returned yet.”

Jingwei heard the laughter and didn't stop. It looked down at the branch that had been pushed back, noticing that the two forked ends had split off, and when it fell into the crevice, it had briefly gotten stuck. It picked up the same branch again, not flying out to the distant sea, but instead heading towards two nearby rocks that were close to the shore. When the tide went out, it placed the forked ends into the crevice; the next big wave came, and the branch swayed but didn't immediately return to the shore.

This wasn't exactly filling in the East Sea. The forked branch was still floating in the water, and the small stones had already sunk out of sight. But Jingwei had seen with its own eyes for the first time that the things it brought hadn't been swept back to the surface right away. It stood on the rocks, observing for a while, and then flew back to the mountain forest. From then on, it no longer just picked the nearest wood and stones: the light branches would be blown away by the waves, so it chose slightly thicker ones; the stones sank beneath the surface, but it still brought them, because sinking beneath the surface didn't mean they never existed.

Over the next few days, Jingwei didn't randomly scatter wood and stones across the entire sea. It first chose three shallow bays: one that was sheltered from the wind, one that was sandwiched between the rocks, and one that was right in the path of the incoming tide. Every day when the tide went out, it would drop a short branch of similar shape into each of the three places, and then stop to observe from a high vantage point. The bay sheltered from the wind kept the branch the longest, the bay in the path of the tide would have the branch swept back to the shore after just one wave, and the branches in the rock crevices would easily get stuck, but could also be dislodged by the next big stone.

Aman wanted to mark the three places, so he drew circles, crosses, and a bird that looked like a bent cucumber on the rocks. A young fisherman pointed at the last one and asked, "What's that?" He replied, "That's the bay in the path of the tide, the wind is strong, and the mark got blown askew." The young fisherman picked up a stone to help him draw it straight, but just then a wave came crashing in, and both of their pant legs got wet. Aman immediately added, "Now the mark is even more accurate, this place is indeed in the path of the tide."

Jingwei didn't pay attention to whether they drew it like it or not. It first dropped two smaller stones into the rock crevices on either side, and then placed the forked branch across the middle. The first time, the stones were too far apart, and the branch fell into the water; the second time, one of the stones landed at an angle, and the wave swept it away. It picked up the same branch again and flew around the rocks once before releasing it, waiting for the tide to recede and expose a relatively flat spot before letting go.

The branch finally stayed in place. The next time it went back, it didn't immediately add more branches, but instead brought smaller fragments of stone to fill in the gaps beneath the branch. Some of the stones immediately sank beneath the surface, while others were swept away by the water; only a few remained. But when the next big wave came, the water spray was divided into two streams, and for a brief moment, a low trough appeared in the middle.

The people on the shore saw the change and some of them exclaimed, "The sea is indeed being filled in!" But an old man shook his head and said, "It's just that the wave has changed its way of moving, don't attribute it to something that will take ten years to happen in just one day." Aman erased the word "retreat" he had written on his bamboo slip and changed it to "water divides into two streams." He erased it too hard, leaving a small indentation on the slip, and then comforted himself, "Good, the slip has also lowered a bit."

Jingwei didn't consider its success in dividing the water spray as a victory. It waited for the tide to rise and saw that the forked branch would still sway, knowing that the structure wasn't yet stable; it waited for the next low tide and then brought more stones to fill in the gaps. This process gave each trip a specific goal: it wasn't a random scattering of wood and stones across the entire sea, but rather a careful attempt to find out how wood and stone could be used without being swept away immediately.

Aman soon realized that simply counting wouldn't keep up with Jingwei's pace. The sea wind made the small stones he was holding clatter, and a few of them had already been swept halfway into the sand by the tide, so he could only arrange the remaining stones in a row in front of him, like a line of children waiting to be called. Every time Jingwei flew overhead, carrying a dry branch or a stone in its beak, and then dived down to land on the rocks, Aman would hurry over to move the stone that had just been dropped, as if to mark it as the "nth" time.

Just as he was carefully arranging the stones, a young fisherman snuck up behind him, laughing and quietly pushed the stone at the end of the row back into the pile, like playing a game of "stealing a chair." Aman looked up, his eyes still lingering on the counting he had been doing, but was suddenly confused by the young fisherman's mischievous behavior - he had no idea how many times he had counted, and could only awkwardly scratch his head and mutter, "Uh, where should I put this stone?" So he had to ask Jingwei for help, calling out loudly, "Jingwei, can you please fly back and bring back that stone you dropped earlier?" Jingwei flew overhead, its wings creating a swift arc, but ignored Aman's call and continued flying off into the distance, as if it couldn't hear the commotion on land.

The young fisherman saw this and raised an eyebrow, smiling and said, "The historian is in charge of the world's affairs, who gets to keep track of the historian's memory?" His words rolled out like the waves, with a hint of teasing. Aman rearranged the stones that had been scattered, and with a serious expression, said, "For now, it seems it's up to you to cause trouble." His words were firm, but his eyes involuntarily glanced over at Jingwei, which was still flying off into the distance, its silhouette bobbing up and down on the waves like an unextinguished flame.

The people on the shore gradually understood what Jingwei was doing. Some children picked up a branch and asked if they could help it drop it into the sea. An old man stopped them, saying this wasn't a game for people on the shore to play. Jingwei was trying to bring back something every time it flew back, and people could see it, but couldn't take away its choice. The children put the branch back and just waited for Jingwei to appear again.

As the sun began to set, the branch that had been left in the crevice was finally swept away by the rising tide and slowly drifted away from the rocks. Jingwei saw this. It didn't consider the brief time it had stayed as a success, nor did it consider everything a waste just because the branch had been dislodged. It flew back to the trees on the shore, resting, its beak pointing towards the mountain forest, its tail feathers still facing the East Sea. The next day, as soon as the sun rose, it picked up a new branch and stone. From that day on, Jingwei's attempts to fill in the sea were no longer just a single, angry toss, but rather a daily back-and-forth.

The next morning, the branch that had been left in the shallow water was found lying on the shore again. It was covered in sand, and a strand of seaweed was still hanging from its tip, like the East Sea had tied a mocking tuft of seaweed to it.

Jingwei landed next to the branch, not immediately flying back to the mountain forest. The tide came in and out, pushing the branch closer and then farther away. The waves crashed against the rocks, making a deafening sound. To the people on the shore, it was just the sound of the waves, but to Jingwei, it was the East Sea asking, "Was yesterday's effort worth it? Can you see the result today?"

Jingwei picked up the branch and flew back to the place where it had dropped it the day before, placing it back down. The waves quickly chased after it, and the branch was swept back towards the shore.

The East Sea asked again, "You have how many days, I will have how many waves. You are how small, I will be how big. Will you come back again?"

Jingwei stood on the rocks, its feathers blown back by the wind. It didn't argue with the sea. The sea was so vast, if it could be filled in just by talking, the people who loved to chat on the shore would have already accomplished great things.

It picked up the branch again, this time not flying towards the most turbulent part of the waves, but rather searching for a shallow bay that was hidden behind the rocks. The branch fell into the water, but it didn't immediately get swept away. Jingwei watched for a while, then flew back to the mountain forest.

This wasn't the first time it had changed locations. The previous place looked calm, but when the tide came in, it swept the branch away from the side; another place had a rock crevice that was too narrow, and the branch was swept out before it even had a chance to settle. Jingwei repeatedly brought back the same branch, not giving its failures a more pleasant name. It wasn't until it found the current shallow bay that it was able to achieve a different result from the previous day.

Some people who had come to collect shells were watching from a distance. The day before, they had said that this little bird was probably just angry and would forget about it after a few trips. Today, someone was about to speak up, but an old man stopped them with a gesture, "Don't laugh. You and I are standing here talking, it's flying back and forth, and that's the truth."

When Jingwei returned, it was carrying a small stone in its beak. The East Sea sent a wave crashing against the shallow bay on the outside. The water spray flowed over the rocks, and the branch swayed again. Jingwei waited for the wave to recede before flying over to release the stone. The stone fell next to the branch and quickly sank beneath the surface.

The sea surface showed no signs of change. The East Sea's voice was even louder, "The things you've thrown in, you can't even find them yourself."

Jingwei lowered its head to stare at the water’s surface. It could not see the tiny stone, nor could it know how long the bent branch would stay. All it could tell was that the last trip had finished, that there were still trees and stones in the mountains, and that another trip lay ahead.

The East Sea, seeing it remain silent, deliberately pushed a puff of foam toward the reef: “Look, everything I bring ashore is more plentiful than what you leave behind.”

Jingwei let the foam drift behind it. If the sea insisted on counting every tide as a triumph, it would be up to it to do so. Jingwei had no green bamboo slips, nor did it argue over bragging rights; it only knew the back‑and‑forth path between the forest and the sea.

It turned again. Wind chased it from the sea, pushing it off course. Jingwei slipped around the reef’s corner, still heading for the forest.

People on the shore looked up at the bird’s shadow shrinking. No one calculated when it would win, nor urged it to surrender. The jokesters who had bent over to pick up a dry twig by their feet, intending to toss it into the sea, paused and set it down again—this was neither their spectacle nor a throw that could earn a claim.

Soon Jingwei returned from the mountains. The East Sea still rolled its waves. It still carried a wooden stone in its beak, flying toward that water with no hope of victory.

In autumn, the East Sea blew another storm. Before night fell, clouds pressed against the mountain tops, and seabirds flew inland. Jingwei was coming back from the mountains, its white bill clutching a small stone.

The bent branch in the shallow bay fell with the waves. The few wooden stones left from days ago were already scarce, now they looked like a handful of grass tossed into rushing water. The first big wave swept past the reef, and the bent branch vanished; the second wave followed, burying even the shallow shoal in murky water.

Jingwei faced the wind and flew forward, only to be pushed back at the reef’s corner. It landed behind a windward stone and still wouldn’t let go of the small stone. Wind slipped through the stone’s cracks, pushing it outward. It tried again, but still could not break through the chaotic surf.

Before the third takeoff, it stared for a long time. Grass near the ground lay flat toward the mainland; clouds higher up moved faster, indicating that wind beyond the reef would be even stronger. If Jingwei flew its usual straight line, it would be pushed out to the open sea before reaching the shallow bay. Instead it flew low along the rock wall, hoping the stone wall would shield it, but the waves slammed against the rock, splashing water that drenched its wing tips.

It barely returned to the windward side, shaking its feathers. The stone in its white bill never slipped, but its weight slowed each launch. People on the shore saw it and worried it might be swallowed by wind and waves like the girl’s night. Some opened their arms to push it back into the forest. Jingwei leapt back a step, neither approaching nor taking off again.

Aman tucked a green bamboo slip into his coat to keep dry, lying behind a stone with only half his face exposed. He didn’t shout “Persistence is a hard push,” nor did he decide to give up for Jingwei. He simply told those nearby, “Clear the path first. It needs a break; at least don’t let us crowd it so it can’t rest.” The crowd moved to a higher dirt slope, leaving the windward stone intact for Jingwei.

In the wind, broken grass and fine branches rolled by. Jingwei watched where they went: light grass flew past the stone’s corner to the distance, heavier damp branches rolled along the ground, sometimes stopping behind a second reef. It tried to fly with an empty beak to the second spot, then turn back, still blown off course, but it could use the two rocks to break the wind into segments. After returning to the starting point, it re‑clutched the stone, flew only the first segment, landed steady, and waited.

That tactic brought it close to the shallow bay, but the sea was still unsuitable for throwing stones. The waves ahead covered the reef’s gaps entirely; the previous branch‑stone positions were unrecognizable. Jingwei waited a long time behind the second rock until a larger wave pushed sea water to its feet. It finally let the stone go, folded its wings, lowered its body, and didn’t treat “I’ve come this far” as a reason to keep going.

Waiting was not easy. Every time the wind eased, it seemed to tempt Jingwei to launch immediately; the next wave always came faster than expected. Jingwei watched the little trees by the shore bowing in the wind and saw the water line rise inch by inch, finally confirming that there was no safe moment to drop a stone today. It clutched the stone and returned along the two sheltered points, rather than casually tossing the stone into the wind to prove it had been there.

The East Sea mocked its retreat, but people on the shore saw something else: Jingwei had learned to postpone an impossible flight rather than surrender it to the sea. It left the stone behind the windward stone, noted its spot, and waited for the storm to truly pass before trying again. The stone that wasn’t thrown became evidence that the next departure still existed.

The East Sea laughed in the wind: “You’ve kept these days, and I just take it in a moment. What are you still carrying that one?”

Jingwei didn’t answer. It watched a little tree by the shore bend under the wind, its branches almost touching the ground, yet never breaking. When the wind eased a bit, the tree lifted a little; the next gust made it bow again.

Jingwei finally set the stone beside itself. It wasn’t discarding it, nor surrendering. It folded its wings and quietly hid behind the stone. The waves crashed ashore again and again, erasing the marks left before. If it had pushed through now, it would only have let the East Sea haul away another bird.

A broken twig was pushed by the wind in front of it, then rolled away. Jingwei didn’t chase. It had always thought that if it just flew a bit faster, it could snatch something back from the sea; this storm forced it to admit that continuing didn’t mean always rushing forward. Sometimes you must guard the next departure before turning persistence into a fleeting temper.

The storm raged on for a long time. Jingwei didn’t calculate when the wind would stop; it only watched the little tree stop bowing so low. Later, the waves still roared, but the tree’s tips could sway gently in the wind.

It walked to the spot where it had left the stone. The stone was still there. Jingwei used its white bill to pick it up and tried to fly out from behind the stone. The wind still faced it, but it no longer pushed it back immediately.

This time it didn’t go farther into the sea; it flew just above the nearest shallow water. As the waves receded, a corner of reef emerged from the murk. Jingwei flew over, released its beak. The stone fell softly, and in a moment it was gone.

It returned to the shore, waiting for another gust. The previous trip had shown the path was still open, but that didn’t mean every trip should rush into danger. Jingwei looked at the sea and at the slowly quieting tree tops in the mountains. When it could hold a branch steady, it would take off again.

The East Sea said, “Everything you left before is gone.”

Jingwei turned and flew back to the shore. That trip could not recover the wooden stone swept away by the storm, nor could it make the sea retreat. It simply turned “everything is gone” into “there’s another one.”

After the wind, the forest was littered with broken branches. Jingwei landed in a clearing, picked up the lightest piece. It didn’t try to carry more while the wind eased, nor did it turn back to contest the sea. Night fell; it placed the dry twig in a windward spot and waited until it could fly steadily before setting off again.

The East Sea still roared, but this time its sound didn’t feel like victory; it sounded more like a self‑boast ignored by everyone.

The storm passed, the weather grew colder. Jingwei still flew from the forest to the East Sea. When the cold wind hit, it rested on a sheltered tree; when it could fly again, it picked up the wooden stone it had left behind. The sea didn’t quiet because of winter, and Jingwei didn’t consider itself stopped just because it flew slower.

Spring arrived, and new shoots sprouted among the dead branches in the mountains. Jingwei didn’t break live branches; it only searched the ground for old wood blown down by the wind. When people saw it picking and dropping, they couldn’t help but say, “You’re filling the sea? Just take any branch you find.”

The old man replied, “They’re fighting the sea, but they didn’t say they’d have to cross the mountains.”

The man touched his nose and fell silent.

Later, he often chopped wood at the foot of the mountain. Once he saw Jingwei carrying an old branch and taking off, he casually cleared fallen leaves from the branch. Jingwei didn’t end up carrying more, and he didn’t go to the sea to claim he helped fill it. After returning to the village, someone asked him; he simply said, “I just made room; it was still it that flew.”

When the heat came, the sea shone blindingly bright. After each flight, Jingwei often stayed under the shade of a tree on the shore. Earlier, people thought it had given up when it rested, but after a long wait they always saw it take off again. Later, when it stopped again, everyone simply gave it a quiet space, no longer circling to ask, “How many did you drop today?”

Sometimes the tide brings branches back to shore. Jingwei, ever determined, picks up the old twig and carries it back again; other times, after a whole day of searching, it can’t find the thing it dropped earlier, and it doesn’t even hover on the water’s surface looking for it. Instead, it flies into the mountains, grabs a fresh one, and starts a new run. When it fails, it isn’t sent back to the beginning; it’s simply sent to the next departure.

There are also days of relentless rain, when the mountain roads are muddy and the sea is dim. Jingwei waits in the forest for the storm to pass, and people in the village start to think it’s finally grown tired. When the clouds clear, the bird’s silhouette appears as usual, and the guessers quickly lower their nets, as if they themselves had just given up.

A child has been coming to the sea since he could walk. At first he only pointed at the bird’s shadow and shouted, but later he learned to ask his grandfather, “When will it finish filling the sea?”

The grandfather said, “I don’t know.”

“Then why does it keep flying?”

The grandfather thought for a long time and said, “Maybe it knows that some things can’t be called unfinished just because we can’t predict when they’ll be done. It’s better not to start them at all.”

The child found that explanation too roundabout, so he squatted down and drew a bird. When the tide came in, it quickly erased the drawing. He moved higher, drew again. Watching him, the grandfather suddenly felt that his answer wasn’t entirely wrong.

When the autumn wind blew again, the stone path by the shore had fewer people mocking Jingwei. In the past, fishermen would look up before hauling in their nets and see that tiny black silhouette—its passing always carried a cool breeze, as if reminding them whether the tide that day was good for sailing. So the fishermen would gently shake their ropes, stand on the ship’s rail, squint into the distance, and mutter, “The wind’s fine today, and Jingwei’s busy too.” They no longer laughed at the bird’s wood‑stone, but treated it as a natural omen.

A wood‑collector met it on the mountain road and his steps slowed unconsciously. The dark‑feathered bird, clutching a scrap of wood, suddenly swooped down from the branch and plunged into a pile of stones freshly washed by the tide. The wood‑collector looked up, startled, then laughed, “Ah, this little guy knows how to pick stones better than I do!” After saying that, he gently set a dry twig on the ground, clearing a narrow path, and kept walking. Wildflowers beside the road swayed in the sea breeze, as if applauding this unplanned courtesy.

No one else carries wood‑stones for it, and no one claims its persistence as their own triumph. Each time, Jingwei must sprint across the crest of the tide, clutch the floating twig tightly, only to be swept up by a sudden wave, the wood carried far away by the sea. After the wave recedes, it shakes off salt‑slick feathers, stands up again, and the light in its eyes shines harder than the sea surface. On the shore, Aman carries a battered bamboo basket, holds a worn‑out pen, and stands on a reef to record every fall and rise. He sometimes writes the wrong characters, laughing at himself: “This time I wrote ‘Jing‑wei’ as ‘Jing‑wen,’ looks like even the pen doesn’t want to help.” But he never treats his pen as a savior; it’s just a witness.

People gradually learned to swallow a light “useless” when that tiny silhouette swept by. Once a child shouted loudly at the sea, “Jingwei, you’ll never fill the ocean!” The waves swallowed his voice into foam, echoing a bubbling laugh. Later, children stopped mocking loudly and instead gently tossed small stones into the water, as if sharing the obsession with Jingwei. Aman looked up to the horizon, saw Jingwei dive again, clutching a shard of stone, and the silhouette drew a thin black line in the sunset.

The East Sea remains vast, and even when the tide rises it can still cover everything Jingwei leaves behind. It hasn’t grown bigger than the sea, nor suddenly gained the power to summon wind and rain. It simply treats rest as part of the journey, and being washed away as the starting point for the next run. Each time the tide retreats, Jingwei breathes in the damp crevices, shakes off salt‑laden feathers, then flaps again, clutching a new twig, and flies deeper into the sea. Aman still writes quietly, sometimes looking up, a flash of respect in his eyes, yet still holding that childlike curiosity and defiance.

Another winter morning, frost clung to the shore. Jingwei swooped down from the tree, clutching the small stone chosen last night. A child wrapped in an old coat stood far away, looked up as it flew toward the sea, and didn’t ask what would happen.

The stone fell, the splash closed. Jingwei turned and flew into the forest. Seasons cycled behind it, the next run still ahead.

Many years later, the East Sea was still the East Sea. Standing on the shore, looking out where water met sky, no place bowed because of a little bird.

A new tide receded, the bent twig thrown into the shallow bay yesterday lay back on the sand. Jingwei flew from the mountains, dropped beside the twig. It looked at the sea, then lowered its head to pick up the original twig. The twig was wet with sand, heavier than yesterday, so it first flew to a reef to rest, then continued toward the shallows.

The East Sea sent a wave that pushed it off its original course. Jingwei didn’t force through; it circled the wave, flew to the other side of the reef‑shielded water. When its white beak relaxed, the bent twig fell, just landing between two old stones.

The wave came again quickly. The bent twig trembled, as if it could snap away at any moment. Jingwei waited on the shore for a while, not treating “temporary stay” as a victory, nor leaving only when it was washed away. The forest still had wood‑stones, and this run was already finished.

It turned and flew back into the mountains. Soon it returned with a small stone. The stone fell into the water with a faint sound, and the sea closed again. From afar nothing changed, only the bird’s silhouette returned from the sea once more.

A child at the shore asked Aman, “After all these years, how many times has it flown?”

Aman, holding a green bamboo slip, thought and said, “Enough to make me count in confusion, not enough to make the sea say a single lie.”

The child asked again, “What else do you remember?”

Seeing Jingwei fly from the forest, Aman wrote truthfully: today again Jingwei carried a wood‑stone into the sea, the tide receded, the shore was as before. He folded the green bamboo slip into the “Twenty Bamboo Slips of Mountains and Seas,” leaving wins and losses to time, and letting the child keep watching.

This time Jingwei carried a lighter piece of dry wood. Wind blew from the side; it angled a bit with the wind, waited for the gust to pass, then turned back to the sea. The bent twig still jammed between old stones, the new dry wood lay beside it, gently bobbing with the waves.

The East Sea said, “Look, I’m still here.”

Jingwei called twice, “Jingwei, Jingwei.”

It didn’t say the sea was gone, nor that it had won. It simply turned and flew into the forest, answering that boast with the next departure.

Evening tide rose, the morning’s bent twig finally broke free from the old stone, pushed toward the shore by water. The newly thrown dry wood vanished somewhere. People at the shore saw it but didn’t sigh for Jingwei. Everyone knew that the next dawn, that little bird would decide on its own whether to come again.

At night, the sea’s roar drowned the village. In the forest, Jingwei rested on a tree, a stone that could be carried tomorrow lay at its feet. It didn’t vow to guard the stone, nor pre‑announce forever. When dawn broke, it opened its eyes, picked up the stone, and flew out of the woods.

The East Sea still has no end. Jingwei crossed the mountain base, the village, and old reefs, still clutching a wood‑stone in its white beak. The story didn’t end when the sea was filled, nor when someone declared its effort futile; it continued with that eastward‑bound silhouette, starting a new run.
