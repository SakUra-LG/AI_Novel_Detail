Fan Xiliang pasted the last piece of window paper, stepped back to admire it for a while, and said that if the north wind still managed to sneak in, it could only mean the wind could read the characters “Fan House” and recognize his name.

Meng Jiangnü pressed down the lifted corner with her hand: “Don’t let the wind start its tricks yet. The paste isn’t dry, and this window paper is already trying to move out.”

The two had only been married a short time, and their home was cramped; the pantry held just enough grain to be carefully rationed. Yet each morning someone chopped firewood, each evening someone lit a lamp, and the once‑empty house gradually filled with the sounds of daily life. Fan Xiliang could repair farm tools and weave baskets, but he wasn’t good at mending clothes; when Meng Jiangnü lifted his old coat, she found layer upon layer of patches on the shoulders, thick enough to look like a secret little wall.

“It’s still wearable,” Fan Xiliang said.

“Which part is wearable?”

“Everything except the spots where the wind leaks through.”

Meng Jiangnü turned the coat inside out; the lining was already worn thin. Fan Xiliang had intended to slip it back unnoticed, but she caught his eye, so he had to explain that when he lived alone he’d add an extra bundle of firewood in winter, and a threadbare coat didn’t matter.

“Now we’re a couple, not just you bargaining with the north wind,” Meng Jiangnü said. “We must save firewood and keep warm.”

She pulled out the cotton and fabric she’d saved and measured his shoulders and back. Fan Xiliang stood so stiffly upright it looked like he was taking a big exam rather than getting measured. When she told him to relax, he inhaled sharply and puffed his shoulders out even wider.

“If you keep standing like that, once the winter coat is finished there’ll be room for another person inside.”

Fan Xiliang quickly exhaled, laughed, and replied, “That’s fine—when it gets cold you can snuggle in with me.”

Meng Jiangnü gave his hand a gentle tap with a ball of thread. After the laugh, she carefully noted his measurements, planning to finish a new coat before winter set in. Fan Xiliang steadied the wobbly wooden foot of his old loom with a scrap of cloth and built a tiny thread rack so the silk wouldn’t roll all over the house. The first time he dropped the ball of thread it rolled straight off the loom; he chased it under the table, scooping it up while muttering, “Just testing if the floor’s level.”

Meng Jiangnü lightly brushed that fine ball of thread against Fan Xiliang’s palm, her laugh drifting like a spring breeze over the fields—soft and mischievous. She then pulled out a cloth measuring tape, meticulously checking the thread’s length and width, a hint of seriousness and anticipation flashing in her eyes. She silently vowed to finish the coat before the cold set in, giving her husband a warm garment. Fan Xiliang, meanwhile, padded the loose wooden foot of his ancient loom with an old cloth and fashioned a small wooden frame to keep the thin silk from tumbling around like a mischievous kitten. Unfortunately, the first time the thread ball was placed, it bounced down like a dandelion in the wind, rolling straight off. Fan Xiliang scrambled under the table, snatching it up while holding a fallen leaf, muttering, “Just checking the floor’s even,” as if giving the mishap a silent trial. Aman happened to be returning from the well at the village entrance, hurried steps, and stopped beneath the old locust tree. He looked up to see Fan Xiliang crouched under the table, clutching the thread ball, while Meng Jiangnü stood nearby with a ruler, measuring intently. Aman took a green bamboo slip, wrote lightly, “Newlyweds prepare winter clothes; husband first tangled by a ball of thread.” He glanced up at Fan Xiliang, a faint smile curling his mouth, as if saying, “Is this worth noting?” Fan Xiliang looked up from under the table, a mix of embarrassment and helplessness in his eyes, and whispered, “Is this worth noting?”

Fan Xiliang looked up from under the table: “Is this worth noting?”

Aman, dead‑serious, replied, “The little chronicler never thinks a thing is too small. Besides, the thread ball won outright.”

Meng Jiangnü gave him a bowl of water and told him to record only the winter coat, not to write a saga about the thread ball. Aman made two quick edits and left, not lingering in the couple’s domestic drama.

In the days that followed, Fan Xiliang spent his mornings fixing plows for neighbors and returned to tend the cotton for Meng Jiangnü; while she cooked, he sat by the lamp untangling the knotted thread one strand at a time. One day he boasted that he’d straightened everything, but when Meng Jiangnü lifted a knot, three thread balls dangled together. They stared at each other for a moment, and Fan Xiliang admitted first, “They probably don’t want to be separated.”

“Then you’ll have to persuade them.”

These tiny incidents weren’t earth‑shaking, but they made life feel hopeful. When Meng Jiangnü stitched the first stitch, Fan Xiliang promised that when the cold wind blew, he’d wear the new coat and never hide the ragged old jacket in the bottom of the cupboard again. He also said he’d plant beans in the vacant corner of the yard next spring; Meng Jiangnü planned to add another blanket so the house wouldn’t have to bundle the lamps up every winter night.

That night the wind truly rose outside. The freshly glued window paper whispered softly but didn’t crack. Fan Xiliang added more firewood, Meng Jiangnü dropped a needle by the fire, and the fabric slowly came together. Both thought the biggest challenge this year would simply be keeping the thread from knotting and keeping the north wind out.

When the conscription officers entered the village, Fan Xiliang was busy repairing a doorway threshold.

The first drumbeat sounded from the east side of the village; dogs barked, voices rose, and footsteps crowded the narrow lanes. The levy officers carried a roster, checking each household, announcing that the court was recruiting labor to repair the Great Wall, and any man whose name was called had to join the team that very day.

Fan Xiliang’s name was read out loud and clear.

Meng Jiangnü stepped to the door and begged the officer to let her settle the house’s affairs, even if it meant a day’s delay. The leader shook his head, saying the roster already bore a mark; missing one man meant they’d have to pull another from the same clan or neighboring families. Fan Xiliang had his hammer clenched tight; hearing this, he slowly released his grip.

“I’ll go,” he said.

Meng Jiangnü turned to him: “You’ve been sick once, the north is about to freeze—how can you just walk away?”

Fan Xiliang didn’t claim he wasn’t afraid. He simply glanced at the neighbors gathered at the lane’s mouth—old folks and youths just old enough to work the fields. If he fled now, the men they needed to replace him would likely be those very people. He put the hammer back in the toolbox and told the officer he needed at least time to change his clothes.

The officer gave him only the time it takes to light a incense stick.

The new winter coat was only half sewn, the cotton still not fully layered. Meng Jiangnü wanted to rush it out, needle in hand, but knew she couldn’t finish in a moment. She pulled out Fan Xiliang’s old coat, tightened the loosest stitches, and tucked it into a small bundle of dry provisions.

“I’ll bring the new coat later,” she said.

Fan Xiliang had meant to tell her not to go far, but seeing her resolve, he changed his words to, “Wait for me to come back and wear it.”

“When?”

“They say the work won’t take long; we should be back before winter.”

Meng Jiangnü wouldn’t settle for a vague promise. She chased the departing convoy to the village gate and asked the escort where they were headed. Some said north first, others that they’d split at the pass. She noted the ferry and post‑road the troops would cross and asked where a family could send a garment to meet them. The leader, annoyed by her questions, waved them on with a single line, “Take the official road toward the Great Wall.”

As the convoy set off, Fan Xiliang found himself wedged among many laborers. He looked back and saw his doorway still missing a piece, so he told Meng Jiangnü that there were spare wood shims in the toolbox; if the door swayed, she could jam them in.

Meng Jiangnü’s eyes welled up: “You’re still worrying about the door now?”

“If the door’s steady, you’ll sleep through the night without waking.”

She handed him his old coat, not letting go right away. Fan Xiliang whispered that he’d look out for his fellow villagers and asked her not to head north alone in the worst snow. Meng Jiangnü replied simply, “If you return on time, I won’t have to go.”

The officer urged them onward again. Their hands finally part, Fan Xiliang cradled the old coat to his chest. Meng Jiangnü followed the convoy to the village edge, then past the old locust tree by the fields. Fan Xiliang glanced back several times, his figure gradually swallowed by the stream of people ahead and behind.

A woman lunged to block the convoy, only to be pulled back by relatives; another tossed a flatbread far ahead to her husband, the bread landing in mud but still being scooped up and wiped clean by a laborer. There were no drums, no fanfare, no proclamations of heroism. They marched north carrying unfinished harvests, half‑built roofs, and words left unsaid, driven by a roster toward the frontier.

Meng Jiangnü stood in the wind until the convoy vanished. When she returned home, the half‑finished doorway indeed gave a slight wobble. She found the wood shim Fan Xiliang had mentioned, propped the door steady, but didn’t shut it tight.

On the table lay the unfinished winter coat, the needle paused at the spot where Fan Xiliang had left before departing. Meng Jiangnü sat down again, stitching one stitch at a time forward. Fan Xiliang’s promise was to return before winter; her task was to finish the garment that could keep the north wind at bay.

The leaves in the fields turned from green to gold, and Fan Xiliang never came back.

At first, Meng Jiangnü left the gate ajar every evening. When the wind blew, the threshold would creak, and she’d think someone was pushing the door; but when she stepped into the courtyard, all she saw were locust leaves rustling across the ground. Later, as the nights grew colder, she still set aside a portion of food, but the next day when she reheated it, the porridge in the bowl would always cool faster than she could wait for it.

The villagers who had been conscripted never sent word. Some tried to have traveling merchants deliver a letter, but the note vanished halfway; others went to the county office and only got the vague reply “the work sections are constantly rotating.” Meng Jiangnü refused to sit at home and waste days on guesses, so she took Fan Xiliang’s name and hometown and went from house to house asking other families who had also lost loved ones.

An eastern neighbor recalled the troops crossing the great river, while an old father in the western village knew the laborers had split into two groups at the pass; another man who fell ill midway said Fan Xiliang’s unit turned onto the north‑west mountain road. The clues were fragmentary, but they finally stitched together a trail she could follow.

When the first frost fell, Meng Jiangnü tucked the last layer of cotton into her winter coat. Inside the inner seam she sewed a tiny patch of old home cloth, embroidered with a minuscule “Fan” character. Even if Fan Xiliang lent his outer coat to someone else, he wouldn’t mistake that hidden stitch.

A neighbor woman came to help her pack, saw shoes, socks, dried food and herbs tucked in the bundle, and couldn’t help but comment, almost lifting the whole thing.

“Your bundle is packed tighter than my pantry’s winter grain jars.”

Meng Jiangnü pulled out a small earthen bowl. “The road is long; take one less thing.”

The neighbor, eyeing a second pair of shoes, said, “I’m just afraid the road isn’t finished yet and the bundle will convince you to turn back first.”

Finally, Meng Jiangnü let out a brief laugh. She left behind the items she could trade along the way, taking only the winter coat, dried food, the marriage certificate, and the little wooden token Fan Xiliang had made. The smile was short, but her plan was crystal clear: where she could find lodging, which stretch to hitch a ride with a caravan, where she’d hide her money, and, if she couldn’t find anyone, to look for returning laborers.

Her aunt, who had cared for her like a mother, still urged her to wait until spring. Meng Jiangnü hugged the winter coat to her chest and said, “If I wait for spring, the coat will be too late. He promised to come back, and I promised to give him a coat for winter. One of those two promises has to take me north.”

Aman heard she was searching for her husband, spread out a green bamboo slip at the village entrance, and tried to copy every route the families mentioned onto a single page. After writing it front and back, he realized the three paths were tangled together on the slip.

“If this slip could walk, it would probably get lost too,” he said.

Meng Jiangnü pointed to each line for him: “Crossing the river is the common road; the split at the pass turned northwest. Separate the rumors from what you’ve seen with your own eyes.”

Aman hurriedly rewrote, quietly admitting that sometimes the historian’s first job is to note what he doesn’t know. He didn’t decide the direction for Meng Jiangnü; he only copied the verified ferry crossing and mountain trail onto a fresh slip for her. When he tried to help lift the bundle, he lifted it and said, “I’ve checked—this winter coat really shows determination.” The neighbor woman burst out laughing. Meng Jiangnü took the bundle back: “The coat has resolve; the person has even more.”

Aman looked up toward the distant ridge, muttering to himself that his record was truly “unprecedented.” He lightly tapped the tip of his brush on the slip, wrote the characters for “winter coat,” but accidentally wrote “Han” instead of “cold.” The children nearby erupted in giggles; Aman scratched his head awkwardly and whispered, “Well, that’s the ‘Han’ character meaning ‘cold,’ I guess.” Meng Jiangnü smiled and shook her head, saying softly, “Don’t mistake a ‘Han’ for a ‘cold’—our road doesn’t need any extra ‘Han’ characters.”

Along the way, Meng Jiangnü trudged with the heavy bundle, her steps laborious. Aman recorded each footprint, occasionally looking up at the sky as if searching for a sliver of warm light. The mountain path twisted and turned, stones tumbled, wind whistled, and the winter coat swayed gently on her shoulders as if alive, keeping pace with her. Aman spotted a little squirrel hop onto her shoulder and laughed, “Even the squirrel wants to help deliver the coat!” Meng Jiangnü looked up at the squirrel and replied, “It should bring us a ‘pine’ coat too.”

When they finally reached the foot of the Great Wall, Meng Jiangnü’s heart raced. Aman handed her the green bamboo slip, reminding her to check every section of the stone wall. She looked up at the towering ramparts, her eyes misting over. Aman whispered, “See, the wall looks like a giant dragon guarding your future.” Meng Jiangnü nodded, a flash of determination in her gaze.

Aman kept quietly noting beside her, his fingers tapping the slip as if applauding each of her steps. Meng Jiangnü lifted her head, stared at the distant peaks, and silently whispered Fan Xiliang’s name, ready to set out on the quest to find her husband.

The neighbor woman burst out laughing. Meng Jiangnü took the bundle back: “The coat has resolve; the person has even more.”

Before dawn broke, she shut the courtyard gate, handed Fan Xiliang’s unfinished toolbox to a neighbor for safekeeping. This time the door left no gap, because she was no longer waiting in place for someone whose return time was unknown.

The north wind swept down the village road, the winter coat wrapped in the innermost layer. Meng Jiangnü bade farewell to the crowd and set out on the thousand‑mile journey to find her husband. Aman followed the bamboo slip to the fork ahead, noting that she was heading for the great river crossing, then let her lead. The only one who truly had to walk the whole road was Meng Jiangnü herself.

The first stretch of official road beyond the village seemed straight, but by afternoon a landslide blocked it. Travelers were forced onto a narrow field path that could only fit half a foot; below was a shallow ditch just beginning to ice over. Instead of forcing her bundle through, Meng Jiangnü took out a length of cord, re‑tied the bundle tighter, and let the others pass one by one. When it was her turn, a peddler’s bamboo box suddenly popped open, spilling wooden combs, needles, thread, and two rattling drums across the ground.

The peddler scrambled to pick them up, leaning too far and nearly falling into the ditch with his box. Meng Jiangnü steadied him with her shoulder, halted the line behind her, and carefully retrieved each item that had rolled toward the ice. The last drum got stuck under a grass root; the peddler couldn’t reach it, so Meng Jiangnü nudged it with the handle of her umbrella, and the drum “ding‑ding” bounced back onto the path. The peddler sighed in relief, “It never sold, but at least it gave me a safe‑return celebration.”

After the laughter died down, the peddler secured his box again. Learning that she was heading north with the winter coat, he told her the waystation ahead had been abandoned, and if she couldn’t reach the town before dark she could seek shelter at a small earth‑god temple west of the road. He didn’t choose her route; he simply described the condition of the road he’d just traveled. Meng Jiangnü thanked him and kept moving, checking boundary markers and wheel ruts at every fork, refusing to follow anyone who said “the north is all the same” without verifying herself.

As snowflakes began to fall that evening, she hadn’t made it to the town, so she followed the peddler’s advice to the little temple. The gate hung crooked, the roof leaked wind, but the corner of the wall stayed dry. She first inspected the winter coat for dampness, then divided her dried food into a night portion and a next‑day portion. The tinder went out twice in the gusty wind; on the third try she fashioned a tiny windbreak with an empty cloth bag and finally coaxed a weak flame to life.

An elderly woman who had also taken shelter asked if the bundle contained gold or silver. Meng Jiangnü answered that it was her husband’s winter coat. The old woman hefted it lightly, “That garment’s got some weight.” Meng Jiangnü recalled Aman’s similar comment at the village entrance and whispered, “The farther the road goes, the more it seems to know where it’s headed.”

At night the wind slipped through the cracks, sounding like a knocking door. Meng Jiangnü woke several times, keeping the bundle pressed to her side. At sunrise she re‑tied the wandering shoelaces for the old woman, who in return pointed out an old detour that avoided an icy slope. No one completed her search for her husband, but a real piece of information, a courteous yielding of the path, and a shelter allowed her to keep moving onto the next leg.

When Meng Jiangnü reached the great river, days of rain had just raised the water level.

All the ferries were tied up on the shore, and the boatmen said the current was swift; they’d have to wait for the wind to slacken. The banks were crowded with hurried merchants and a few families searching for lost relatives, everyone anxious about delays. Meng Jiangnü asked about the north‑bound labor crews, but the answers conflicted: some said they’d crossed the river from the east, others claimed they’d turned back to the south bank, and still others mistook last year’s convoy for the one they saw this autumn.

She didn’t immediately follow the loudest voice. First she asked when the crews had been seen, then inquired about the laborers’ clothing and the direction they were escorting, and finally she produced Fan Xiliang’s little wooden token, asking if anyone remembered a young man who could fix farm tools and always hung his tools on the left side.

An old man who sold hemp rope remembered that a group of people had indeed stopped at the ferry crossing a few months prior. Among them was a young man who had fixed a broken oar for the boatman and even crafted a wooden shim from scraps. The old man didn’t know the young man’s name, but he clearly recalled him mentioning that his newlywed wife back home was sewing winter clothes.

Meng Jiangnü gripped the wooden token tightly. Fan Xiliang had indeed passed through here.

The wind didn’t let up, but the boat’s canopy tore open a gash first. Fearing the canvas would rip further, the boatman refused to carry passengers, even though the current had slowed slightly. Meng Jiangnü inspected the damage, pulled out some coarse thread, and asked her fellow travelers to hold the canopy down. Her fine needle wasn’t suited for thick canvas, so she borrowed an awl from the old man to pierce the hemp rope. Stitch by stitch, pull by pull, she closed the tear.

The patched canopy wasn’t neat—the stitches were uneven, some long, some short—but it held against the wind. The boatman checked the mooring lines twice more, waiting until the dead branches floating in the river center stopped spinning wildly, before allowing everyone to board. Everyone wanted to cross first, so the bow of the boat quickly crowded with bundles. Meng Jiangnü didn’t push forward; instead, she invited women with children and those with bad legs to sit in the middle cabin first. She placed her winter clothes on her lap, sat near the patched area, and kept a close eye on the stitches to ensure they didn’t come loose.

As soon as the boat left the shore, the river wind pressed the canopy into a deep dip. A merchant thought the canopy was about to collapse and panicked, raising his hand to prop it up, only for the wind to blow his hat into someone else’s arms. The person who caught the hat asked, “Are you protecting the canopy or holding my hat?” The merchant, still propping up the canopy, replied, “I protect whoever is closest to me!” The tense crowd let out a laugh, and the boat’s rocking suddenly seemed less frightening.

Halfway across the river, one stitch in the patch pulled loose. Meng Jiangnü couldn’t stand up to re-sew it, so she threaded a short rope through two canopy rings from the inside, distributing the tension to the intact fabric. She asked someone nearby to hold the end of the rope while she tied a knot. The boatman kept steering steadily, not altering course for this minor scare. By the time the boat reached the north bank, the tear had only widened by a finger’s width and hadn’t ripped further toward the top of the canopy.

After landing, Meng Jiangnü first asked the boatman about the route the labor crew had taken months ago. The boatman couldn’t recall names, but he remembered the team leader fixing a broken wheel on the shore and later asking a young carpenter in the group to shim the axle. The old hemp rope seller added that the cart had headed northwest upon leaving, not along the main northern post road. With these two testimonies matching, she crossed out the most loudly rumored northern route on her notes—the one no one had actually seen with their own eyes.

She returned the leftover coarse thread to the old man, but he only took back the awl, saying she might need the thread on the road. Meng Jiangnü didn’t argue; she wrapped the coarse thread around her small wooden token and continued asking about the next inn where they could stay. She hadn’t crossed the river by rushing into danger once; she had waited for the water to calm, fixed the canopy, arranged seating, and stabilized the tear, completing each step carefully. Searching for Fan Xiliang was the same: she preferred to ask one more time rather than let urgency lead her down the wrong path.

Aman caught up from behind, originally intending to help hold down the canopy. But when the wind gusted, the canvas nearly swallowed him whole. He poked his head out from under the fabric, saying, “I just wanted to take notes, not become the boat canopy myself.”

The boatman pulled him out, saying, “Stand firm. If your green bamboo slips fall into the river, I’m not ferrying words for you.”

Meng Jiangnü didn’t stop working with her hands; she told Aman to press down on the knot. Together with several passengers, they finished repairing the canopy. The boatman promised to ferry these relatives-seeking travelers across first once the water calmed, waiving Meng Jiangnü’s fare.

While waiting, another woman took out half a letter her husband had sent back. Only a few characters remained: “Transferred to the northern garrison.” Her hometown was in the same county as Fan Xiliang’s. Meng Jiangnü compared it with her recorded routes and confirmed that the two teams had merged at the pass and likely turned northwest together afterward.

By evening, the river surface finally smoothed out. The ferry still rocked heavily as it left the shore, so Meng Jiangnü tied her bundle to her chest and held onto the gunwale. A child started crying from fear of the water, so she let him touch the outer layer of her bundle and said, “The winter clothes inside are going to a very far place to find someone. Even the clothes haven’t cried, so you can hold on for a little while too.”

The child asked, “Do clothes walk?”

Aman, squatting nearby, answered, “Not originally. But after meeting Lady Meng, they had to learn.”

A light laugh rippled through the boat, easing the tension slightly. But Meng Jiangnü kept her eyes fixed on the opposite bank. Jokes might make the river journey feel shorter, but they couldn’t answer whether Fan Xiliang was safe.

After landing, the wind made their wet clothes feel even colder. Meng Jiangnü first checked her bundle—the winter clothes were dry—then carefully noted the post road described by the boatman. A fellow traveler invited her to stay at the same inn, and she agreed to walk together to the pass, looking out for each other and continuing to verify news about the conscripted men.

Aman’s path diverged from theirs. He clutched his green bamboo slips to his chest, accompanying them only to the fork in the road on the shore. He wrote down that Meng Jiangnü had earned her crossing with her own needlework and confirmed that Fan Xiliang’s team had turned from here onto the northwest mountain path.

The next morning, Meng Jiangnü continued north along the colder post road. The great river was now behind her. Her bundle was still heavy, but it no longer carried just the weight of longing; it also held the first reliable piece of news that Fan Xiliang had indeed passed through this place.

Past the great river, the mountain paths grew steeper with each passing day.

Meng Jiangnü traveled with a group of women searching for their relatives. They looked out for each other during the day and took shelter in village houses at night. Her shoe soles wore thin on the stone roads, so she used the hemp rope given by the boatman to re-stitch them tightly. Someone suggested she take out the winter clothes and wear them, but she shook her head. “These are for Fan Xiliang. I can still walk, but he might not have any clothes left to change into up north.”

Snow had already fallen at the mountain pass. The group couldn’t distinguish the old path, so Meng Jiangnü found deep ruts pressed into the stone by wheels and asked a woodcutter if he had recently seen a large group of laborers. The woodcutter pointed toward the pass, saying the escort teams often changed badges and split up there. If his name could still be found, there should be a corresponding wooden token.

The guards at the pass refused to let them near the construction site, saying that family members had to wait outside. Meng Jiangnü took out her marriage certificate and the small wooden token made by Fan Xiliang, explaining that she wasn’t there to storm the gate, but to deliver winter clothes, find her husband, and clarify which section he had been assigned to.

The guard flipped through several old registers but couldn’t find Fan Xiliang. He closed the book and told her to return home and wait for news.

“Just because the register doesn’t have it, does that mean the person doesn’t exist?” Meng Jiangnü asked.

“Work sections change quickly. It’s not rare to miss a name.”

“For you, it’s just a missed entry. For me, it’s a whole family.”

She refused to block the gate and argue, so she moved to the roadside to ask sick laborers who were allowed to return home. One said Fan Xiliang had been in the quarrying team; another remembered him fixing a wheelbarrow for everyone, but neither knew his final destination. Meng Jiangnü arranged each piece of information in chronological order and realized they were talking about the same person, part of the same team that first quarried stone and then transported materials.

When Aman arrived at the pass, she had already been asking questions for half the day. Her footsteps had left deep marks on the stone path, and her sweat dripped into the cold wind like strings of small beads. The guard glanced at her, his brows furrowing slightly, but the corners of his mouth twitched upward, as if watching a kitten dance in a corner. Aman looked up and caught a faint, almost imperceptible smile in the guard’s eyes, as if he were thinking, “This woman is quite a spectacle.” She coughed softly, her voice sounding crisp in the cold wind, like a child grumbling, “I’m not here to cause trouble; I just want to ask around.” The guard shook his head, muttering, “A woman with a bundle of clothes won’t ask her way to a city wall.” Aman felt a pang of sadness but couldn’t help smiling softly to herself, whispering, “These ‘winter clothes’ are truly cold; even the wind dares not approach.” She placed her green bamboo slips in a sheltered spot, squatted down, and began copying the old route again. The characters on the slips were slightly blurred by the cold, so she gently traced them with her finger, as if comforting words trembling in the wind. The guard suddenly looked up, a flash of surprise in his eyes, as if seeing something strange, before returning to a serious expression and muttering, “Lady, stop asking. The city wall is not what you want.” Aman looked up, her eyes shining with determination, and replied softly, “She isn’t asking for the wall; she’s asking for a name. The longer the wall, the more the name cannot be omitted.” Her voice was like wind chimes from afar, clear and firm, as if telling the guard that she had only one goal in her heart: to find Fan Xiliang, who had been conscripted for the construction.

Aman answered, “She isn’t asking for the wall; she’s asking for a name. The longer the wall, the more the name cannot be omitted.”

After speaking, he stepped aside, refusing to negotiate with the guard on Meng Jiangnü’s behalf. The laughter from the journey had faded here. The grim faces of the returning laborers and the incomplete registers all indicated that what lay ahead was no ordinary place for visiting relatives.

As evening approached, an old laborer heard the name “Fan Xiliang” and stopped in his tracks. He remembered the young man sharing his rations with a sick companion and using small wooden shims to steady a cart wheel. The old laborer had once walked the north‑west mountain trail with him, but they were later separated at a newly built section of the wall.

“He even said,” the old man said, looking at the bundle in Meng Jiangnü’s arms, “someone from home will bring a winter coat, and we shouldn’t laugh when he keeps looking south.”

Meng Jiangnü hurried to ask where he was now. The old man fell silent for a moment, then only said that when he left, Fan Xiliang was still moving stones beneath that stretch of the wall and had vanished afterward. He sketched the mountain pass and beacon tower’s locations, telling her to first find a laborer named Zhou at the worksite—Zhou had been in the same small squad as Fan Xiliang.

The guard, satisfied that the testimonies matched, finally allowed Meng Jiangnü to join the grain‑caravan the next day, on the condition that she not interfere with the work. She thanked the old man and memorized the route.

At dawn the grain carts rolled out of the gate, but the wheels sank into the frozen, hard ruts as soon as they hit the mountain road. The driver’s whip cracked uselessly, and they had to unload a few sacks of grain. Meng Jiangnü didn’t try to lift the heaviest sack herself; instead she gathered loose stones from the roadside and jammed them into the empty space behind the wheels, then helped push the cart with everyone else. On the first push the stones flew out of the wheel and landed right beside Aman’s open green bamboo slip. He picked one up, examined it, and said, “That stone came from the direction of the Great Wall faster than we did.”

“Then send it back to brace the wheel,” Meng Jiangnü replied.

On the second try, the crew spread a torn straw mat over the stones, and the wheel finally held without slipping. After clearing the frozen rut, the driver pointed out the shape of the beacon tower on the north‑west section: two platforms, one high and one low, with a mountain pass squeezed between them—exactly matching the route the old laborer had drawn. Meng Jiangnü tucked that landmark onto the corner of the cloth wrapper around her marriage certificate, so she wouldn’t have to keep opening the paper in wind and snow.

The farther north they went, the more returning sick laborers they met along the road. Some coughed so hard they could barely finish a sentence; others wore straw sandals with only half a sole left. Meng Jiangnü handed out a few sprigs of medicinal herbs but didn’t unpack Fan Xiliang’s winter coat—that was a promise she had to deliver herself. She used a thick cord to tie a sick worker’s sandal sole securely, and when he heard Fan Xiliang’s name, he recalled a young man on the western slope who used to reinforce the stone‑lifting frames.

The rumors still didn’t confirm where Fan Xiliang was now, but they clarified the western slope route. Meng Jiangnü asked about dates, squads, and sections one by one, keeping identical names and hearsay separate. Aman, stationed at the rear, simply recorded who had actually seen what and never stepped in to ask for her. When they reached the two beacon towers, his slip already listed three corroborating clues: a broken oar repaired, a wheel shored up, a stone‑lifting frame reinforced—all pointing to the same handy carpenter who always thought of his newlywed wife’s winter coat.

The convoy split at the mountain pass. The grain carts headed east, while Meng Jiangnü’s work section lay to the west. The driver urged her to wait for the next convoy, but she saw that it was still early and laborers kept coming and going along the road, so she decided to follow the clear path onward, agreeing that if she couldn’t find a work shelter before nightfall she would return to the beacon tower. She wasn’t careless because she was finally close; the nearer she got to the answer, the more she needed to walk the whole way.

Just before they crossed the last ridge, the north wind loosened a knot on her bundle. Meng Jiangnü stopped in the wind‑shadow, re‑tied it, and checked that the winter coat, marriage certificate, and wooden plaque were all there. The gray‑black city wall was still hidden in the distance, but the shouts of workers drifted intermittently. She paused a moment before moving on, because she could tell those weren’t welcoming cries for family—they were the endless calls of people hauling stones in the bitter cold.

The next day, after crossing the ridge, a thin line of gray‑black wall stretched across the snowy plain. Meng Jiangnü saw the Great Wall for the first time. Far off, the workers’ shouts were cut short by the wind, and countless silhouettes shuffled at the wall’s base, too small to make out faces.

She clutched the winter coat and headed toward the construction site. Aman only wrote on his green bamboo slip that she had reached the last work section, then closed the booklet. From there on, every person she asked could only push the answer farther from hope.

The wind at the foot of the wall was harsher than at the mountain pass, cutting words in half as it blew.

Meng Jiangnü first found the supply tent and announced Fan Xiliang’s name. The overseer flipped through the current roster, said the man wasn’t there, and sent her to the eastern work section. The eastern supervisor replied that the material carriers had already been reassigned to the western slope. She walked back and forth with the coat, only to hear “transferred,” “don’t remember,” and “ask elsewhere” each time.

In the past she might have taken those answers to mean Fan Xiliang was simply farther away. But the missing‑name ledger she’d seen earlier reminded her: a name can be lost on paper, and a person can be pushed on to the next spot.

She stopped asking the overseer and instead crouched at a laborer’s rest spot, pulling out Fan Xiliang’s small wooden plaque. She described how he repaired wheels, always carried tools in his left hand, and shared rations with the sick. A young laborer recognized the carving style and said he’d used a wooden shovel that Fan Xiliang had fixed; another said that when the first snowstorm hit, Fan Xiliang gave his old coat to a shivering comrade.

“Then what did he wear?” Meng Jiangnü asked.

No one answered at once.

She kept searching the work line for a laborer named Zhou and finally found an old man in a wind‑sheltered stone shed. When he heard why she was there, he first glanced at the bundle, then lowered his head. He had indeed been in Fan Xiliang’s squad and had seen him during the last few days.

The deadline was tight; laborers worked from before dawn until nightfall. Fan Xiliang’s old coat could no longer fend off the cold, so he gave the relatively intact layer to a companion. That night the snow and wind blocked the road, yet he still helped haul stones. By the next morning, he could not stand up again.

Meng Jiangnü asked, “Where’s the doctor?”

The old man shook his head.

“Then where did you take him?”

The old man’s lips moved a few times before he said Fan Xiliang was dead. The site wouldn’t halt work, and there wasn’t time to send the dead laborer home, so they buried him with many others at the foot of the newly built Great Wall. Later, earth covered the site, even the simple markers were hidden.

Meng Jiangnü stood where she was, still holding the coat aloft. She had crossed rivers, mountain paths, and passes, keeping the winter coat dry and intact the whole way; now, at the point closest to Fan Xiliang, the garment could no longer reach a living hand.

“Where exactly?” she asked.

The old man led her to a section of wall that looked slightly newer in color, pointing to the spot near the mountain pass. He couldn’t say which stone or earth it was buried under, only that before Fan Xiliang was laid to rest, his larger work badge lay beside him; the corner of the badge bore a curved scar he’d made while repairing it himself.

A supervisor overheard their conversation, came over, and scolded them, saying too many people were buried under the wall to stop work and dig for one family. Meng Jiangnü produced the marriage certificate and demanded that Fan Xiliang’s name be added to the death register. The supervisor replied that the old ledger was sealed and told her to take the coat back, treating it as if her husband had died in the northern lands.

“Treat it as what?” Meng Jiangnü finally looked up. “He had a name, a family, a promise of a winter coat to bring home. You bury him under the wall and think you can erase who he was?”

The surrounding laborers froze their work. Some knew Fan Xiliang, some had comrades buried in the wall, but no one’s family had ever come that far to ask face‑to‑face.

Meng Jiangnü walked to the spot the old man had pointed out and placed the winter coat on her lap. She felt the embroidered “Fan” character on the collar—her own handiwork—and remembered the threshold at home that was only half finished. Fan Xiliang had promised to return before winter, and the northern lands had already seen a few snowfalls. She gently stroked the stitching, as if she could hear her husband's breath. The fabric of the coat whispered a soft rustle in her hands, like a north wind murmuring in her ear. She lifted her gaze to the distance, imagining Fan Xiliang bearing a heavy load, his steps sinking in slushy snow, his ankles turning a faint violet from the cold. Along the way she met an elderly farmer carrying a basket of freshly peeled radishes. The farmer chuckled, “This coat isn’t as pricey as a radish, but it’ll keep you from freezing like a wooden puppet.” Meng Jiangnü giggled, handed him a radish, and received a warm flatbread in return, their laughter echoing in the biting wind. Continuing onward, she crossed a wheat field buried under snow; each step made the snow crack and squeak. Her sleeves fluttered in the wind, looking as if she were wrapped in a thin veil of mist. She stopped, looked up at the sky, and seemed to pour her longing into the heavens for her far‑away husband. At last she reached the edge of the Great Wall, its towering stones disappearing into the clouds, cold seeping out of every crack. She laid the winter coat gently on her lap, brushed the embroidered “Fan” again, and her eyes welled up involuntarily. She did not leave. As dusk fell she remained seated at the wall’s foot, facing the part of the wall that buried her husband. The grief pressing on her chest had not yet spoken, yet it was already so heavy that no one dared to urge her onward.

At sunrise, Meng Jiangnü again begged to search for Fan Xiliang.

The foreman still refused. “Once the wall is built, we can’t tear it down for a single dead man; the laborers’ life‑records are kept, and once a family has wept, they should go home.” When Meng Jiangnü asked where that register was, he could not produce it and ordered her taken away from the work path.

An elderly man named Zhou and several laborers stepped in front of her. They said nothing violent, only that Fan Xiliang was indeed buried there, and many of his comrades had no names recorded at all. More and more workers stopped, quietly staring at the wall’s base. Seeing the crowd cease work, the foreman stepped back a few paces but still refused to let anyone dig.

Meng Jiangnü spread the winter coat fully, laying it on the icy ground.

First she took out Fan Xiliang’s small wooden tag and placed it on the coat’s collar. Then she laid out the marriage certificate, an old piece of household cloth, and the notes she had kept of his whereabouts along the way. Each item proved that the person trapped beneath the wall was not a blank entry on any register.

“Fan Xiliang,” she called out to the Great Wall, “the coat is ready.”

The wind swept over the wall’s crest, offering no reply.

She spoke of the paper they once glued over their windows after marriage, how he’d tangled three skeins of thread together and complained they wouldn’t let go; of the threshold finally set firm and the toolbox watched over; of the rice bowl reheated countless times and the courtyard gate waiting from autumn until frost. The words she had planned to say face‑to‑face now had to be sent, one by one, into stone and earth.

“You promised to come back wearing it,” she called again. “I promised to bring it. My words have reached you—where are you?”

Her voice struck the wall, leaving only the sound of wind.

At first Meng Jiangnü only wept. She pressed her hand to the coat, as if fearing the north wind would steal the last warmth she could give her husband. But when she recalled Fan Xiliang giving his old coat to a sick friend, her own collapse on a snowy night, and his nameless burial, the sorrow she had held in for so long burst out of her mouth.

She cried out in a raw, unrestrained sob.

In her cries were the lantern light of a newlywed cottage, the final farewell at the village entrance, the bundle she’d guarded while crossing a river, and the fleeting “just consider it” whispered at the wall’s foot. The laborers around lowered their heads; some began calling out the names of dead comrades. Relatives searching for loved ones gathered by the wall, their wails layering upon each other.

The wind suddenly stopped.

Meng Jiangnü kept weeping, but a deep, low rumble rose from the wall’s crest. It wasn’t the sound of hammers or wheels. It rolled from deep within the stone, as if countless sighs long suppressed were waking at once. The bricks shivered lightly, and dust fell from the seams.

The foreman shouted for everyone to retreat in panic. Meng Jiangnü didn’t move; she kept calling Fan Xiliang to the wall. Each shout made the wall tremble more. First a stone loosened, then a long crack split the wall, stretching from its base up toward the heights.

“Give them back!” Meng Jiangnü shouted through her tears.

A thunderous roar drowned out the wind.

The section of wall shattered under her cries. Bricks, tamped earth, and stones tumbled to either side, clouds of dust rising to reveal the previously sealed foundation. The onlookers stepped back to safety, yet could still hear Meng Jiangnü’s sobs echo through the roar.

The collapse was no ordinary fall, nor a simple mistake by the builders. It was a woman who had walked a thousand miles, finally learning her husband lay in an anonymous grave, forcing the silent wall to give up its secret.

Dust settled slowly, and the wall’s foundation showed layer after layer of buried traces. Meng Jiangnü couldn’t stop her tears, but she rose gradually. She knew Fan Xiliang lay beneath the opened earth. Her next step would be to recognize her husband among the forgotten.

When the dust cleared, many skeletal remains were exposed beneath the broken wall.

The foreman, who had just been urging everyone to leave, fell silent. The laborers set down their tools and began clearing the loose earth piece by piece, no longer chanting the wall‑building cadence. Meng Jiangnü cradled the winter coat and followed the elderly Zhou toward the burial spot he remembered.

There were more than just Fan Xiliang here. Some bodies still clung to frayed grass ropes; others bore wooden tags etched with their hometowns. Relatives on the perimeter called out names one by one, while living comrades tried to recall which team each had been on, what tools they’d used, and where they’d been laid.

Elder Zhou found a broken work badge. A curved nick on its corner matched the mark Fan Xiliang had left when he patched it. Beside it lay a small wooden wedge with a faint line pattern carved into it.

Meng Jiangnü recognized the wedge. When Fan Xiliang repaired his home’s threshold, he had made a pair—one stayed in the toolbox, the other he slipped into his old coat, thinking it might be useful on the road north. She placed the little wooden tag she’d brought beside the work badge; the two carvings were identical.

The elder recited the final burial order, and several laborers from the same crew came forward to confirm. Meng Jiangnü finally verified that the bones before her were indeed Fan Xiliang’s.

She did not call the wrong name, nor did she ask why he never returned home. The answer had been pressed beneath that wall for far too long. She gently laid the new winter coat over him, smoothed the collar, and placed the embroidered “Fan” close to the work badge.

“The north wind can’t reach you now,” she whispered.

Soft cries rose around her. Other relatives, seeing her identify her husband with the token, began searching for their own tags and keepsakes. The laborers recounted every known hometown, facial feature, and team experience, giving names to the previously nameless skeletons.

Aman arrived outside the broken wall segment, keeping his distance from Meng Jiangnü. He opened a green bamboo slip and, in order, wrote down the conscription, the coat delivery, the death notice, the weeping, the wall’s collapse, and the bone identification.

Meng Jiangnü asked everyone to choose a quiet plot of land to bury Fan Xiliang. The foreman at first wanted to order a rapid rebuild, but faced with the many relatives naming their dead and the laborers who had stopped work, he could no longer treat them as just another nameless foundation. Together they dug a grave on a sunny slope, laying Fan Xiliang to rest alongside those whose identities had been confirmed.

The winter coat was not taken back. Meng Jiangnü let it accompany Fan Xiliang into the earth, and the old household cloth sewn inside the collar was left there too. She kept the marriage certificate close to her heart, for it was not only proof of his identity but also proof that he once had a home to return to.

After the burial, she sat by the grave until sunset. In the distance the broken wall left a huge gap, wind rushing through it, and nothing could ever again trap a cry inside the stones.

When Meng Jiangnü rose, her husband did not miraculously return. What she gained was a grave with a name and a truth that could no longer be erased by a casual “just consider him dead in the north.”

As twilight deepened, she gently touched the three characters “Fan Xiliang” on the tombstone. Aman closed the green bamboo slip and followed the group out of the cemetery. The wind remained cold, the Great Wall still stretched long, but beneath that broken segment the bones were no longer anonymous, and the winter coat finally reached the one it was meant for.
