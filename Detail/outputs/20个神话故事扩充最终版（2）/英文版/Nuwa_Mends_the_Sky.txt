Before the heavens fell out of balance, the people had just finished harvesting a season’s grain.

In the early morning, smoke still curled up from the village rooftops when a thunderous roar erupted from the northwest. Mount Buzhou was slammed by a massive force and toppled, snapping the pillars that held up the sky. The heavens first sagged to one side, then split open with a black fissure that quickly stretched far into the distance.

Before anyone could fully see what was happening, flames rained down from the sky crack. The fireballs plunged into the forest, and the wind whisked them along the treetops. The ground tilted downward, rivers spilled over their old banks, dragging mud and sand into the fields. Wild beasts driven out by the fire surged toward the village, and both people and animals scrambled for any escape route.

An old man tried to run back to his house for grain, only to be blocked by a rushing flood. Several children were stranded on a high stone in the drying yard as the water kept rising around them. Some people beat at sparks on the roof, others hauled livestock up the slope, and the shouts of names were quickly drowned out by the roar of water and the cracking earth.

When Nüwa arrived from the Central Plains, the first thing she saw was the ever‑widening crack in the sky.

She didn’t rush up to seal the fissure right away. There were still living people trapped on the ground, and if she didn’t move them first, the next wave of fire would wipe out their only way out. Nüwa lifted her hand and pushed back the torrent surging toward the drying yard, using earth and stones to fashion a temporary curved levee so the old man and the children could climb the embankment to higher ground.

A frightened beast barreled into the crowd. Nüwa didn’t chase it deep into the forest; instead she tossed a vine rope to steer it into an empty valley, then turned back to douse the flames threatening the granary. She told the villagers not to scatter, to first count their children and elders, and to bring the weak and wounded together to the leeward side of the rocky slope.

The sky cracked again with a loud snap. More fire fell along the edge of the black fissure, and water that had been held back burst in from the opposite side. Nüwa looked up and realized this wasn’t a random series of floods and fires: the sky‑supporting pillars had broken, the four corners had lost their footing; the sky split, the earth tilted, and water and fire ran wild together.

“Which side do we rescue first?” someone asked from the slope.

“Save the people right in front of us first, and the heavens will follow,” Nüwa replied.

She moved a massive boulder to block the next surge of flood and diverted a mountain spring away from the fire. The villagers split into teams as she directed: one team hauled grain and water, another guarded the makeshift levee, a third beat wooden drums to scare off the approaching beasts. Nüwa stayed at the most dangerous spots, shuttling back and forth, each action buying only a brief moment of safety.

The first round of teamwork didn’t go smoothly. Those guarding the levee all tried to stand at the narrowest gap, crowding into a tight bunch and accidentally blocking a basket of soil behind them; the grain‑carriers feared sparks would land in their sacks, clutched their loads tightly, and no one had a free hand to comfort a crying child. Nüwa stepped onto a high stone and drew three lines on the ground with a long vine: “Levee guards take the first line, grain carriers the second, elders and children the highest third. Don’t crowd the same path.”

Aman was in charge of copying the three routes onto a green bamboo slip. He had just finished writing when he looked up and saw a burly man lugging two bags of grain onto the elders’ path. Aman shouted after him, “Second path! Those grain bags aren’t even heavy enough to need a man’s back yet!” The man glanced down at his bulging sacks, wanted to laugh but couldn’t stop, and awkwardly turned at the fork, nearly colliding with an empty vine basket. The basket tumbled down the slope, and when Aman reached out to stop it, the basket’s rim caught his head.

“I see the road, but the road can’t see me!” he muttered through the basket’s opening.

Two children, who had been sobbing so hard they could barely breathe, burst into giggles when they saw him fumbling with the basket. A nearby woman lifted the basket, let one child grab its edge, and led the other up the third path. Aman rubbed his forehead, intending to note the scene on the slip, but Nüwa was already back from the fire: “Count the people first; we’ll add the jokes later.” He immediately joined the line for the headcount and discovered an elder who was supposed to guard the granary was missing.

Nüwa followed the shouts muffled by smoke to the granary. An elder was beating at sparks on the roof with a damp mat, a seed jar clutched at his feet that he was reluctant to abandon. Nüwa steadied a collapsing beam, urged the elder to take the jar and flee, and she ripped the burning thatch off the roof and tossed it onto empty ground. When the two returned to the high slope, the three teams were no longer chaotic: the levee crew filled the gaps with baskets of earth, the grain crew stacked the sacks higher, and the children took turns beating wooden basins in the leeward spot to drive away the frightened beasts.

This arrangement didn’t stop the world from tilting, but when the next flood came, no one was left blocking the slope’s mouth. Nüwa saw the elder hand the seed jar to a child for safekeeping, then turned to face another falling fire. The precious seconds she bought weren’t wasted; the people had learned to protect themselves and each other in those fleeting moments.

Dusk should have settled, yet the cracked sky glowed a fierce red from the flames. Distant peaks continued to topple, and river channels kept shifting. Nüwa climbed the highest rock and saw one corner of the heavens sagging low enough to press against the mountains, while the opposite side revealed a dark, yawning void.

Blocking water only outside the village can’t stop the flood, and burning a patch of forest won’t halt the next blaze. If the sky crack keeps widening, every temporary safe spot will eventually be swallowed.

Nüwa turned to the crowd, urging them to hold the high ground, and said she would scout in all directions to find a way to mend the sky and prop up the heavens again. She left a wall of earth to shield the refugees and then headed toward the deepest part of the fissure.

That night, no one treated the disaster as just another storm. The sky crack, flood, fire, and fleeing beasts had all descended together. Nüwa also knew that what needed fixing wasn’t a single village, but the entire world that had lost its support.

Nüwa first walked toward the direction where the sky was sinking.

In the northwest mountains a support had vanished, so the sky tilted toward that spot; the southeast ground sank accordingly, rivers rushed in, and the flood grew deeper. She set stone markers along the way, recording how the crack spread and how the water changed course, and she checked which mountains could still bear the fiery trials.

Aman chased after her from the high refuge, clutching the green bamboo slip. He had intended to draw the sky’s wound, looked up several times, and in the end only left a long line on the slip.

“Why did you draw only one line?” Nüwa asked.

“The crack keeps growing. If I kept drawing, the slip would have to stretch all the way to the horizon.”

Nüwa told him not to rush to map the whole thing, just to note each observation’s spot and change. Comparing the stone markers, she saw the same fissure was wide in the northwest and narrowed toward the southeast, showing that a patch‑up couldn’t start haphazardly in the middle; otherwise the edges would keep tearing apart.

They reached a ridge surrounded by water. Nüwa stacked stones to make a temporary bridge, letting the trapped people escape. Someone begged her to stay and completely block the river mouth, but she pointed out that the upstream ground was still tilting, so even a high wall here would only force the water to flow around it. Aman, finger pressed to the water, muttered, “This water feels like it wants to play hide‑and‑seek with us.” A child nearby looked up and laughed, “Then it must be trying to run away!” Nüwa raised her hand, gently nudging a big stone toward the bank; the stone rolled with a crisp clatter, as if tapping a rhythm for the river. She tapped the stone with her palm, and it slid across the water like a tiny boat bobbing on waves. Then she piled several stones into a makeshift dam; the top of the dam fizzed where the water lapped, looking like a bubble wall. Aman pointed at the dam and the water, saying, “See, the river’s saying hello.” An elder nearby chuckled, “That river really knows how to talk.” Nüwa nodded, kept stacking stones more securely until the dam was safe enough for passage. Her movements were steady and rhythmic, as if weaving a safe channel for the river. Aman recorded each stone’s placement, ensuring he wouldn’t miss any detail later. Villagers watched, some shouting, “Hurry, don’t let the water sweep us away!” Nüwa replied calmly, “The river is nature’s force; we just need to guard it with care.” Her voice rang like a gentle echo in the valley, warm and resolute. Aman looked far ahead, noting silently: this time Nüwa’s stone bridge was both a shield against disaster and a guardian of humanity.

“Is there no way out?” a villager asked.

“There is, but we can’t just treat this one rush of water.”

Nüwa divided the crisis in the heavens and the earth into three interconnected tasks: first, to refine the five-colored stones that could withstand the heavenly fire to seal the tear in the sky; second, to re-establish the four pillars of the sky that had lost their heavenly column; and third, to eliminate the fierce creatures that were harming people with the floodwaters and guide the accumulated water back into the rivers. If the order were reversed, the places that had been repaired would still be torn open by the tilted sky.

To determine whether ordinary stones could be used to repair the sky, they had to be tested in the disaster. Nüwa picked up a few stones that had been burned by the falling fire, and with a light touch, they crumbled into dust. She then took some stones from the rapid currents, and most of them had already developed cracks from the impact. What she was looking for was not colorful decorations, but divine stones that could preserve their nature in the face of fire, water, and heavy pressure.

Aman bowed his head and wrote "five-colored stones," and when he finished, he asked, "If the five colors are in the wrong order, will the sky look a bit skewed?"

"Let's find them first and then talk about how they look," Nüwa said. "They are for repairing the tear, not for changing the sky's clothes."

He quickly erased the sentence about the colors and only left the criteria for selecting the stones. He did not help Nüwa search for the stones, nor could he decide on the materials for her; he was only responsible for keeping the messages from various places from getting mixed up.

Nüwa returned to the high ground where people were taking refuge and arranged for them to reinforce the temporary earth embankments. She also asked those familiar with the mountain roads to indicate which places still had intact, colorful stones after the fierce fires, and which river valleys had their old stone layers exposed by the floodwaters. The clues soon pointed to five different regions.

Some people volunteered to follow her, but Nüwa only let them be in charge of transportation and taking care of the evacuation route. The search, identification, and final refining of the stones had to be done by her personally, because only she could withstand the power of the heavenly fire, and only she knew how the materials for repairing the sky should match the tears.

At night, Nüwa again inspected the stone markers. The tear in the sky was still expanding, and the four pillars were continuing to sink. The time left for the human world was limited. She set the first destination as the mountain valley that had been burned by the heavenly fire but still glowed with red light. She then proceeded to the riverbed, the windy pass, the collapsed mountain, and the old riverbed.

Aman divided the three tasks into separate records on the green bamboo slips, finally giving up trying to fit the entire sky onto one line. Nüwa, on the other hand, set out with a clear order: first, to find the five-colored stones and refine them to repair the sky; second, to stabilize the four directions; and third, to calm the floodwaters that were still surging in the human world.

The first divine stone was hidden in the mountain valley that had been burned by the heavenly fire.

The grass and trees in the valley had already withered, and ordinary rocks crumbled with a light touch. However, one red stone was still glowing in the remaining flames. Nüwa waited for the fire to subside, then personally took it out from the cracked rock layer. The red glow did not dim after leaving the flames, indicating that it could withstand the most intense heat when repairing the sky.

The second clue was in the old riverbed that had been flooded by the waters. Nüwa dove into the turbid current and felt a blue stone being pressed between two water flows. Years of impact had not caused it to crack, but instead made its surface more smooth. She lifted the blue stone, and the water flow changed direction, revealing the buried old shore.

Aman stood guard at a high point and saw Nüwa come out of the water. He quickly wrote on the green bamboo slip, "Blue stone obtained from the river bottom." The wind blew water droplets onto the slip, and the character "blue" became blurred.

"Should this be recorded as a water-colored stone?" he asked.

Nüwa looked at it and said, "The stone hasn't changed; it's your writing that got wet first."

Aman wiped the slip dry and rewrote the origin clearly, not allowing his ink marks to change the color of the divine stone.

The white stone was on the high ridge that was exposed to the wind all year round. The strong gusts could lift and shatter rocks, but Nüwa climbed against the wind and saw the white stone firmly embedded in the ridge. She did not use brute force to pull it out but instead stabilized the shaking mountain body and then removed the stone along its grain, lest the entire ridge collapse towards the people taking refuge below.

The black stone was under the collapsed mountain. Several villagers wanted to work together to move the massive rock, but Nüwa told them to retreat to a safe place first. She then supported the fallen mountain wall herself. The black stone was still intact despite bearing the weight of the mountains, making it suitable for repairing the sky's most sunken side.

The last yellow stone was buried in the deep mud of the dried-up old river. It had been soaked by the floodwaters and then endured the scorching sun after the water receded, without shattering or fading. When Nüwa took it out, the five stones reflected each other, and the five colors of red, blue, white, black, and yellow were finally complete.

Each stone Nüwa found responded to different disasters: some had withstood fierce fires, some had been washed by floodwaters, some had not cracked in the wind, some could hold up mountains, and some had maintained their original shape despite experiencing both wet and dry conditions. Nüwa only judged based on the traces left by the disasters and did not destroy the mountains and rivers to conduct further tests.

Transportation was still a difficult task. Many mountain roads were broken, and the five-colored stones could not collide with each other. Nüwa used vines to fix them separately and personally supported the heaviest black stone. The villagers only took turns pulling on the flat sections of the road. When they encountered sudden floodwaters blocking the road, she first sent the stones to higher ground and then went back to assist the transporters.

When they reached the middle of the mountain, a stone bridge had been broken into two pieces by the previous tremors, leaving only a narrow ridge that could barely fit the tip of a foot. The red stone was afraid of colliding with the blue stone, and the white stone had to avoid the heavy pressure of the black stone. The original single-file procession could not pass through at the same time. Nüwa placed the five stones on stable rock platforms, first crossing the narrow ridge alone, and then pressing and checking each place to confirm which stone surfaces could bear her weight.

The villagers used vines to create a triangular rope bridge between the two ends, not to lift the divine stones, but to stabilize the people crossing. The first person to pull the rope was too nervous and tied the knot at his waist multiple times, eventually creating a large lump. Aman squatted beside him and counted for a long time, "One, two, three... are you crossing the bridge or trying to pickle yourself in the rope?" The man looked down and couldn't even tell where the rope head was, so he had to let the older person behind him untie the knot.

They laughed and joked, but when it was time to actually cross the bridge, everyone became quiet. Nüwa first carried the red stone across, and after it was stable, she protected it with a divine power. She then transported the blue, white, and yellow stones one by one. Each time she sent a stone over, she returned to the original place, never letting the divine stones lose her control during the process. The black stone was the heaviest, and the narrow ridge creaked under the pressure. The villagers did not rush forward to support it but instead tightened the vines on both sides to prevent loose rocks from falling and hitting the people below.

Aman stood at the end point, checking the colors, and when he said "black," the wind blew a patch of dust onto his face. The person next to him looked at the divine stone and then at Aman, saying, "Now there are two black ones." Aman wiped away two white marks and replied seriously, "One is for repairing the sky, and the other is for keeping accounts; they have different responsibilities." Nüwa put the last black stone in place before allowing everyone to relax and adjust their shoulders.

After the stone bridge, there was still a section of road with accumulated water. The procession did not speed up after crossing the dangerous bridge but instead increased the distance between the five stones. People in the front used wooden poles to probe the road, while those in the back collected the vines. Nüwa constantly moved between the five stones, watching over them. Only when the five-colored light was complete and unbroken on the high ground of the furnace field did everyone truly breathe a sigh of relief. Aman also drew the incorrectly tied knot in the corner of the slip, noting, "This knot is only suitable for joking, not for crossing the bridge."

Aman followed at the end, responsible for ensuring that none of the stones were left behind. He counted twice but still wasn't reassured, so he recited the colors one by one. The person next to him said that they were all visible, but Aman replied, "Last time, I tried to draw the sky's tear on one page, and I've come to realize that sometimes eyes can be more flawed than the slips."

He held a pale blue stone in his hand, gently tapping it, and it emitted a clear, crisp sound, as if he was greeting the stone. Aman looked up at the distant mountain peaks, which swayed in the wind, as if they were also greeting him.

"Don't forget this red one," a village woman said with a smile, still shaking a small wooden stick in her hand. The stick accidentally hit a stone on the ground, which rolled a few steps away, and Aman quickly chased after it, but in the process, he accidentally dropped a gray stone into the river.

Aman lifted his head, and the corners of his mouth slightly curled up, as if he had just heard a joke. He whispered to himself, "Why are my hands so slippery?"

He put the stone back in its original place and continued to recite the colors, his voice singing a small song to each stone.

"Don't worry, I'll remember each one," he said softly, his eyes shining with determination.

The person next to him laughed and gently patted Aman's shoulder, as if saying, "Don't be afraid, we'll repair the sky together."

Aman nodded and continued to follow the procession, ensuring that each stone was safe and sound.

The crowd laughed and soon continued on their way. The laughter was only used to alleviate the fatigue of transporting the stones and did not make Aman the main force behind finding the stones.

Upon arriving at the high ground, Nüwa placed the five-colored stones around the divine furnace. She first swept a strand of heavenly fire across the surface of the stones, and all five divine stones remained intact, each emitting a stable glow. This confirmation only needed to be done once, which was enough to prove that they came from different dangerous places but could withstand the same heavenly fire used for repairing the sky.

Aman finished writing down the colors and their origins, then stepped back out of the furnace yard. The five-colored stones were finally all gathered. Nüwa turned to face the divine fire and began to figure out how to truly melt the five different stone natures into one.

Nüwa set the divine furnace on the high ground, on the side sheltered from the wind.

She surrounded the heart of the furnace with mountain rocks tough enough to withstand heavenly fire, then drew the spark from the embers falling through the cracks. The five-colored stones were placed inside one by one. Their surfaces lit up quickly, but they didn’t melt right away. Mending the sky didn’t mean just roasting a few stones red-hot; it meant turning these divine stones into a single, unified force strong enough to seal the heavens.

A sudden gust of mountain wind swept across the furnace yard, pushing the flames to one side. Nüwa moved a giant boulder to block the wind and stood herself at the opening, using her divine power to gather the scattered fire back into the heart of the furnace. Just as the flames steadied, floodwaters from the slope below surged up along newly cracked channels, soaking the outer edge of the furnace base.

The wood-carriers panicked and tried to block the water with dirt. Nüwa stopped them from standing directly in the rushing current. Instead, she told everyone to move the dry firewood to higher ground, then walked to the slope’s edge herself. She didn’t just build a wall; instead, she carved a shallow trench following the natural lay of the land, guiding the floodwaters around the divine furnace and into an empty valley.

As the water changed direction, it swept away one of the protective rocks. Nüwa held up the tilting furnace wall with one hand and pushed the giant stone back into place with the other. Inside, the five-colored stones clattered against each other, their light flashing in chaos. Once she stabilized the furnace, she didn’t start over, nor did she throw away the stones that had already begun to temper. She simply adjusted their positions so each stone faced the heart of the fire again.

Although the furnace walls were steady, the five-colored stones inside had crowded to one side. The red stone pressed against the fire’s heart, glowing so brightly its outline was almost invisible. The black stone sat on the outside, blocking the green stone from reaching the heat. Nüwa couldn’t just reach in and move them; the heat of the five stones was enough to melt ordinary rock. First, she lowered the fire, coaxing the stray flames back into the cracks. Then, she used five stone pestles of different lengths, pressing them against the divine stones from outside the furnace.

The villagers were responsible for holding the ends of the pestles, but they could only push when Nüwa gave the order—they couldn’t fiddle with them on their own. When the first command, “Left, advance,” came, the two people on the left pushed at the same time, but the pestle didn’t budge. The third person, getting nervous, gave it a shove too, and there was a loud *thud* from inside the furnace. Everyone froze. Aman, clutching his bamboo slips and hiding behind the earthen wall, whispered, “Is that the sound of the stones agreeing, or are they angry?”

Nüwa looked at the shifting lights in the furnace. “You heard three separate commands as one.”

She told everyone to ease their pressure and re-identify each pestel. To avoid confusion, the left side would only call out colors, and the right side would only call out advances or retreats. Aman volunteered to repeat the commands, but he accidentally shouted “Aman advances half an inch” instead of “Green advances half an inch.” The people holding the pestles bit back their laughter and asked, “Well, why don’t you advance then?” Aman looked at the scorching furnace yard and immediately stepped back behind the safety line. “The repeater does not participate in the displacement.” Nüwa didn’t have time for his sass; she just raised her hand to signal the start of the next round.

This time, the red stone retreated outward, the green stone rotated to where the fire flow passed, and the black stone sank to the bottom to support the other four. A thin gap was left between the white and yellow stones, allowing the circulating fire to pass through. Nüwa checked each of the five colors to ensure their light was stable before raising the furnace temperature again. The villagers held the pestles, their arms going numb from the vibration, but no one pushed an extra inch.

Then, another surge of water came from the protective trench. Aman saw it and quickly warned the wood-carriers to lift the last two bundles of dry firewood. He used an empty clay jar to catch the water dripping through the cracks in the earthen wall. The jar filled up fast. He ran out to dump it, slipped, and spilled all the water right back where it came from. He paused, looking at the empty jar, and explained, “The route was correct; the execution was just a little wet.” Someone nearby laughed, took the jar, and helped him bail out the seeping water.

Nüwa wasn’t distracted by the laughter. She waited until the five-colored stones were back in their proper places and confirmed the furnace base wasn’t tilting again before removing the pestles. This mishap became the method for controlling the furnace fire later on: reduce the fire first, separate the positions, then fuse the colors one by one. The divine stones weren’t thrown away to start over, and the mistakes people had just made weren’t in vain.

In the night, the red stone softened first, looking like a clump of frozen sunset clouds. The green stone followed, seeping with a watery glow, while the white, black, and yellow stones kept their original shapes. Nüwa didn’t rush to increase the heat. If she only chased intense, sudden heat, the colors that melted first would burn away, and the remaining stones still wouldn’t be able to connect.

She used her own divine power to protect the red and green stones, continuing to guide the heavenly fire to circulate evenly. As time passed, the edges of the white stone began to brighten. The heavy pressure borne by the black stone stabilized the heart of the furnace, and the yellow stone slowly relaxed at the end.

People on the mountain saw the furnace’s glow and thought the sky was already mended, preparing to return from their shelters. Nüwa sent her voice far and wide, telling them to stay on the high ground. The sky wasn’t sealed yet, and the four corners of the world weren’t straightened. Any new surge of water could flood the lowlands again.

She kept watch by the furnace alone, night after night. When falling fire drew too close, she guided it into the furnace. When the floodwaters changed course again, she deepened the side trenches but didn’t let helpers risk getting close. All the others could do was send dry firewood and food and water from a safe distance. The one truly maintaining the divine fire and judging the changes in the five colors was always Nüwa.

Finally, all five colored stones entered the tempering state. The five lights no longer repelled each other; instead, they touched each other repeatedly at the edge of the furnace’s heart. Every time they were about to connect, the fierce wind from outside blew down through the cracks in the sky, scattering the lightest white light.

Nüwa looked up, found where the fierce wind was coming from, and simply opened a corner of the wind-sheltering stone wall. This let the wind pass through the furnace chamber instead of swirling inside. The wind stopped crashing around chaotically and instead helped the divine fire circulate around the five colors. The scattered white light gathered again, gradually connecting with the red, green, black, and yellow lights.

At dawn, the divine furnace hadn’t gone out, and the floodwaters had been diverted away from the base. Though exhausted, Nüwa still stood guard before the fire. The stone refining entered its final stage: all five colored stones had softened and glowed, needing only to truly merge their powers into a stone paste strong enough to be lifted to the sky.

She saw another cluster of fierce fire falling from the edge of the sky’s crack and reached out to guide it into the heart of the furnace. The five colored lights rose simultaneously, illuminating the tilted sky. The furnace fire finally stabilized, and the stone materials began to merge into a five-colored stone paste capable of mending heaven and earth.

After the five-colored stones melted in the furnace, they didn’t immediately blend into one.

The red light rose upward, the green light flowed along the furnace walls, the white light was pulled by the fierce wind, the black light sank to the bottom, and the yellow light protected the furnace base. They were each stable, but like five rivers each rushing in its own direction, they couldn’t become the complete material needed to seal the sky.

Nüwa first suppressed the white light, which was easiest to lose, then lifted the black light from the bottom, letting the light and heavy elements restrain each other. The red light was still too intense, so she guided the green light to circle around it, using its watery nature to contain the fire. The yellow light rose from the bottom of the furnace, connecting the four lights in the center.

The first connection lasted only a moment before another burst of fierce fire fell from the sky’s crack. The furnace heart was shocked, and the red light almost burst through the stone walls. Nüwa didn’t retreat, nor did she treat the fire as new material to experiment with. She used her divine power to seal the furnace mouth, letting the incoming fierce fire dissipate along the protective trench, then gathered the five colors that had just touched back together. Her fingers traced a thin line of light in the air, as if she were sewing the cracks in the sky with her fingertips. The flames, like mischievous children, jumped onto the furnace walls, giggling, but were gently pressed down by Nüwa’s palm, like a caught kitten. “Don’t run, don’t run!” Nüwa whispered, her voice sounding like wind chimes shaking. The firelight, pressed by her palm like a flattened cookie, slowly became soft. Then, she lifted her palm, and the red light was gently pushed away by her fingertips, like releasing a bird from a cage. The red light danced between her fingertips, like a child playing jump rope, making a *click-clack* sound. “Oh, stop jumping, stop jumping!” Nüwa laughed, her voice like a crisp bell. Her palm reached toward the furnace mouth again, like a small hand slipping into a big one. The five colored lights intertwined in her palm, like a rainbow appearing after rain.

This time, she didn't try to change the five colors all at once. First, she made the blue and red colors compatible, then added the white light, followed by the black light rising, and finally, the yellow light stabilized the whole thing. The order came from the real obstacles she had encountered earlier, rather than a pre-listed formula.

The five-colored lights finally merged into a rotating flow of light at the heart of the furnace. The colors could still be distinguished, but they no longer scattered in all directions. As the stone flow passed through, the fine cracks on the furnace wall were naturally sealed, indicating that it had acquired the power to fill gaps.

Nüwa took a huge stone that had been split by heavenly fire and covered the cracked surface with a small amount of stone pulp. The two halves of the stone merged in the light and did not separate again even when blown by the wind. She only did this once to confirm, and then devoted all her energy to the real heavenly crack.

The birds that had been unable to stay outside the furnace due to the heat waves began to fly over from high above. The stone pulp collected the scattered heavenly fire into the five-colored light, which no longer spewed outward. Nüwa confirmed that after the material ascended to the heavens, it could not only fill gaps but also seal the fire within the crack.

A cloud of five-colored light gradually formed above the furnace. Nüwa held it up with both hands, and the stone pulp rose from the furnace. It neither fell like ordinary molten stone nor was blown away by the strong winds, but instead remained intact with her divine power.

At this time, the heavenly crack was still extending eastward. Nüwa observed the place where the fire was most intense and the edge of the crack was shaking the fastest, deciding to first repair the weakest edge on the northwest side. If she were to seal the center directly, the continuing expansion of the edges would reopen the sealed area; only by stopping the crack's expansion could she possibly close it from the center.

She divided the remaining stone pulp into several clusters, each connected to the others by light. This way, when they ascended to the heavens, even if the wind blew away the outer material, the five-colored stone would not be lost entirely.

The furnace fire slowly subsided, not because the floodwaters extinguished it, but because the five-colored stone had all left the furnace. Nüwa looked back at the high ground, confirming that the carriers were all in a safe place, before lifting the light cluster to ascend to the sky.

The refining of the stone was now complete. The red, blue, white, black, and yellow colors were no longer just five stones from different dangerous places, but had become the heavenly mending stone pulp that could fill the heavenly crack and bear the heavenly fire. Nüwa did not stop to celebrate, because if the material could not truly be delivered to the crack, all her previous efforts would be in vain.

The strong winds pressed down from above, and the five-colored light cloud in her palm shook violently. Nüwa stabilized her body and continued to move along the northwest edge of the crack she had surveyed. The last furnace light on the ground grew distant, and the real action of mending the heavens began at this moment.

The closer she got to the heavenly crack, the more the wind was like an invisible flood.

Nüwa held the five-colored stone pulp and moved against the wind. The falling fire flew out from the depths of the crack, and she could not guide it away one by one as she had on the ground. Instead, she had to prop up a protective light outside the stone pulp, allowing the fire to pass by on both sides. Each time she withstood the impact, the five-colored light dimmed a little.

The northwest edge of the crack was still trembling. Nüwa first pressed the first cluster of stone pulp into the outermost fine crack, and the five-colored light spread along the broken line, like roots grasping loose soil. The continuously expanding crack finally stopped, but did not close.

She moved along the edge toward the inside. The second cluster filled the spot that the strong winds had repeatedly torn, and the third cluster sealed the largest gap where the fire was falling. Each time she mended a place, she had to hold the sky on both sides with her palms until the stone light was completely connected. If she simply threw the material up, the wind would blow it away before it could solidify.

There was a place where the edge of the crack seemed narrow, but behind it was a large area of the sky that was sinking downward. Nüwa did not neglect it because of its narrowness; instead, she first divided out a stream of black and yellow stone light to support the weight, and then used white light to seal the surface. This choice used up one less cluster of material and prevented the sky from reopening due to its weight.

The people on the ground saw that the sky had appeared with a strip of colorful light. The colorful light was not for decoration, but indicated the position where Nüwa was moving. Where she passed, the dark cracks became narrower, and the falling fire that threatened the earth also decreased.

When she reached the center, the greatest difficulty appeared. The sky on both sides was uneven due to the loss of support, and even if the stone pulp was filled in, a curved long crack remained in the middle. If Nüwa pressed down on one side, the other side would continue to sink; if she only added stone pulp, the limited material would leak out from the crack.

She suspended the remaining light clusters by her side and stood between the two pieces of the sky. With one hand, she lifted the sinking part, and with the other, she slowly pulled the higher part back. The weight of the heavens pressed down on her, and her figure sank downward several times, but she never let go.

The two sides finally reached a position where they could connect. Nüwa immediately sent the stone pulp into the long crack, and the five-colored light flowed from both ends toward the center. The red light blocked the fire, the blue light calmed the turbulent flow, the white light filled in the finest parts, the black light bore the heavy pressure, and the yellow light locked all the parts together.

When only a gap the width of a finger remained, the stone pulp was almost gone. The strong winds blew fiercely from this hole, trying to reopen the edge that had just been closed. Nüwa did not go to find new divine stones, nor did she let the people on the ground bear the burden for her.

She put all her remaining strength into the last crack, and the five-colored light in her palm was pushed into an extremely thin line. The light entered the gap, was first pushed back by the wind, and then, under Nüwa's continuous support, moved forward again. The stone light on both sides finally met, and the entire heavenly crack emitted a low, resonant sound.

The sky closed.

The wind sound suddenly weakened, and the last few clusters of fire in the high sky extinguished. The original darkness that had pierced the sky disappeared, leaving only the colorful marks condensed from the five-colored stone, reminding people that the sky had once been cracked.

Nüwa slowly returned to the high ground. The crowd saw that the fire was no longer falling and thought the disaster had ended, but she looked toward the sky, which was still tilted. Although the sky was mended, the broken heavenly pillar had not been restored, and the four extremes were still low-lying. The accumulated water on the earth did not immediately recede just because the sky had closed. She raised her hand and gently brushed away the remaining ash, as if whispering softly to the broken sky. Aman stood beside her, holding a bamboo slip, his brow furrowed, muttering to himself, "This is not just ordinary roof repair." He looked up at the sky and was suddenly startled by the tail feathers of a passing crow, which made him laugh. Someone in the crowd whispered, "Does the crow also want to help?" Aman shook his head, laughing, "It can only sing." Her gaze remained firm, as if she could see every inch of the crack in the sky. She gently lifted her hand, pointing to the distant mountains, indicating for the crowd to follow. Aman followed behind, recording every breath and every tremor, silently praying. Nüwa's steps were steady, her gaze as bright as the North Star, as if she could penetrate all darkness. Her figure stretched out in the evening sun, like the most tenacious guardian between heaven and earth. The mending of the sky was complete, but it had only solved the problem of the crack and the falling fire. If the four extremes were not re-supported, the newly mended sky might still sink under its own weight. Nüwa did not accept congratulations and, after a short rest, set off toward the East Sea to find a pillar that could support the heavens.

In the depths of the East Sea lived a giant turtle.

Its back could support the mountains above the sea, and when its four legs came down, the deep sea would stabilize. Nüwa came to the seaside, explained the situation of the broken heavenly pillar and the unbalanced four extremes, and the fact that the human world was still suffering from floods, and asked for its help.

The giant turtle was silent for a long time. If it left the deep sea to support the sky, the seabed would also lose its guardian; if it did nothing, the sky might sink again. Finally, it was willing to use its four legs as pillars to support the sky, leaving its back in the sea to stabilize the water.

Nüwa did not take this sacrifice lightly. She promised the giant turtle that after the four extremes were stabilized, she would guide the turbulent waters back, so that the sea would no longer suffer endless impacts due to the tilt of the land. The giant turtle nodded, leaving the choice to her to complete.

Nüwa cut off the turtle's legs and, with her divine power, sealed the wound, preventing the turbulent waves from disturbing the giant turtle. The entire process was without struggle or delight in suffering. When the four legs of the turtle left the sea surface, each brought a powerful force that could support mountains.

The first leg was sent to the northwest, where the heavenly pillar had originally fallen, and the sky was the lowest. Nüwa stood the turtle's leg firmly in the deep earth, and the sky rose with it, and the five-colored mendings were no longer squeezed.

The second leg was placed in the low-lying area of the southeast. The land had been tilted in this direction for a long time, and the floods had gathered into a vast ocean. After the pillar was stable, the water did not continue to rise, and the outlines of the distant mountains that had been submerged reappeared.

The remaining two roots supported the northeast and southwest. Wherever Nüwa went, she first checked the height of the sky and the direction of the water flow, then placed the turtle's feet in the area that was truly under stress, stabilizing the low-lying areas, the gathering water, the turbulent winds, and the shaking earth.

When she was about to place the last root, a sudden tremor came from the northwest. Nüwa immediately stopped and compared the positions of the four directions, discovering that the southeast anchor point was still slightly low. She returned to adjust it, making the four pillars share the weight of the sky together, rather than letting one place bear it alone.

The four extremes finally stabilized.

The sky no longer sank towards the northwest, and the earth no longer tilted towards the southeast. The river water still overflowed in the low-lying areas but began to have a direction to flow back. The strong winds in the high places gradually subsided, and the five-colored stones that Nüwa had previously used to repair the sky emitted a soft glow on the stabilized sky.

Nüwa did not leave just because the situation had become calm. She separately summoned the east wind and the west wind and let the cloud energy from the four directions pass by the anchor points, confirming that when any one side was under stress, the other three could work together to support it. The sky remained balanced despite the changes in the wind and clouds, and the turtle's feet were truly stable.

Nüwa returned to the East Sea to thank the giant turtle. The seawater had stopped surging wildly due to the balance of the four directions, and the giant turtle, although it had lost its four feet, still steadily supported the sea and the mountains. Nüwa set up a stone monument by the sea to record its contribution to the human world, so that later generations would know that the four extremes did not appear out of thin air.

The pillars that supported the sky and the earth were reestablished, but the aftermath of the disaster was not over yet. Most of the fierce beasts that had escaped earlier had returned to the mountains, except for a black dragon that had taken advantage of the flood to expand its territory and was still destroying the dikes in the low-lying areas. Many villages were also surrounded by accumulated water and could not return.

Nüwa looked towards the water surface, where the black clouds were rolling. Setting up the four extremes allowed the water to stop rising indefinitely, but it would not automatically drive away the troublemaker, nor could it clear the river mouth that was blocked by mud and sand.

She left the East Sea and followed the direction of the receding water to the lowest place, then turned to eliminate the last troublemaker in the flood, making the sky and the earth suitable for living creatures again.

The black dragon was coiled in the old river mouth, pushing the receding floodwater back towards the villages.

When Nüwa arrived, the water surface was churning with turbulent waves, and the newly exposed fields were once again flooded. The black dragon used the water's momentum to collide with the temporary dikes, trying to occupy the low-lying areas before the four extremes were stable.

Nüwa did not engage in a struggle with it above the villages. She first led the people to higher ground, then used giant stones to block the side flows that led to the houses, and afterwards walked to the center of the main river, leading the black dragon away from the residential area.

The black dragon was not willing to follow the main river. It swung its tail and smashed the giant stones at the side flow, and the water wall immediately turned towards the villages. Nüwa did not turn back to chase the water; instead, she clapped her hands towards the riverbed, raising a slanting ridge of earth. The water wall collided with the ridge and was forced to split into two streams, passing by the houses on both sides. The people on the high ground saw their rooftops appear and disappear in the waves, and no one could laugh; they just followed Nüwa's previous arrangement and continued to send the elderly and children to even higher places.

Nüwa took advantage of the black dragon's momentary distraction to grab its tail and pull it towards a distant deep pool. The black dragon coiled its body, using the water to roll Nüwa to the center. The whirlpool became tighter and tighter, and the turbulent water made it impossible to see the direction. Nüwa deliberately sank to the riverbed, stepping on the rock layer, and did not forcibly struggle with the rotating water; when the black dragon swung its tail again to gain strength, she released her grip and changed her position, re-grasping the dragon's horn from the outside of the whirlpool.

The black dragon spat out a surge of turbulent water. Nüwa dodged to the side, and the turbulent water hit the empty stone beach behind her, carving out a deep groove. She immediately changed her position, leading the next attack towards the same groove. The black dragon continued to strike, thinking she was retreating, but it did not realize that it was moving further and further away from the village entrance, and the floodwater under its feet was also flowing back into the old river through the deepened groove.

Aman stayed on the high ground and did not run to the battlefield to add to the chaos. He recorded the positions of Nüwa and the black dragon while also helping the evacuees find which slope paths were not yet cut off by the water. A child saw the dragon's tail flip up in the distance and anxiously asked, "Will Nüwa be rolled around by it?" Aman stared at the water and replied, "I think the one that will get dizzy first is the dragon. It has turned so many times and still hasn't discovered that it has dug its own riverbed." This lightly spoken judgment made the child feel relieved, but he did not take the struggle itself as a joke.

The black dragon finally realized that the water level was dropping and suddenly turned towards the village. Nüwa had already blocked its way, standing between the dragon and the side flow. She grasped the dragon's horn with one hand and wrapped the remaining five-colored divine light around the dragon's body with the other. The black dragon rolled over in the waves, and the five-colored light tightened around its scales; when it tried to dive into the muddy water, Nüwa fixed the light chain on a giant rock in the river, not allowing it to get close to the people again.

When the last surge of turbulent water fell, the black dragon was pressed down in the deep pool, far away from the houses. Nüwa did not relax because she had the upper hand; she first confirmed that the side flow had been blocked by the earth ridge and that the evacuees were all on high ground before sealing the five-colored light lock. The black dragon could no longer raise a water wall, and the deep groove that it had opened itself continued to divert the accumulated water. Subduing the troublemaker and giving the flood a way out were completed in the same action, and the village did not become the cost of the clash between the two powerful beings.

The black dragon charged forward, raising a water wall that obscured both banks. Nüwa faced the water and grasped its horn, pressing it back into the river. The black dragon rolled over in the waves several times, each time being intercepted by Nüwa. Finally, Nüwa used the remaining five-colored divine light to lock down the dragon's body, completely subduing the black dragon and making it unable to stir up waves to harm people.

The main culprit had been eliminated, but the accumulated water in the low-lying areas still needed a way out. When the heavenly pillar broke, a large amount of mud and sand blocked the river mouth, and the water stayed in the fields. Nüwa gathered the villagers to cut down the withered reeds, burned them into reed ash, and filled the most easily breached gaps.

The reed ash solidified the mud and sand when it encountered water, first stabilizing the foundation of the dikes. Nüwa then removed the giant stones that blocked the main river, clearing the old path that led to the great river. The accumulated water flowed from the shallow ditches into the riverbed and finally stopped spreading towards the houses.

Aman came down from the high ground, carrying the green bamboo slips, and his shoes sank into the soft mud left by the receding water. He pulled out one foot, and the other sank in, so he had to stand in place and ask, "The sky and the earth have stabilized, why is this mud still not willing to behave?"

Nüwa, who was examining the river mouth, turned back and said, "Because repairing the sky does not include telling you to look before you step."

The people nearby could not help but laugh. Aman did not ask anyone to carry him; he walked to the hard ground, supporting himself with a reed pole, wiped off the mud, and continued writing. He only left a bit of lightheartedness when the disaster had receded and did not make jokes about the dead, the sacrifices, or the black dragon.

Over the next few days, Nüwa verified the results in every place. The five-colored patches in the sky remained intact and no longer dropped fiery rocks; the four extremes stood firmly, and the order of morning and evening and the wind direction were restored; the floodwater receded along the old river, and the black dragon was subdued in a place far away from human habitation; the fierce beasts that had been startled also left the village edges, following the scent of the mountains and forests.

People returned to their original homes. Some repaired their stoves, some cleared their fields, and some first preserved the emergency grain stores on the high ground, in case the river water was not yet completely calm. Nüwa did not wave her hand to restore the losses to their original state; instead, she let the stabilized sky and earth give people the possibility of working and living again.

Aman wrote on the green bamboo slips: the mountain of Not-Nonexistence tilted, the sky cracked, and water and fire erupted together; Nüwa found the five-colored stones, protected the furnace, and refined the stones to repair the sky; she also used the turtle's feet to set up the four extremes, subdued the black dragon, and used reed ash to stop the water. The bamboo slips were written up to this point, and in the distance, the sounds of birds and cooking smoke had already resumed, corresponding to each of the disasters he had recorded.

When he wrote the words "five-colored," he thought of his previous worry that the colors might be in the wrong order. He looked down and saw the colorful marks on the horizon, finally acknowledging that Nüwa was right: first, one had to repair the sky, and whether it looked good or not was something to discuss later.

The sunset fell beside the patched sky, and the light shone on the land that had been receded by the water. Nüwa stood on the high ground, confirming that the last of the water had returned to the river, before she finally stopped.

The restoration of the stability of the sky and the earth did not mean that the disaster had never occurred. The tilted mountain, the changed river, and the five-colored marks in the sky still preserved the cost. However, the fierce fires had been extinguished, the floodwater had receded, and people had relit their cooking smoke. When Aman closed the green bamboo slips, the complete story of Nüwa's repair of the sky finally had a complete ending.
