On the day of division, the older brother kept the better land, houses, and farm tools for himself, leaving only a patch of barren land at the foot of the mountain, two leaky thatched huts, and an old cow to the Cowherd.

The sister-in-law pointed at the old cow, saying, "It's old, but it eats a lot. If you can't take care of it, it's best to find another solution soon."

The Cowherd led the old cow by the halter, not contesting the few acres of good land. He had lost his parents in his childhood, and although his brother's family had given him a roof over his head, they often left the heaviest tasks for him. Now that he had set up his own household, even if he received less, he at least had a door he could open and close by himself.

The door of the thatched hut creaked open as soon as he pushed it. The Cowherd fetched some wood to prop up the door, then climbed onto the roof to patch the thatch. After fixing one side, the other side leaked; after pressing down on the other side, a clump of old thatch fell off and landed in his collar. He stood on the roof, sighing, "This hut is quite fair; it won't let me rest on any side."

The old cow in the yard let out a lowing sound, as if in agreement.

The barren land was even harder to work with. The soil was hard, and the old plow had only half a iron mouth left. The Cowherd worked as a laborer during the day to earn a piece of old iron, and at night, he used the fire to repair the plow. The first time he hitched the old cow to the plow, the plowhead just sank into the ground and got stuck on a rock. The Cowherd used all his strength, but the old cow looked back at him with an expression as if to ask who was pulling who.

"Don't worry," the Cowherd said, patting the old cow, "We may not have much, but at least we have this plow, which is the most decent thing we own. We can't expect it to work perfectly on the first day."

He didn't scold the old cow; instead, he dug up the soil to find the rock, then divided the land into smaller plots and worked them one by one. The old cow seemed to know where the soft soil was, and it would nudge him away just in time. After a few days, they managed to avoid the rocks and uncovered a deeper, wetter soil layer.

The Cowherd followed the wet soil and found a spring. He used stones to create a shallow ditch and led the water into the field. The barren land was finally suitable for planting beans and millet. On the day of planting, he divided the seeds into two portions, one to plant in the field and the other to save for future use, in case a storm came and ruined the next year's crop.

Aman passed by the foot of the mountain and saw the Cowherd and the old cow working together, preparing the land. He asked, "Who's in charge here?"

The Cowherd looked at the old cow and said, "Big decisions are made together, small ones are left to it. As for what's big or small, it decides that too."

Aman wrote, "One person, one cow, relying on each other. The Cowherd is in charge of speaking, the old cow is in charge of making him sound reasonable." The old cow swished its tail, almost hitting Aman's old pen. Aman stepped back and added, "The old cow is also in charge of correcting him."

The harvest was not bountiful, but it was enough for the Cowherd to save some seeds and get through the winter. He gave the old cow the driest corner of the hut and slept in the other side, which leaked less. When it rained at night, he would check on the cowshed first, then fetch a bucket to collect the water dripping from the hut. The bucket had three holes, and the raindrops fell in a rhythmic pattern, as if the broken hut had its own imperfect orchestra.

The Cowherd smiled and said, "Let's enjoy the music this year; next year, I'll fix the roof for you."

Just then, the old cow suddenly spoke up, "Don't promise too much. Your roofing skills are no better than this rain."

The Cowherd's bucket almost fell from his hands. He looked at the old cow, which had been by his side for so long, and asked after a moment, "You can talk?"

"Yes."

"Why did you start talking today?"

"You used to talk to yourself; that was entertaining enough. I couldn't join in."

The Cowherd was both surprised and amused, and eventually sat down beside the old cow. Whether this old cow had a good or bad past, it had never complained about the broken hut or the simple food. From then on, the Cowherd and the old cow not only worked together but also truly discussed their daily lives. They turned the barren land into a green patch, bit by bit, and gradually fixed up the leaky hut into a home.

As summer drew to a close, the old cow called the Cowherd to the East Mountain Lake.

"Tonight, a celestial weaver goddess has descended to the mortal world to rest," it said. "One of them is called the Weaver Girl. Go take a look."

The Cowherd was patching the thatch, and after hearing this, he didn't move for a long time: "What's there to see?"

"See if you can talk to someone other than me."

"What can I offer a goddess from the sky?"

The old cow lowered its head to chew on some grass: "Prepare a sentence that says 'hello.' Don't ask her if she can fix the roof for you right off the bat."

The Cowherd followed the old cow's advice and went to the lake's edge, but he didn't get close to the water. He waited under a tree, watching as several women walked towards the lake, laughing and chatting. Soon, a strong gust of wind blew a red cloud dress off the shore, over the reeds, and onto a nearby tree branch.

The old cow had mentioned that one of the women wore a red dress. The Cowherd took down the cloud dress without hiding it. He placed the dress on a clean rock, then stepped back, calling out loudly, "There's a red dress blown over here; I've put it on the rock. Wait until I'm far away, then come and get it."

The lake's edge fell silent for a moment, and someone asked, "Who are you?"

"The Cowherd who cultivates the land at the foot of the mountain."

"Why didn't you bring the dress over yourself?"

The Cowherd looked to the side: "If I went over, you'd probably run faster than the dress."

The goddesses laughed. They retrieved the cloud dress and, as the heavenly gate was about to close, they boarded the clouds and left one by one. The Weaver Girl in the red dress, however, didn't leave immediately. She adjusted her sleeves, came out from behind the reeds, and asked the Cowherd why he was there.

The Cowherd didn't want to pretend that the old cow's words were his own, so he told the truth: it was a talking old cow that had sent him. He added that he had thought he would at least prepare a teapot, but there wasn't even a stove at the lake's edge.

The Weaver Girl looked at the empty water bottle in his hand: "So you didn't bring tea, and you didn't bring a cup?"

"I brought good intentions."

"Can good intentions quench thirst?"

The Cowherd thought for a moment and handed over the water bottle: "If not, this can."

The Weaver Girl smiled and took the bottle. She spent her days weaving cloud brocades in the sky, seeing only orderly palaces and regular comings and goings of the gods. Few people considered whether the goddesses were convenient or not, and even fewer people admitted their own preparations were insufficient and spoke so candidly.

Aman was recording the sunset on the mountain path, saw the Cowherd talking to the Weaver Girl from a distance, and mistakenly thought the Cowherd was borrowing colors from the clouds. Only when he got closer did he realize the clouds could answer back, and he quickly opened his bamboo slips.

The Weaver Girl asked, "What are you writing today?"

Aman replied, "Originally, I was recording the sunset, but now I need to add a line: after the sunset, the Cowherd discussed water bottles with the farmer."

The Cowherd reminded him not to write people as scenery. Aman corrected himself immediately, only recording that the Weaver Girl had chosen to stay by the lake and talk, and the Cowherd had returned the cloud dress. He asked for her name and then left, leaving the choice to them.

The Cowherd gently stroked the old cow's mane, whispering, "Don't think of me as your shadow; remember, I'm still a person." The old cow narrowed its eyes and shook its head, as if to say, "Humans have emotions, cows have cows." Aman smiled and lightly tapped his pen on the bamboo slips, recording this gentle reminder.

The Weaver Girl stood by the lake, holding a small grass hat, with her hair blown by the wind. She looked up at the horizon, whispering, "If I could wait for the next time the clouds open, I'd like to see the old cow's barren land." The Cowherd heard this and furrowed his brow, but didn't say much, only handed her a tattered cloud dress with a few strands of starlight still on the sleeves.

"Don't worry, I won't leave," the Cowherd said, his voice tinged with unease. The Weaver Girl looked up, with a hint of hesitation in her eyes, but then nodded. She put on the cloud dress, feeling as if she was wrapped in a soft, misty veil.

"The hut at the foot of the mountain is old and leaky, and the food is simple," the Cowherd added, his eyes filled with concern. The Weaver Girl smiled, her mouth slightly upturned, as if she had heard a strange joke: "If you call the food 'starlight food,' I won't dare eat it." She shook her head, her eyes sparkling with curiosity.

Aman stood by, silently recording, his pen tip dancing on the bamboo slips, occasionally pausing, as if capturing every detail. He didn't intervene or offer any suggestions, only recording this gentle conversation in its entirety.

When the Cowherd handed the Weaver Girl the cloud dress, the old cow let out a soft lowing sound, as if applauding their meeting. The Weaver Girl looked up at the old cow, whispering, "Thank you." The old cow responded with a clear, crisp sound by tapping its tail on the ground.

The Cowherd and the Weaver Girl stood by the lake, their eyes meeting, with the air filled with the scent of grass and the lingering warmth of starlight. Aman looked up, gazing at the distant Milky Way, silently praying: May they be able to meet again on the seventh night, no matter what the future holds.

The Weaver Girl missed the evening sky gate, but she didn’t look flustered. She said she could wait for the next cloud path to open, and first wanted to see the barren field the Old Cow talked about. The Cowherd worried she’d only come along because she had nowhere else to go, so he told her plainly that the house at the foot of the mountain was rickety, the food was simple, and she could leave at any time.

“What if I still want to go after I know?” the Weaver Girl asked.

“Then I’ll go back first and hide the leakiest pot.”

“Why?”

“So you won’t think my house is a puddle when you step inside.”

The two walked side‑by‑side down the hill, keeping a respectful distance so neither would feel awkward. The Old Cow waited by the field; when the Cowherd finished his sentence and even brought along someone willing to learn more about him, the Cow gave a satisfied moo. The Weaver Girl thanked him and explained that she stayed not because the cloud robe bound her, but because she had seen the Cowherd’s honesty and courtesy and wanted to meet him again in the mortal world.

When the Weaver Girl visited the Cowherd’s home for the first time, she indeed first saw three wooden pots on the floor.

The Cowherd had stacked them in a corner, but that night when it rained, the roof dripped three streams of water right onto them. The Weaver Girl heard the sound, turned to look at him, and said, “You’ve hidden them well; the rain didn’t agree.”

The Old Cow, lying by the door, said, “I warned you before; his roof‑repair skills are limited.”

The Cowherd had no choice but to climb the ladder that night. The Weaver Girl steadied him, then noticed the grass weaving was wrong, so she turned the interlacing technique from the cloud brocade into the grass curtain. One of them fed the grass, the other tied it; soon the rain ran down the slope and no longer dripped inside.

The next day the Cowherd taught the Weaver Girl how to tell a bean sprout from a weed. She pointed at the most vigorous green leaf and asked if it should all be kept; the Cowherd said that was the grass that takes the nutrients fastest.

“The more patterns in the sky, the prettier,” the Weaver Girl said, “but the more lively the earth, the more headaches it brings.”

“So I see your loom hurting, and you see my field hurting; it’s perfectly fair.”

The Weaver Girl quickly learned to hoe, but she made every row too neat, even straightening seedlings that the wind had tipped. The Cowherd told her that crops aren’t silk; you don’t need every plant to stand in the same pose. She asked him why he always handed the shuttle the wrong way when weaving. They each exposed the other’s quirks, and neither could claim to be clever.

After a few visits, the Weaver Girl no longer stayed only one night. She taught the Cowherd how to distinguish different threads and helped the villagers weave sturdy cloth; he showed her the seasons, the soil, and village life. One sudden rain came, the drying field hadn’t yet been covered, and both rushed out. The Weaver Girl used a cloth curtain to shield the rain, the Cowherd pushed the cart of grain, and the Old Cow hauled the last bundle back to the shed, saving the entire season’s harvest.

Aman was hiding from the rain and saw it just as the storm cleared, then unfolded a green bamboo slip. He said, “When a celestial maiden falls to the mortal world, the first big thing she does is rush to grab the grain.”

The Weaver Girl wrung out her sleeves. “If we don’t harvest, what will the Cowherd eat in winter?”

The Cowherd answered, “What he knows about farming now is already more than what he knows about weaving.”

Aman asked how many weaving patterns he knew. The Cowherd stretched out a finger, then felt one was too many, and quietly pulled his hand back. The Weaver Girl laughed and held the grain basket. Aman only recorded that the three of them worked together to protect the food, and the Weaver Girl, like them, rolled up her sleeves and grabbed the harvest by hand.

Over those days, feelings slowly clarified. The Cowherd respected the Weaver Girl’s choice but worried about his poverty and feared she might be punished by the heavens for staying. He didn’t want to bind her with a single lakeside meeting, so he asked if she was simply craving the freshness of the mortal world.

The Weaver Girl didn’t answer right away. She showed the Cowherd a strip of cloth she had woven: the first few inches were fine and ornate, but later it turned into a durable plain pattern.

“The cloud brocade is pretty, but it always repeats the old pattern.” She said, “In the mortal world, I learned for the first time that a piece of cloth must shield someone from the wind, and that a bag of grain is worth grabbing in the rain. I stayed not because of novelty, but because every stitch here has purpose, and because I want to share these hard days with you.”

The Cowherd confessed that he had grown used to loss; the more he cherished, the more he feared speaking up. All he could offer was a barren field, a grass house, and a promise that would never be forced. The Weaver Girl said those were more precious than orders in a glittering palace.

The two confirmed their love under the repaired eaves. The Old Cow glanced up and said, “Finally clear.” “If I wait a few more seasons, the grass will taste a second flavor.”

After a laugh, the Weaver Girl still seriously explained that a celestial‑mortal union could invite pursuit. The Cowherd didn’t say that love would erase all risk; he only promised to share the burden. The Weaver Girl didn’t think the mortal world was a forever sunny paradise. Because she had seen broken houses, barren fields, and violent rain, her choice was not a spur‑of‑the‑moment impulse.

After that she no longer appeared as a casual visitor. She moved the loom to the window, the Cowherd opened a new field behind the house. Each kept their skills and began to enter the other’s life, ready to turn love into a lasting responsibility.

The Cowherd and the Weaver Girl were married in front of their neighbors.

The wedding was simple. The Cowherd borrowed a few long benches, the Weaver Girl decorated the courtyard door with plain cloth she had woven, and the Old Cow brought a basket of freshly harvested beans. Aman recorded the guests’ names on a green bamboo slip; halfway through he noticed the Old Cow standing in the line and asked whether it counted as a relative of the groom or a family elder.

The Old Cow said, “Both sides count. Without me, he wouldn’t even know which way the lake goes.”

The Cowherd reminded him, “Today, let’s keep this quiet.”

Aman solemnly wrote “Old Cow First” and then whispered that it was the first time he’d seen a cow sit in the same seat as the matchmaker and the elders. Everyone laughed; the Weaver Girl personally gave the Old Cow a pot of good grass. The Old Cow gurgled as it ate, then lifted its head and stared at the Weaver Girl with rough eyes, as if saying, “This girl isn’t just a weaver; she’s my new master.” The Weaver Girl laughed, handed the grass pot to it, and softly said, “You eat, and I’ll give you more.” The Old Cow took a bite, let out a satisfied grunt, as if confirming the sincerity of the gift. Then the Cowherd returned from the field with a basket of fresh straw, his face muddy but his grin childlike. The Old Cow followed, occasionally tying a rope of straw into a knot to remind him not to stack the straw too high or it might topple in the wind. After the Cowherd stacked the straw, he looked up to see the Weaver Girl standing at the door, holding a small hoe, ready to pull weeds from the field. She raised her hand to tell him to rest first, and she would go work. The Cowherd nodded, went back to the field, and began plowing. The Old Cow walked beside him, sometimes pulling a rope of straw in half, as if making a “rope tool” for the Cowherd. The Weaver Girl tied a rope of straw to her waist, walked and laughed, “Is this rope long enough?” The Old Cow gurgled, wagged its tail, as if saying, “Enough.” The Cowherd heard it, lifted his head, laughed, and said, “You old cow, you really know how to joke.” He piled the straw into a small mound; the Old Cow nudged it gently with its nose, and the mound wobbled like a tiny hill. The Weaver Girl watched, laughing like a child, but inside she thought, “This is my new life.”

After the marriage, the Cowherd farmed, the Weaver Girl wove, and they didn’t each stay on one side. When the Weaver Girl was busy weaving, the Cowherd cooked and wound thread; when he returned late from the fields, she would go to the edge of the field to bring water and point out the bean sprouts she had finally learned to recognize. She still looked at the furrows as if they were warp and weft, and after a few steps she would straighten a crooked seedling; the Cowherd, every time he moved between the loom, would make the thread look like a rope of grass.

“If you weave another foot, the cloth will be able to bundle the straw in the field by itself.” the Weaver Girl said.

“Durability is also a strength.”

“But I was going to make a handkerchief.”

After they had children, the house became even more chaotic. The daughter, once she could walk, loved to grab colored thread; the son, seeing the Cowherd pull the plow, took a wooden spoon and carved a groove on the ground. The Weaver Girl raised the thread basket, the Cowherd swapped the wooden spoon for a blunt small shovel; when they turned around, the two children swapped tools again, neither following the parents’ instructions.

The Old Cow became the most reliable caretaker. When the children were tired, they leaned on it; when they woke, they tied colored thread to its mane. At first it disliked the fuss, but later it found that each time it tied a thread it received a fresh stalk of grass as payment, so it no longer objected. One day the Cowherd came home and saw the Old Cow wearing three cloth flowers on its head and asked why it didn’t shake them off.

“Running a family isn’t easy,” the Old Cow said, “to earn a bundle of grass is a bundle.”

Life in the village had its own real hardships. When the harvest was poor, the Weaver Girl traded her fine cloth for seeds; when the child fell ill, the Cowherd would travel far to find a healer while she stayed by the bedside all night. Once the loom broke, and a gust of wind lifted a corner of the roof. Each time, they solved the problem through division of labor and neighborly help; no one could wave a hand and make the trouble vanish.

The Weaver Girl would sometimes look up at the night sky. She knew that one day the heavens would notice her absence, but she didn’t hide her worry from the Cowherd. They agreed that if the summons came, they would first protect their child and not let either of them shoulder the choice alone.

The Cowherd asked her if she regretted it.

The Weaver Girl was mending his cuff when the needle paused for a moment: “I regret that you tore the sleeve again. As for marrying you, I never did.”

“Can this garment be repaired?”

“It can. Your habit of bumping into branches while walking isn’t inevitable.”

Years passed, the barren field grew into a plot that could feed a family, and the thatched house got a sturdy roof. Neighbors no longer saw the Weaver Girl as a mysterious guest; they borrowed her shuttle, asked her to teach them how to weave, and even watched over the children when the harvest was busy.

Before Aman left the village, he wrote on a bamboo slip that the Cowherd and Weaver Girl had married, had two children, and lived a life of farming and weaving that complemented each other. The slip didn’t say “no more worries,” only the house they had built together, the grain they had reclaimed, and the children they had put to sleep.

One night, the four of them ate in the courtyard while the old ox lay in the shadow of the lamp. The children fought over daytime stories, the Cowherd ladled soup for the Weaver Girl, and she cut the last thread from his cuff. It was these ordinary moments that, when they were forced to part later, made them lose not just a word of love but a home that had been running for years.

The old ox began to go to the fields less often.

At first it was just slower steps, so the Cowherd let it rest in the shade. Later, even the tender grass it loved was left in the trough. The Weaver Girl squatted to check and saw that the ox’s years had truly ended.

The Cowherd wouldn’t admit it. He cut the grass finer and called in an elder who knew livestock ailments. After examining, the elder said the old ox wasn’t sick with any disease; it had simply been with them for too long, and its strength was naturally fading.

The children didn’t understand death. The daughter put the cloth flower she’d once tied on the old ox’s side, and the son brought a bundle of tender grass, asking if it would still be able to carry them to the field when it woke. The old ox lifted its head, touched the children’s hands, but did not answer.

Meanwhile, the celestial loom had gone empty for several years. The cloud brocade the Weaver Girl wove had a unique pattern that no one else could replicate. The missing brocade finally caught the Queen Mother’s attention; a celestial inspector followed the remaining cloud silk and discovered that she had lived among the Cowherd, even raising children.

The Weaver Girl sensed the search light in the clouds and knew her secrecy was over. She told the Cowherd that the Queen Mother prized heavenly rules and would never easily admit a fairy staying on earth. Even if she were willing to accept punishment, the other party might forcefully separate the family and children.

The Cowherd asked if they could all leave together. The Weaver Girl shook her head; the clouds would guide them, not hindered by earthly mountains and rivers. All she could do was return and explain her choice, hoping to spare the family from blame; the Cowherd didn’t want her to face it alone.

That night, the old ox suddenly called the Cowherd over.

“I was once the celestial golden ox, but I fell from the heavens for breaking a rule,” it said slowly, “so I know the Queen Mother’s nature. If she comes personally, you can’t catch up on earth.”

The Cowherd grasped the ox’s mane: “There must be another way.”

The old ox told him that after its death it could preserve its hide properly. It wasn’t ordinary leather; in an emergency it could be worn to harness the remaining divine power and lift toward the sky. But that power was enough only for one chase, not to make a mortal an eternal celestial.

“I don’t want to trade your life for a way out,” the Cowherd said.

“My days are finished, not yours to take,” the old ox looked toward the lit house, “I once helped you till the barren land, then helped you find the Weaver Girl. This last journey also lets me send you one more thing.”

The Weaver Girl came, bowed deeply to the old ox. She didn’t take the request for granted and asked if it had any other wish. The ox only asked that they not leave the children behind and that the Cowherd listen more to her when repairing the roof in the future.

That was its last word.

Before dawn, the old ox died quietly. The Cowherd’s family buried it where the first spring water was found in the barren field. The children put the cloth flower into the earth, and the Cowherd placed a wooden plaque beside the grave, noting that it was not a livestock taken from a split family but a companion who walked with him from loneliness to a household.

Following the last wish, the Cowherd kept the ox hide. Every time he folded a strip, he remembered the times the ox pulled the plow, protected the grain, and watched over the children. That hide was not just a tool to fly; it was a farewell that could not be wasted.

In the evening, a neat, cold golden cloud appeared. The Weaver Girl stood in the courtyard, recognizing it as a sign of heavenly arrival. She told the Cowherd to bring the children inside; she would don her simplest clothes and explain why she hadn’t returned for years.

The family had just lost the old ox, and a new separation loomed at the door. The Cowherd placed the ox hide in the most accessible box and held the Weaver Girl’s hand. They all knew that their calm life would be interrupted, yet they decided to honor their earlier promise: protect the children first, then face it together.

The golden cloud fell into the courtyard as the Queen Mother followed behind it.

The accompanying celestial attendants unfolded heavenly rules and ordered the Weaver Girl to return immediately. The Queen Mother did not enter the house to see the earthly cloth she had woven, nor did she ask how they had lived these years; she simply said that the celestial and mortal realms had boundaries, and that the Weaver Girl had abandoned her duty and stayed on earth, so she must return to the heavens.

The Weaver Girl stood at the courtyard gate, admitting she had left the celestial hall but not claiming that marrying the Cowherd was a mistake. She said she had not been deceived or forced; every descent and final stay was her own choice. If anyone wanted to hold her accountable, they should first hear all she had done on earth.

The Queen Mother replied, “You can weave cloud brocade, so you should stay in the heavens. How can a few acres of land and a few pieces of cloth counter your heavenly duty?”

“If cloth can keep people warm and a field can feed a family, it is no trivial matter.”

The Cowherd stepped out of the house and offered to go to the heavens with them. The Queen Mother, seeing he was only a mortal, forbade him to approach. The children heard the argument and ran to hug the Weaver Girl. The celestial attendants hesitated to pull, so the Weaver Girl used the moment to urge the children to listen to their father and to tell the Cowherd not to hurt himself in the conflict.

The Cowherd said, “We agreed to face it together, not for you to leave everyone.”

The Queen Mother stopped listening, waved her sleeve, and summoned the cloud wind. The celestial attendants separated the Weaver Girl and her family, lifting her toward the sky. The Weaver Girl kept looking back; the daughter reached out to call her mother, the son ran out of the courtyard, and the Cowherd held one of them, but the cloud wind pushed them so hard they could not move forward.

Before ascending, the Weaver Girl threw down a weaving shuttle. It was the wooden shuttle she had used for years on earth, dropped beside the Cowherd’s feet, reminding him that their shared life was not a dream that vanished upon waking.

The golden cloud rose higher and higher.

The Cowherd suddenly remembered the old ox’s last wish. He placed the children in two sturdy bamboo baskets, carried them on a pole, and draped the ox hide over his back. The daughter clung tightly to the basket’s edge, the son held the Weaver Girl’s shuttle. The Cowherd first checked the knots, ensuring the children were secure, then turned toward the ox’s grave and whispered, “One last favor.” He gently stroked the bamboo’s grain, as if speaking to the ox, praying silently. The ox’s gravestone bore the inscription “Never Forgotten.” The Cowherd whispered it, and his voice echoed in the valley. When he reached the grave, he placed the bamboo basket on the stone table, lifted his hand and tapped lightly, producing a crisp sound. The ox’s shadow seemed to smile, as if saying, “Don’t fear, I’m here.” He placed his hand on the basket, gently stroking, as if soothing a newborn child. The children’s eyes sparkled with curiosity, as if they were eager for this unfamiliar world. He looked up at the distance, silently chanting, “May you all be safe.” Then he lifted the basket, draped the ox hide over his back, and prepared to move on. The ox hide glowed with a warm, faint light.

The ox hide glowed with a warm, faint light.

The Cowherd’s feet left the ground, and the pole rose with him. He initially lost balance, then lowered his shoulders, keeping the two bamboo baskets steady. The children, though frightened, sat as he instructed. The father and children crossed the roof, chasing the golden cloud that had taken the Weaver Girl.

The Queen Mother heard the shout behind her, turned to see a mortal flying to the heavens, and immediately recognized the power left by the golden ox. She ordered the celestial attendants to speed up, but the Cowherd used the last divine power of the ox hide to keep closing the distance.

The Weaver Girl was still struggling in the clouds. Unable to free herself, she let a long ribbon fall behind her. The ribbon stretched across the sky, and the Cowherd caught its end, bringing the distance between them close enough to see each other's expressions.

“First, protect the children!” the Weaver Girl shouted.

“None of them will be left behind!” the Cowherd replied.

The leather of the ox’s hide was fading, yet the Cowherd kept stepping forward on the cloud mist. The old ox didn’t take back his wife for him; it only gave him one chance to chase. The one who truly lifted the children, braved the high‑altitude wind, and chose to keep going was the Cowherd himself.

As his hand was about to touch the Weaver Girl, the Queen Mother stopped the cloud carriage. She plucked a jade hairpin from her hair, looked coldly at the family that was still in pursuit, and the Weaver Girl recognized the magical weapon. Her face changed instantly, and she shouted for the Cowherd to stop.

But the Queen Mother’s hand was already raised. The earthly houses shrank into a point far below, leaving only a few steps of cloud path between the couple, and in the next instant those steps would be cut off by an impassable force.

The Queen Mother drew the jade hairpin into the void.

At first it was just a silver line, then countless stars burst from it, turning into a ceaseless river of the heavens. The sound of water cut across the sky, first separating the Cowherd’s outstretched hand, then pushing the Weaver Girl’s cloud carriage to the other side.

The Cowherd immediately stepped back; the children on the two ends of the bamboo basket trembled in the wind. He steadied the basket, and when he looked up, the Weaver Girl was already on the other side of the wide river. The distance that had once been a few steps away now required the sound to travel over the shimmering waves.

“Mom!” the daughter shouted from the basket.

The Weaver Girl rushed to the shore, only to be stopped by an immortal messenger. She begged the Queen Mother to let the children stay with her, but the Queen Mother refused, saying that the children were born on earth and should return with the Cowherd. The Weaver Girl asked when she could see them again, and the answer was still the same: first return to the weaving hall, and the matter would be discussed later.

The Cowherd ran along the riverbank, looking for a narrower stretch of water. The heavenly river stretched out with his footsteps; no matter which way he looked, it seemed endless. He set down the bamboo pole and tried to probe the water with the long ribbon the Weaver Girl had thrown, but the tip of the ribbon touched the river surface only to be sucked back by the star‑waves.

The Weaver Girl was also searching on the opposite shore. She wove a long rope from cloud silk, intending to throw it to the Cowherd, but before it crossed the river’s heart it dissolved into fine light. The couple could see each other, yet there was no boat and no bridge to step on.

The son asked his father whether the old ox’s leather could fly again. The Cowherd touched his back; his divine power had been exhausted in the chase. He didn’t coax the child with false hope, only said that the old ox had given all it could, and the rest of the journey would have to be waited for and found by them themselves.

The Weaver Girl heard from the other side and clutched the cloud silk that hadn’t crossed the river. She remembered the old ox’s dying wish that they never leave the children on either side. Now the family was indeed split across the two banks, but no one had willingly given up.

The Queen Mother ordered the Weaver Girl to leave. The Weaver Girl refused, saying that if heavenly law only allowed her to obey but not to choose her own life, then even if she returned to the loom, the cloud brocade she wove would only have broken threads.

The immortals in the heavens began to murmur. Some had once thought the Weaver Girl’s absence was just a missing celestial in the weaving hall; seeing the two children shouting by the river, they realized that what had been torn apart was not a duty but a family that had lived together for years.

The Queen Mother still did not remove the heavenly river. She only allowed the Weaver Girl to stay on the opposite shore long enough to say goodbye.

The Cowherd lifted the children out of the bamboo basket. The daughter raised the wooden shuttle left by her mother, and the Weaver Girl, across the river, told her how to thread it; the son recounted what had happened at the old ox’s grave, and the Weaver Girl heard the cloth flower burying itself in the earth, and for a long time she did not respond.

Finally, the Cowherd asked, “When will we have to wait?”

The Queen Mother said that mortals and immortals could not be together every day. Whether they could see each other again depended on whether heavenly law allowed it, and whether they were willing to accept the separation.

“The separation is not our choice,” the Weaver Girl said.

The Cowherd stood on the other side and replied, “But waiting can be.”

The two agreed that no matter how long, they would look at each other under the brightest star on the riverbank. The Weaver Girl was taken back to the weaving hall, and the Cowherd stayed with the children on this side of the heavenly river. The river the Queen Mother drew did not disappear; the silver waves have since crossed between them every night. The Weaver Girl stepped onto earthly dust; the grass blades under her feet were gently crushed, making a “clack clack” sound. The old ox laughed hoarsely beside her, as if saying, “Don’t crush the grass into fragments; let’s not crush our hearts either.” The Cowherd held the children in his arms, humming a country tune. The children looked up curiously and asked, “Dad, why do you always crush the grass into fragments?” The Cowherd laughed and said, “That’s my practice of harvesting on the pasture. Don’t worry, our grass will grow greener.” In the Queen Mother’s hand, the silver river was like a huge ribbon, slowly cutting across. The silver waves glittered in the night, like silk spread in the heavens. Her voice was low and solemn: “Cowherd, your wish has been heard, but the existence of the heavenly river is insurmountable.” The Weaver Girl looked up at the river, a flicker of reluctance in her eyes, yet she understood that this river was the separation of fate. She gently held the Cowherd’s hand and whispered, “I will wait for you, until the Qixi Festival.” The Cowherd nodded, his eyes reflecting the starry reflection on the river, and silently made a promise: once a year, under the stars, we will meet. The heavenly river became an unavoidable price: they could not stay together for long, but they did not forget each other; they could only keep the family’s memory alive in their separation.

That night, the Weaver Girl restarted the loom on the opposite shore, but left the first cloud silk very long; the Cowherd settled the children by the river and told them how his mother protected the grain in a downpour. The heavenly river became an unavoidable price: they could not stay together for long, but they did not forget each other; they could only keep the family’s memory alive in their separation.

The days of looking across the river passed for a long time.

Whenever the Cowherd brought the children to the shore, the Weaver Girl would stop the loom. She arranged the cloud silk into different hues and told the children that she was fine; the children raised the wooden shuttle or arranged freshly harvested grain on the ground. The heavenly river was too wide; their words were often drowned by the water’s sound, and they could only guess the meaning from gestures.

As the Qixi Festival approached, many magpies flew to the riverbank. They had heard the story of the Cowherd and the Weaver Girl in the mortal world and had seen the family watching each other every night, so one by one they flew onto the river. The first magpie spread its wings, the others landed beside it, and the flock stretched from both banks toward the river’s center.

The Cowherd initially thought they were just migrating, until the black‑and‑white birds’ backs formed a walkable path. The silver waves of the heavenly river flowed beneath their wings, and the magpies kept linking each other, never breaking apart.

The Weaver Girl ran from the opposite shore. The celestial messenger tried to stop her, but more and more immortals begged the Queen Mother: the heavenly river had already kept the couple apart for so long that if they were not even allowed to meet for a day, the remaining rule would be only cruelty.

The Queen Mother looked at the bridge of magpies formed by the flock and finally relented. She allowed the Cowherd and the Weaver Girl to meet on the Qixi Festival, but before dawn they must each return to one side, and thereafter they could only meet once a year on that day.

This was not the complete life the couple had hoped for, but it was the first real path that could bring them to each other after the heavenly river appeared.

The Cowherd lifted the two children onto the magpie bridge. He tried to keep each step light, afraid of crushing the birds that carried them. The daughter shouted for her mother all the way, and the son clutched the wooden shuttle tightly. The Weaver Girl walked from the other end; at first she could still see clearly, but later tears filled her eyes, leaving only a figure growing closer.

The whole family finally embraced in the middle of the bridge.

The Weaver Girl first checked whether the children had grown, then looked at the Cowherd’s shoulders, worn by the bamboo pole. The Cowherd returned the wooden shuttle to her, saying that over the years the children had learned to distinguish warp and weft, but he still kept swapping the shuttle.

The Weaver Girl laughed with tears: “We only see each other once a year, yet you still keep your old habits so well.”

That light laugh did not erase the separation; it only let the children confirm that their mother was still the one who teased her father in memory. Then they sat on the bridge and went through the year’s missed moments one by one: which field was bountiful, what pattern the daughter would weave, what flowers the son added to the old ox’s grave, and which part of the cloud brocade the Weaver Girl had woven for the color in front of their home.

Aman opened a green bamboo slip on the distant shore. He did not cross the bridge, nor did he urge them to speak in a way suitable for record‑keeping; he simply wrote that the magpies had built a bridge with their wings, and the Queen Mother had allowed the Cowherd and the Weaver Girl to meet once a year on the Qixi Festival.

As the night began to fade, the first magpie flapped its wings. The bridge creaked softly, a gentle reminder that the meeting was almost at its end. The child clung to the Weaver Girl, refusing to let go, but she didn’t want the flock to exhaust themselves, nor did she want the promise of next year to be taken back. She kissed each child’s forehead in turn, then arranged with the Cowherd that next year they would still cross the same bridge.

The Cowherd led his children toward one bank, while the Weaver Girl returned to the other. After the last magpie took flight, the magpie bridge dissolved back into a flock of birds, scattering into the brightening sky, and the Milky Way continued to flow across the heavens.

Aman closed the green bamboo slips. That night, even though the lovers met, the Milky Way did not vanish. The Cowherd and the Weaver Girl remained separated, meeting only for a brief moment each year on the seventh day of the seventh month; it was precisely because of the long wait that every ordinary conversation on the bridge weighed more than any grand vow.

Since then, whenever the seventh day of the seventh month arrives, people see magpies flying in swarms toward the night sky and know that they are once again building a bridge for the Cowherd and the Weaver Girl. Some listen to the wind beneath the grape trellis, as if they can hear the quiet whispers of a family reunited the next year. After dawn the bridge disperses, but the yearly promise remains intact; the Cowherd, the Weaver Girl, and their children will keep waiting for the next time the starry river clears the way.
