He’d seen frail old folks and watched hard‑earned treasures vanish in a single storm, so he decided that if he could master immortal arts, he could dodge all the uncertainties. The master asked, “What did you learn today?”

But when he thought about the two batches of materials he had ruined, the answer wasn't so simple. His fellow apprentices didn't know whether they would succeed in the end, but they still waited for the seasons to pass; he had abandoned his post and hurried the process, reducing all their efforts to zero, simply because he couldn't see the quick results.

Each time the cypress tree recovered, it was like being brought back to that moment again.

He remembered putting the bucket by the road while fetching water, thinking a quick glance at the star sigil was harmless; leaving the stove for a moment, believing he could fix it on his return; and finally trying a shortcut, convinced that success would prove the rules unnecessary. Each choice wasn’t born of malice, but of treating his own urgency as more important than his promise.

Wu Gang stopped hiding behind “I just want to learn more.” Seeking knowledge itself isn’t wrong; the mistake was promising something and then refusing to give it the time it needed, and after failing, immediately betting on another shortcut.

That realization didn’t stop the camphor tree from healing. When the next axe fell, the scar still softened in the moonlight. The immortal master didn’t grant forgiveness simply because Wu Gang had an epiphany in his heart.

If reflection were only a few nice words under the tree, it would be no different from his apology after the stove broke. Wu Gang decided to let understanding turn into action.

He no longer blamed a dull axe when he was tired, nor did he search for a weakness that didn’t exist on the tree. Before each swing he checked his tools, focused on the spot where the axe would land, and after stopping he admitted the real outcome of that round. Even if the tree healed, he didn’t deny what he’d just done, and he didn’t brag that the next swing would be different.

Once, a new spell’s light came from beyond the moon. The old Wu Gang would have stopped the axe to stare, even if only for a glance. He looked up at the glow, paused his motion, and then returned to the camphor tree.

No one was watching this time, and no one praised him. He simply remembered what he was carrying on his shoulders.

The camphor tree still fell and healed with each chop, and the external task remained almost unsolvable. But Wu Gang’s inner cycle began to shift: from chasing novelty, short bursts of force, and blaming failure, to choosing one task, steady commitment, and facing the result honestly.

He also understood why punishment was set in the Moon Palace. There was no side task to disguise his distraction as diligent study, no fellow cultivator to repair the stove for him, and no clamor of years to mask repetition. Each time the axe sounded, it asked the same question: when there’s no shortcut, would you still stay?

Wu Gang’s answer became to keep swinging the axe.

That didn’t mean he liked eternal punishment, nor that his longing to return had vanished. He still hoped one day to fell the entire camphor tree, and he still wanted to face his fellow cultivators and admit his loss. Reflection was not abandoning a goal, but no longer forcing the goal to appear instantly through wrong means.

The camphor leaves trembled softly in the axe’s echo. The tree body recovered, and Wu Gang lifted the axe again. The punishment didn’t lift, and the Moon Palace didn’t suddenly open an exit; what truly finished was his transformation—he finally admitted his mistake of not being focused on immortality, and began practicing the concentration he had once lacked.

Later, the sound of felling the camphor in the moon grew steady.

Wu Gang still faced the same camphor tree and the same axe every day. The tree still fell and healed with each chop, and his reflection didn’t reduce the number of recoveries. The condition that the tree must fall to end the punishment never changed.

Aman stepped once more into the stone corridor of the Moon Palace, his footsteps lightly striking the silent green stone slabs, echoing through the empty hall. Before him the camphor tree still stood, its trunk smooth as new, with almost no axe marks on its surface, as if it had just been soothed by moonlight. Aman squinted, first thinking Wu Gang had not touched the tree these days, muttering to himself, “How obedient is this tree?”

As he lowered his head to inspect, a muffled chopping sound came from afar, a dull “dong—dong—” echoing in the corridor like an old bell. Aman turned in rhythm, seeing Wu Gang holding the axe handle, brow furrowed, the blade flashing cold light in the moon. His movements were still hurried, the axe falling on the trunk again and again, splinters drifting in the air, then strange mist spreading quickly, the bark as if wrapped in soft silk, healing itself instantly, the original gouge restored.

Sweat beads trickled from Wu Gang’s forehead; he wiped them away, a hint of frustration in his eyes. He raised the axe again, the blade cutting a silver arc in the air, striking the same spot on the trunk. As the splinter hit the ground, the bark, like petals soothed by spring wind, closed gently, emitting a soft “sizzle.” Aman stood beside him, holding a green bamboo slip, his pen scratching dense characters, but he unconsciously wrote “cut” as “heal,” laughing, “Aman, my hand is so ‘heal’—the tree won’t even fall!”

He looked up at Wu Gang, who now lowered the axe, his arm trembling slightly, muttering softly, “How stubborn this tree is, cut and it heals, heals and it cuts…” The sound sounded especially lonely in the empty Moon Palace. Aman stepped closer, lightly patting Wu Gang’s shoulder, his voice low but teasing, “Senior, your axe seems more ‘persistent’ than my writing; every time it falls, the tree ‘heals’.”

Wu Gang lifts his eyes, a bitter smile flickering across his face before he sinks back into his stubborn focus. He raises his axe again, the blade slicing a thin line of light across the moonlit sky as it strikes the opposite side of the tree trunk. Splinters fall like snowflakes, the bark snaps back together instantly, as if it had never been wounded. On Aman’s green bamboo slip, new characters are densely scrawled: “Seventh cut, the tree still heals.” He looks toward the distant stars and murmurs softly, “The nights in the Moon Palace are always so long.”

The Moon Palace feels even colder and heavier now; the shadows of the stone pillars stretch longer in the axe’s glow, like silent judges. Wu Gang’s breathing quickens, and the axe in his hand is no longer just a tool but a heavy burden. Aman puts down his pen, gently closes the bamboo slip, and whispers, “If I only watched the osmanthus tree, I’d probably write that you’re making no progress.” His words echo in the silence, like a mirror reflecting the regret and loneliness that still burn in Wu Gang’s eyes.

Wu Gang stands beside the tree, gazing at the unyielding osmanthus, his impatience turning into contemplation. His hand trembles slightly, but he no longer swings wildly as before; instead he slowly lowers the axe and closes his eyes.

“If I only watched the osmanthus tree, I’d probably write that you’re making no progress,” Aman says.

Wu Gang sets the axe down and exhales, “It really hasn’t lost a single branch.”

“What about you?”

“At least I won’t throw this axe on the ground just to glance elsewhere.”

He writes that line on the bamboo slip, but he does not ask the immortal master to intervene on Wu Gang’s behalf. The small chronicler can witness the change, but he cannot alter the punishment or declare that repentance is sufficient.

Wu Gang lifts the axe again. He swings, pulls back, and the tree’s scar closes. The motion looks the same as the first time, but the choice inside is different. He no longer calls each round “the last time,” nor does he immediately switch to a new spot after the tree heals.

When the handle loosens, he fixes it first; when moon dust piles at his feet, he clears a stable spot; when osmanthus petals fall on his shoulder, he waits until a break to wipe them away. These small actions have no magical effect, but they keep the work from being ruined by impatience.

Those who look at the moon from Earth sometimes catch the shadow of the osmanthus tree and hear elders tell the tale of Wu Gang’s axe. Some say it’s a mighty man battling a divine tree, but the chronicler clears it up in the slip: the real punishment lies not only in the tree’s endless restoration but also in Wu Gang’s long‑term confrontation with his own unwillingness to finish anything.

Wu Gang hears this and says, “You wrote it too heavily.”

Aman asks, “Shall we lighten it?”

He looks at the osmanthus tree, shakes his head, and says, “The material in the furnace is heavier than this sentence.”

The moonlight passes the palace wall, the immortal master never appears, and Wu Gang never receives permission to leave. The osmanthus still heals after each cut, and a single reflection cannot make up for past greed; the punishment forces him to learn true focus over a long time.

So the axe keeps striking.

Wu Gang may still be impatient in the long years, and he still has to pull his heart back to the present each time. Change is not a one‑time epiphany that never wavers, but a different choice made whenever the old thought returns.

Aman records the Moon Palace’s osmanthus tree, the axe, and the endless labor at the end of the bamboo slip, then steps back. The slip only keeps the sound of the axe and the moon’s shadow, so future generations know why there are always woodcutters in the moon.

Wu Gang rests for a moment, then stands again before the osmanthus tree. He admits the tree will still heal, that the sentence has no visible end, and that none of this is a reason to look for shortcuts again.

The next swing falls, and the scent of osmanthus spreads. The axe mark slowly closes in the moonlight, and Wu Gang lifts the axe again.

Wu Gang remains punished in the Moon Palace, the osmanthus still heals after each cut. Yet each round of labor trains him to focus, to take responsibility, and to remember his mistakes. When people look up at the moon from Earth, they see both eternal punishment and a person’s long, delayed reflection.
