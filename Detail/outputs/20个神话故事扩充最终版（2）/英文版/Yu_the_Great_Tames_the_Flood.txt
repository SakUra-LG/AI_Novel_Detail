The sky hadn’t fully brightened, but the smoke from Willow Bend’s chimneys was already tilting its neck and drifting upward. The east wind had been too fierce last night, making the firewood crackle and pop in the stove. Women squatted by the well, scrubbing clothes, their wooden paddles thudding against the stone troughs with a dull, rhythmic beat, like drums urging the spring plowing. Old Potter was bent over, repairing a winnowing basket. The bamboo strips were as soft as noodles in his hands; he wound them twice, pressed down three times, and with a sharp *snap*, bit off the excess tips. A spray of spit landed on the bamboo nodes. “The wheat ears are heavy this year,” he grunted. “We’ll need seven full days of sun before Grain Rain!” A kid nearby tiptoed to steal a piece of millet cake from his cutting board, got a gentle flick on the forehead, and watched crumbs rain into his pocket. Suddenly, someone shouted, “The eastern embankment has collapsed!”

No one believed it. There had been two false alarms last year, both caused by badgers digging tunnels. It wasn’t until the dogs started barking madly that a few shirtless men, splashing through muddy yellow water, stumbled into view. They carried split elm beams on their shoulders, leaving long trails of mud behind them. The flood came with terrifying speed. First, it overflowed the field ridges, swelling the bean shoots. Then it licked at the base of the mud walls, bubbling up through the brick cracks. Finally, it smashed through the fences, carrying haystacks, chicken coops, and half a basket of unpicked cucumbers straight toward the ancestral hall steps. An old woman clutched her incense burner so tightly she wouldn’t let go, a bronze tripod upside down on her head, clanging like a bell. Aman rolled down from the western slope. The green bamboo slips in his arms were tied tighter than his own life. His chin bumped against the sharp edge of the third slip, and he mumbled incoherently, “...record... flood enters the village...” He spat out a mouthful of muddy water, coughing for a while before realizing the ink had already blurred into gray spots and the brush bristles had splayed out like dandelions. “Historian,” old Li, who carried manure, wiped his face, water still dripping from his ladle. “If those slips rot in the water, we won’t even have a reference for which direction to flee!” Aman struggled to sit up, shaking the water from his sleeves. “I’m not here to manage the direction—I’m here to record how the direction changes.”

A wet duck swam past his knee, quacked twice, and snatched the bald brush he had tucked behind his ear. The crowd was already surging toward the high ground, carrying babies in baskets on their backs, pots balanced on shoulder poles, and infants strapped to their chests. One toddler rode bare-bottomed on a donkey, tilting his head back to drink milk, while his other hand clutched a piece of caramel-colored malt sugar. The sweet, fishy scent floated on the turbid water, faint and almost inaudible. Aman knelt in the mud, digging through the scattered bamboo slips, his fingertips bleeding but never letting go. Suddenly, he felt a shadow overhead. Looking up, he saw the old woman with the bronze tripod standing behind him, holding an umbrella. One of the ribs was missing, and rain poured down the gap onto his left shoulder. “Write it down,” she said. “Record more names, so the water doesn’t take them away without us knowing who they were.” Aman raised his hand, tying a knot in the mud with his fingers, as if adding a talisman for himself. He muttered to himself, *This old lady really thinks an umbrella is a waterproof shield. What a craft that could actually block all the water.* Aman’s Adam’s apple bobbed. He dipped his finger in the muddy water and drew a single horizontal line on the top slip: “Beginning of the Flood.” He looked up and saw a duck hopping right by his feet, as if trying to help him write, but only splashing the muddy water into a bigger mess. Aman smiled helplessly and whispered, “You little guy, don’t treat the brush like a toy.” The duck quacked, as if responding, as if saying, “I want to write too!” Aman looked up again, seeing the floating bamboo slips on the water like little boats, rocking with the waves. He couldn’t help but think of his father Gun’s failed attempt to dam the water. He made a silent vow: *This time, I must change from blocking to channeling. Only then can the floodwaters finally be guided to the sea, and the Nine Provinces be at peace.*

The eastern embankment collapsed for the seventh time. The cracks in the rammed-earth dike gaped like mouths, unable to swallow the water, instead sucking up the mud and oozing it out. Gun stood at the breach, his straw raincoat collar stuffed with wind, his fingers digging into his palms until his fingernails turned white. He stared at the water—the level had risen another three inches since yesterday. The waves slapped against the newly built reed-weave dam, making a *pfft-pfft* sighing sound, like a trapped beast gnawing on its own paws. “Raise it three feet!” he roared. His voice was hoarse, shaking the reed flowers loose so they drifted down. A dozen strong men immediately jumped into the chest-deep water, lifting stones, filling in silt, and pressing down wicker baskets. The places where their shoulders were rubbed raw had scabs like salt frost. Someone handed him a bowl of hot ginger soup. He took it and gulped it down, the bottom of the bowl clacking against his teeth. He turned around and smashed the empty bowl into the mudflat. “Bring another thirty baskets of cobblestones! Before tonight, we must seal off the northern hollow!”

But the next morning, the southern bank burst. On the third day, a corner of the western ridge reservoir collapsed, and the muddy current sliced diagonally through the rice paddies, turning the furrows into snake-like forks. On the fourth night, a thunderstorm struck suddenly. A lightning bolt hit the locust tree on the main dam. After a deafening boom, the entire section of the dike wall sank silently, as if the earth had yawned and swallowed half a year’s hard work in one gulp. The water rose higher. It wasn’t a slow rise; it pushed up—toppling bridge piers, capsizing ferries, knocking down half-dried cured fish from the drying racks, and even soaking the eaves of the ancestral hall until the joints popped loose. The children dared not approach the pond, pointing at the whirlpools and saying, “There’s a dragon turning over down there.” The old men shook their heads. “Where’s the dragon? That’s just the pit your fathers and grandfathers filled with their lives.”

The execution platform stood on a bend of the Si River. The bronze axe head caught the autumn sun, cold and blunt. When Gun slipped the ceremonial sword into the guard’s hands, his wrist trembled, the iron ring struck the jade ornament, and a sharp clink rang out. The execution officer’s proclamation was low, yet each word hammered itself into the soil: “Block the flood of a hundred rivers, let the high dam fall into the low, and when the work fails, the harm grows worse.”

The night before the execution, he sat in a corner of the prison barracks, tracing a line on the ground with a charcoal stick: the starting point was a mountain stream upstream, the end a solitary rock deep in the downstream marsh. The line grew thicker, eventually turning into a blot of black. A guard brought in a bowl of rice, glanced at the marks, and asked casually, “What are you drawing, sir?” Gun didn’t look up; he just smoothed the ink blot, mud and ash staining his fingertips. “It’s how I thought I could hold it back,” he said.

A few days later, a messenger sent by the tribal chief arrived on the lingering tide. He stood in the middle of the ruins, eyes sweeping over the collapsed floodgate, the floating baskets, the clothes hanging on dead branches, and finally landing on the young man quietly straightening his conical hat among the crowd. That man was crouched, pulling a soaked spindle from the water, his hands covered in mud. The messenger spoke, his voice soft but silencing the surroundings: “Gun failed to control the waters, and the flood remains. Now the task of taming the waters passes to you.” The young man rose, took the token in both hands. He was Yu the Great. The water was still rising, but instead of ordering higher levees at once, he turned and headed up the mountain path, intent on understanding where the water came from and where it could go.

Before setting out, Yu walked around the village. He didn’t ask “how big is the flood?” He asked each household where the water entered the house, at what hour it rose fastest, and on which side the silt piled after the water receded. Some said the east wall got wet first, others said bubbles rose under the stove, and a few pointed to their own roof, insisting the water came “straight down from the sky.” Aman felt his wrist go sour and couldn’t help asking, “Do we count that as a waterway too?” Yu looked up at a roof with three holes and replied, “Sure, but that route needs a new roof first.”

The homeowner finally remembered the leaky roof, grabbed a wooden ladder and ran off, while the onlookers laughed for a moment. When the laughter faded, Yu arranged the villagers’ statements with stones on a small hill: white stones stood for mountains, black stones for old levees, broken reeds for channels. People gathered around, chattering at first, then noticing that the houses reporting the east wall getting wet formed a low-lying strip, and the bubbling‑stove spots lined up with an old ditch choked full of mud.

An elder nudged a black stone forward and said habitually, “If we build another high wall here, the water can’t get in.” Yu didn’t argue right away; he scooped a handful of murky water and poured it slowly into the shallow pit outlined by the stones. The water pooled behind the high wall, deepening over time, then seeped out at the thinnest spot, sweeping away the grass blades that represented three households. The elder stared at the drifting grass, silent for a while, then bent down and retrieved the black stone.

Aman hurried to reset the grass blades, but planted a whole patch upside down in the mud. A nearby child pointed out, “This house’s roof is upside down!” Aman kept his composure: “That just means the damage is severe; it’s already flipped.” The child thought it over and actually nodded. Yu righted the grass blades, then dug a narrow channel from the edge of the shallow pit. He poured another scoop of water; it ran out through the channel, leaving only a thin layer in the pit. “We don’t need to stop building levees,” he said, “but first we must give the water a place to go.”

The stone‑laying experiment didn’t stop the flood outright, but it showed everyone the difference between “blocking” and “draining.” Yu enlisted those who knew the mountain paths into several teams to hunt for old river traces along the low strip; he himself took Aman up the hills to see where tributaries joined at the valleys. The villagers no longer waited for a fresh command—they handed over the paths they’d trod for years, the water marks they’d seen, and the losses they’d suffered onto this muddy map.

Yu climbed to a high spot, snapped a dry branch, and drew the valleys and rivers on the mud. He didn’t rush to outline a new levee; he followed the water’s footprints to the lower ground. Aman followed, panting, standing on tiptoe to see the sketch, when his foot slipped into a badger’s den. He tumbled forward, arms flailing like a bird trying to take off, and got stuck on a slope, yet somehow kept the green bamboo slip steady in his hands. “I’m not falling,” he explained, lying there, “I’m just testing which side the water prefers.” A nearby cowherd boy quipped, “Well, you certainly tested it right—your face hit first.” Yu stifled a laugh, pulled him to level ground, and they kept moving toward the next pass. As the morning mist lingered, the two crossed a few creek branches. Aman wrote, “Yu climbs high to study the water,” and as his brush touched the paper, a wild rabbit darted past his feet. He almost tipped the whole bundle of bamboo slips into a shallow bay, scrambled to clutch them, muttering, “I haven’t figured out the mountains and rivers yet, but the rabbit already showed me an escape route.”

He frantically dried the slips, only to find the character for “gorge” had spread into a tadpole‑shaped tail. The cowherd boy grinned, “Uncle, your writing’s swimming now.” Aman kept a straight face, “It knows the way.”

On the third night, they stayed in a rocky cave, where the bonfire crackled and popped like exploding stars. Yu the Great spread out several green bamboo slips with their route, the dim oil lamp casting huge, silent shadows on the stone walls. Aman leaned in to take notes, but suddenly muttered, "Uncle used to study the terrain like this, didn't he?" As soon as the words left his mouth, he fell silent - he shouldn't have mentioned it. Sure enough, Yu the Great paused, stirred the fire, and sparks leapt up like golden millet: "He remembered where every rock was submerged, I don't need to remember where they're buried." Aman quickly erased the previous line and started a new one, writing neatly: "Observe its flow, examine its stillness, gauge its momentum before making plans."

At midnight, the rain grew heavier, but Yu the Great didn't put away the route map. Instead, he picked up the oil lamp and walked to the cave entrance. He placed three clay bowls with holes in them side by side under the eaves, one with a layer of loose soil, one with crushed stones, and one with nothing. The rainwater dripped into the bowls through three stone grooves, falling at different speeds. Aman squatted beside him, holding the bamboo slips, and at first, he could still write neatly. However, as time passed, he grew drowsy, his head nodding forward, and he almost dipped his nose into the ink.

The first bowl filled up quickly, the muddy water so murky it was impossible to see the bottom. The second bowl's water was clearer, but it seeped out from the edges, bit by bit. The third bowl filled up the slowest, but the dripping sound never stopped. Yu the Great compared the positions of the three bowls with the soil and rocks on the mountain, judging which slopes were prone to landslides and which could slow down the water. Aman rubbed his nose, woke up, and saw that the mud and sand at the bottom of the bowls had formed three distinct layers. He exclaimed, "Rain can write its own script!"

"And it writes more straightforwardly than you do," Yu the Great said.

Aman looked down and saw that, in his drowsy state, he had written "crushed stones drink water" instead of "crushed stones seep water." He immediately closed the bamboo slips halfway: "Let the rain write its own story, and I'll write mine. We don't need to compare." Yu the Great didn't expose his mistake, but instead, told him to pour the water from the three bowls onto the slope at the cave entrance. The murky water carved out a deep ditch, the clear water only washed away a little fine sand, and the slow-dripping bowl's water followed the mossy trail and turned into a natural stone groove. Aman was now fully awake, lying on the ground to follow the three streams of water and take notes, oblivious to his torn knee.

The next day, they didn't start digging the main canal right away. Instead, they opened three short trenches on different slopes. The first trench was straight, and the water washed away its banks as soon as it flowed through. The second trench was too curved, and the mud and sand clogged the bend. The third trench followed the existing stone grooves, and although the water flowed slowly, it flowed steadily into the next low-lying area. The people involved in digging the trenches gathered around the two failed ones, looking at them for a while. Someone sighed, saying they had wasted half a day. However, Yu the Great told Aman to include the failed sections in the route map: "Today, it's just a short trench that collapsed. If we don't try, tomorrow it will be the main canal and the village that collapse."

Aman drew the two failed trenches with extra-thick lines. When someone asked him why, he said confidently, "Mistakes that took effort should also take up some space." This remark made everyone laugh, but no one was in a hurry to fill in the failed sections. Instead, they used wooden stakes to mark the locations where the banks had collapsed and the sand had accumulated, preparing to avoid the same problems in the next stage of construction.

A torrential downpour struck on the afternoon of the fifth day, forcing them to take shelter among the gnarled roots of an ancient cypress tree. The tree’s branches and leaves spread out like giant umbrellas, raindrops dancing on the tips with a crisp, rhythmic sound, as if the sky were drumming against the earth. The heavy rain poured down like a river set ablaze, splashing against the stone-paved path and sending up sprays of water, as if showcasing nature’s fury. Aman opened a newly shaved narrow bamboo slip, his fingers gliding lightly over the damp surface, feeling as though he were touching a stone just soaked by rain. With solemn care, he wrote: “Water’s nature is best guided, not just blocked.” He looked up toward the distant ridge, where the outlines of mountain peaks were faintly visible through the mist, like giants wrapped in fog. The old herb-gatherer beside him peeked over, chuckled, and said, “Young man, you’re missing a stroke on the right side of that character for ‘guide.’” Aman squinted closely and realized he had indeed missed a dot—his pen tip had been knocked off course by a raindrop while he rushed to dodge the dripping eaves. He bit his lip and retraced the stroke, but the ink bled out, forming a small, stubborn cloud-like blot that seemed to dance on the slip. As dusk deepened, the sky turned a deep blue from the rain, the clouds looked as if they had been splashed with ink, and the light at the horizon was swallowed by the mist, leaving only the swaying shadows of the cypress in the rain. Yu the Great stood on the ridge, gazing into the distance: the muddy river was turning a corner, scouring the boulders at the valley mouth and rolling away in white foam. He untied his bundle, took out a clay jar, scooped half a ladle of clear water, and poured it into his palm. Slowly tilting his arm, he watched the water flow along the lines of his palm, splitting when it hit a bump and pausing in a dip, before finally gathering into a single stream and falling to the bottom of the cliff. Raindrops jumped on the water’s surface in his palm, like tiny dancers performing. “Look at how it walks,” he said. Aman was stunned, then grabbed his brush handle, pressed it against his chin, and nodded vigorously, his mouth twitching slightly. “Hmm... it knows how to walk better than I do.” He looked up, rain dripping from his hair as if washing away the day’s dust. The sound of the rain and the roar of the river blended into a passionate symphony, as if telling them: Yu the Great’s plan was slowly unfolding, and the flow of the water was being guided by his careful hand. Aman patted his own shoulder lightly and smiled. “Uncle Yu, your technique is steadier than my handwriting.” Yu the Great smiled, a mix of kindness and determination flashing in his eyes, as if telling him: though the path to taming the floods is long, it leads to a bright end. The rain fell harder, and the wind shook the cypress branches, as if applauding their efforts. Aman tucked his brush into his sleeve, looked into the distance, and silently noted in his heart: the water’s path must be cleared, the mountain’s path must be channeled, and only through persistence could the floods finally return to the sea, the fields and villages reappear, and the Nine Provinces find peace.

On the morning of the seventh day, the sound of axes was the first to wake the mountain hollow. Yu the Great swung his pickaxe into the cracks of the green oak rock, the shock splitting the skin at the base of his thumb. He spat out the saliva mixed with the earthy taste of soil, blew on his palm, gripped his tool tighter, and kept digging. Aman crouched on the slope, recording: “Third quarter of the Mao hour, began splitting the eastern ridge gap.” Before he finished speaking, loose gravel from behind him tumbled down. Instinctively, he leaned back, and the back of his head thudded against a tree knot with a dull “thunk.” The bamboo slip bounced twice, and a drop of ink landed on the left side of the character for “gap,” turning it into a little black mole. A shepherd boy picked up a flat, round pebble and handed it over. “Uncle, use this to hold down your paper!” Aman rubbed his head, took the stone, and placed it on the corner of the slip, muttering, “It’s steadier than I am.”

During the midday break, everyone gnawed on cold wheat cakes. Aman unfolded a new page, ground ink with water, and wrote carefully, stroke by stroke: “Chisel the mountain to guide the water; use curved force rather than direct confrontation.” As he wrote the right-side radical of the character for “curved,” a bead of sweat rolled down from his temple into his sleeve. His brush suddenly slipped, pulling the hook too long and crossing half the character—it looked as if someone had poked it with a stick. The man sitting next to him, chewing on cake crumbs, glanced over and said, “That character for ‘curved’ looks quite aggrieved.” Aman blew on the wet ink, kept a straight face, and said, “It’s just showing off its strength on purpose.”

Work ended in the evening, and the rain returned. Aman wrapped his straw raincoat tight and wrote quickly: “Rain pours in the You hour, yet the ditch is already a foot deep, and the water drains slowly along the channel.” He had just closed the bamboo slip when a gust of wind blew his conical hat off. He reached out to catch it, but the mud underfoot suddenly sucked his boot with a “squelch.” His body twisted into a pretzel shape. His right hand clung desperately to the bamboo slip, while his left hand grabbed a mossy rock to keep from falling flat on his back. Three young men digging the ditch couldn’t hold back their laughter. Embarrassed and annoyed, Aman simply sat cross-legged in the mud, threw his head back, and shouted, “Laugh all you want! Wait until I carve today’s incident onto the ancestral stele!”

At night, more than ten bonfires were lit. Amid the flickering flames, people took turns wielding chisels to pry stones and carrying wood and soil. Aman curled up by the fire to dry his bamboo slips, his fingers numb from the cold but his hand never stopping. Suddenly, he heard the rhythmic song of ramming earth nearby, rough in tone but strong and unified: “Hey-yo—if you can’t bend the mountain, ask it to shift its shoulder!” Listening to it, Aman quietly scraped away the four characters he had originally written, “Human strength is limited,” and added a new line in the blank space: “When there are many people, even the mountain will make way.”

In the distance the mountain shadows sat still, and only a newly cut shallow channel glimmered faintly. Murky water tentatively spilled into it, turned a gentle corner, and slipped down to a lower spot. A massive boulder at the mountain pass had lain there for ages, its gray‑brown skin stained with rust‑colored water marks. Yu the Great walked around it seven times, his trouser cuffs caked with grass seeds and ancient silt, his fingers repeatedly feeling the stone’s shallow dents, the cracks of his nails catching black grit. “It’s not a hard top,” he said, wiping the sweat from his forehead, then turned to face the crowd. “Let’s give the water a shoulder—dig a shallow groove on the left, raise a berm on the right, and let it tip‑toe over.”

The people split to each side of the boulder as he instructed. Those on the left used copper chisels to shave the stone groove lower, while those on the right packed broken rocks into vine baskets, stacking them to raise the outer berm. At first everyone blamed the other side: the diggers complained the berm‑builders were shaking loose the mud, and the berm‑builders retorted that the diggers kept loosening the compacted edges. Their shouts bounced off the stone wall, sounding like the mountain itself were arguing.

Yu the Great didn’t stand in the middle to judge who worked harder. He scooped a ladle of water and poured it from above; as the water hit the stone it split into two streams—one diving into the shallow groove, the other slamming against the high berm and swirling back, just enough to loosen the berm’s foot. The berm‑builders saw the mud clump collapse and stopped arguing; the diggers noticed the groove was too narrow, so any extra water would spill back. The two crews swapped places, each going to inspect the other’s work.

Aman wanted to help measure the groove’s width, so he laid a green bamboo slip across the ditch like a ruler. The water surged up, nearly washing the whole strip away. He dove, grabbed the slip, his chest pressed into the wet mud, and shouted, “Got the size! About one‑and‑a‑half slips!” A nearby worker pulled him up and teased, “You’re measuring the ditch today—tomorrow you’ll be measuring fish with it?” Aman wiped the mud and replied, “If the fish are willing to line up, we can always negotiate.”

When work resumed, each time the left‑hand crew dug a bit more, the right‑hand crew paused to check the water level; each time the right‑hand crew added a layer to the berm, the left crew inspected the groove to make sure it wasn’t clogged by falling earth. At dusk they released the first test flow. The water didn’t rush straight at the boulder; instead it slipped along the shallow side, rounded smoothly at the high berm, and kept its course. The crowd chased the water for dozens of steps, saw that the downstream bank stayed damp but solid, and felt brave enough to widen the upstream opening a little more.

The second surge came even stronger, and branches piled up at the bend. A young man grabbed a long pole to descend into the ditch, but Yu the Great stopped him. First they used forked wooden rods on the bank to part the branches, then two men, secured with ropes around their waists, slipped in to clear them. When the branches finally gave way, the water shot out like a geyser, splashing everyone with mud droplets. Aman looked down at his freshly washed lapel and solemnly announced, “This waterway is ready for the historian’s seal.”

No one bothered to wipe him off, because the water was now calmly flowing over the boulder, heading toward the lower valley. The boulder stayed put, untouched by any reckless smashing; the new groove and high berm weren’t finished in one go but only after two test runs revealed the right width. Yu the Great had the crew carve the back‑flow line onto the stone surface, a reminder for future builders: giving water a path isn’t the same as just cutting a hole—every bend must survive the next rain.

A few burly men exchanged glances, hefted their copper chisels, and headed for the northern rock wall. At first the hammer blows were scattered, then they woven into a dense rhythm—clangs, clangs, hums—bouncing back and forth through the canyon. A young worker tried three times to wedge a peg into a mortise, huffing and swearing, “This rock is tougher than my mother‑in‑law!” A nearby woman, sifting sand for the foundation, looked up and spat, “What are you yammering about? Your father built dams with arms as swollen as winter melons and never complained!”

As twilight soaked the mountain hollow, the newly cut water diversion took shape, a crooked yet determined arm reaching downstream. Yu the Great knelt in the mud, scooped a handful of pooled water and poured it into a pre‑dug pit, watching how it seeped, lingered, and quietly spread over the edge. A wind brushed the scabbed sunspot on his neck, carrying faint frog croaks from the old reed marsh that had been flooded last year. Midnight thunder rolled, rain slanted down like spears. No one rose to hide; they simply draped their raincoats over the freshly built earthen stacks. The fire dimmed, casting faces smeared with mud, eyelashes glistening with droplets. Someone broke a half‑charred millet cake and handed a piece to a nearby boy, “Eat up, you’ll be swinging a hoe all day tomorrow.” The child bit the half, his cheeks puffing, eyes bright as lanterns. Dawn’s first light tinged the sky; dew trembled on reed tips, ready to fall. Yu the Great stood where he’d marked the night before, drew his pocket dagger and traced a long, sweeping arc in the damp soil—not a straight line, not a jagged break, but a flowing, breathing curve. He pointed at the jagged peaks ahead, “That’s the Dragon Gate. The name’s there, the road’s still waiting to be made.”

Everyone followed his gaze and saw, between the mist-wrapped sheer cliffs, a deep crevice that flickered in and out of view. No one spoke, only the sound of a shovel blade scraping gravel—rustling, steady, growing louder together. The morning fog hadn't lifted yet, and Yu the Great waded barefoot through the newly risen tide of a creek, water dripping from his pant legs. Aman trailed right behind, one arm clutching a bamboo slip, the other holding an old straw coat tied with a half‑cut rope. He stumbled into a soft mud pit, flopped forward, his chin dragging three inches of mud across the ground, while the slip stayed perfectly still against his chest. “Ahem… that wasn’t a fall, that was a water‑level test!” he propped his neck up to argue, a dried willow leaf stuck to his nose. Turning past the locust‑tree hill, thin smoke curled above a low wall. A crisp bark rang out, followed by a little girl’s milk‑sweet shout: “Daddy! The flatbread’s getting cold—” Yu the Great halted a beat, his Adam’s apple bobbing, eyes sweeping over the shadow of a clay jar swinging in a fence gap, then he spun toward the collapsed western ferry‑dam, lifted a leg, and walked on. Aman hurried to turn a fresh page, his pen trembling, ink splattering like tadpoles: “At the hour of the Dragon, passing the old house, hearing a child's voice, not staying.”

In the afternoon they reached Wild Boar Ravine, and a sudden downpour broke out. The group scrambled to pitch a thatched shelter; Aman ducked under a leaky bundle of firewood and wrote: “At the hour of the Monkey, torrential rain, the eaves run like a river; luckily the ditch already bears three‑tenths of the water.” He licked the pen tip to moisten the ink, and his tongue tasted salty‑bitter—he’d unknowingly bitten his lip. The old reed‑craftsman next door glanced over, handed him a piece of ginger candy, and said, “Suck on this so you don’t write with blood instead of vermilion.”

The rain fell harder, and the new ditch held most of the water, but a spot on the old bank’s earth slope began to crumble away. Yu the Great heard three muffled thuds from outside the shelter, dropped his dry rations, and led a few men to investigate. At the foot of the slope a bulging mud lump had formed; if the whole section slid into the ditch, the freshly cleared channel would clog again. Some argued to dig out the slope right away before it collapsed, while others warned that moving earth in the rain was too risky and suggested pulling back for now.

Yu the Great snapped off a long reed and angled it into the mud lump from a safe spot. The reed slipped half a foot in before toppling, showing the inside was already water‑soaked and hollow. He didn’t let anyone get too close to hard digging; instead he cut three narrow trenches on the ridge top, guiding the rainwater seeping into the soil toward both sides. Then he had the crew drive stakes from a distance and use long ropes to pull away the tree roots scattered at the slope’s base. Aman was assigned to stand up high and report any changes in the mud lump, shouting every now and then, “It’s still bulging!” By the fifth shout his voice was tinged with irritation, “How does it stay put better than I do?”

The old reed‑craftsman shouted back through the rain, “You two who can stay stubbornest—let’s see who wins when the water recedes!”

On the sixth check the mud lump stopped growing, and the seepage at the ridge cleared up. Yu the Great still didn’t let anyone relax; he waited until the side trenches were steadily draining before sending the men with waist‑ropes to shave off the loose soil from the edge. The first bucket of mud they hauled up leaked out most of its contents; the second bucket, a tightly woven rattan one, was so heavy the two carriers toppled together onto the ground. Aman rushed to help, only to get splashed himself and end up half a mud statue. He wiped his face and said, “Great, we lost a bucket of mud, but we gained three mud sculptures.”

The group laughed, switched to smaller buckets, made a few more trips, and finally cleared all the dangerous loose soil. When the rain finally stopped, the new ditch still carried water and the slope hadn’t given way as a whole. Yu the Great ordered the three temporary side trenches to be left in place, plus a row of observation stakes, so that after every heavy rain someone could come by to check water color and soil cracks. Flood control was no longer just the grand feat of carving a dragon gate; it also meant spotting a risky slope on a stormy night, testing a solution, and passing that method on to the next crew on duty.

Aman returned to the thatched shelter and expanded the line “luckily the ditch already bears three‑tenths of the water” into a full paragraph. The ginger candy had dissolved in the chaos, but he kept it stuck in his mouth, speaking with a sweet, muddled tone. The old reed‑craftsman listened for a while, then asked, “Junior scribe, did you write it down correctly?” Aman turned the bamboo slip toward him: “Got it. Just a few characters got a little plump from the rain.”

Outside the shelter, the newly cut side ditch kept slowly draining the hillside, never stopping even as night deepened.

At dusk, on the way back they passed his own home. The courtyard gate was ajar, the clatter of pots and pans clear, and a man's cough sounded heavy and oppressed. Yu the Great paused, picked up a broken branch from the ground, and slowly traced the water’s flow on the muddy earth, the line winding eastward. Aman held his breath while recording, then noticed the final stroke of the character “East” spreading like a lost little snake. He frantically scooped mud onto his fingertips, only making the smear larger, and finally forced himself to add, “This is proof that water’s intent expands on its own.”

On the way back at dusk, they passed his home again. The courtyard gate hung slightly ajar. Inside, the clatter of pots and pans was sharp and clear, mixed with a man’s cough—low, heavy, and stifling. Yu the Great paused for a moment, picked up a broken twig from the ground, and slowly traced the flow of water in the mud. The lines wound their way eastward. Aman held his breath as he recorded it, then suddenly noticed the last stroke of the character for “East” had blurred and spread, looking like a little lost snake. He frantically rubbed the mud off his fingertips, but the smudge only got bigger. Swallowing his pride, he added, “This is proof that the water’s spirit extends on its own.” The next dawn, while the rooster’s crow was still faint, Aman squatted on the worn stone steps outside the threshold, organizing the scraps of his notes from the day before. Suddenly, he felt warm breath brush past his ear. It was that old hen who always loved to peck at his inkstone. He ducked his neck to dodge, and the bamboo slips flew out of his hand, splashing into the puddle at the door. He lunged to grab them, his knee slamming hard against the edge of a blue brick. He hissed in pain but managed to hold the back of the slip with one hand to keep the ink from running. A neighbor walking by with a basin of dirty water glanced over and laughed, “Your Majesty the Historian, does this slip count as washed today?” Aman stood up, shaking his dripping wrist, water droplets flying from his hair. His voice was hoarse. “Washed well—only when clear water carries the words do they truly enter the riverbed.”

The third time he passed by, it was late summer. The cicadas were quiet, and the wind carried the smoky scent of burned rice stalks. Yu the Great stepped over a newly packed earth ridge. The sole of his boot had split, and with every step, mud bubbled out of the crack. His shoulder blades protruded, rising and falling like two gentle slopes beneath his faded hemp shirt. The bronze spade hanging from his belt was long worn down, its edge curled, yet it remained firmly tucked at his right hip. His home sat on the hill above. The wooden bolt hung loose, and the wind made it tap gently against the frame—thump, thump, thump—slow, as if knocking on the heart itself. In the courtyard, a clothesline held up several brown garments, patch upon patch. A clay jar lay tipped over at the base of the wall, its water stain spreading into the shape of the route on the green bamboo slips. Under the eaves, a little girl sat with her bare feet propped up on the edge of a low stool, weaving a grasshopper out of wheat straw. She looked up, staring in this direction for a long time, then suddenly called out, “Papa! The well rope is broken!”

Her voice was clear and bright, cutting through the summer heat. Yu the Great clenched his left hand, his thumb rubbing repeatedly over an old scar on his palm—a souvenir from flying debris when he first pried loose a giant boulder. The moss beneath his feet was thick and slippery, but he stood firm. Behind him, the team carrying baskets was silent. The shoulder poles bent their spines, the baskets filled with freshly gathered mountain stone powder and tung oil residue, ready to coat the newly dug flood channels tomorrow. He didn’t turn around. He simply raised his right hand and gave a very slight nod toward the little girl, as certain as confirming the direction of a water vein. Then he turned, stepped forward with his left foot, and splashed into a muddy puddle ahead. A ripple spread out, quickly swallowed by the larger current. That ripple traveled far, drifting all the way to the edge of the ruins at the bottom of the hill, swirling before turning into a narrow ditch where weeds had just been cleared to reveal its outline—the remnant of an ancient riverbed they had identified by the stars the night before. The sky darkened, and the sunset flowed over the mountain ridge like molten gold.

He didn’t walk fast, but he never stopped moving forward. Behind him, cooking smoke rose, the aroma of food drifted, and children’s laughter rang out again, layer upon layer, like breath held deep within the earth for a long time before finally being released. On the day the gap at Longmen was finally chiseled through, the mountain wind rushed in, making the bamboo slips in Aman’s hands rattle loudly. He pressed down hard, but the edges of the bamboo still curled up, like fish fins swollen by water. “This draft is fiercer than my own kitchen stove!” He tiptoed to grab a slip that was about to slide away, but Yu the Great reached out to stop him. Yu was bending down to inspect the water path between the rocks, sweat mixed with dust trickling slowly down his cheek. During the noon break, several men used coarse clay bowls to scoop spring water seeping from the cliff below to drink. Aman leaned in to record, “The spring water is sweet and clear, like an outlet for the underground waterways.” Before he finished speaking, a bowl of water was splashed at him, wetting the tip of his brush. The old stonewipe wiped his mouth and smiled, “Little Historian, don’t just write. Last night it washed out the small levee. Come closer and see for yourself.”

As dusk fell, everyone worked together to push over the last boulder blocking the gorge. The loud rumble startled a flock of birds into flight, and Aman's bamboo slips quivered in his arms, the ink flowing down the vertical lines, forming a small black stream that flowed right through the middle of the characters "Dragon Gate". He quickly grabbed a cloth to wipe it, but ended up smudging the tail of the "dragon" character into a wiggly shape like a worm. The sudden rainstorm in the middle of the night made the temporary thatched hut leak like a sieve. Aman huddled in the corner, rewriting, the flickering candlelight casting shadows on his eyelashes, making them dance up and down. Suddenly, a loud crackling sound came from above, and when he looked up, he saw that the entire mat of reeds had been lifted up by the water, forming a bulge like a turtle shell about to flip over. He silently moved to a new position, dipped his brush in saliva, and retraced the character "pervious", muttering to himself, "Water has learned to arch over our roofs, what's our little effort compared to that?"

At dawn, someone pointed downstream and cheered: the turbulent waves were swirling into the new channel, rushing towards a wider valley. Aman rubbed his eyes and hurried to record it, his brush moving too quickly and piercing the bamboo slip, the hole staring at him like a pair of sleepy eyes. The water was starting to behave. The first thing to emerge was the roots of the willow trees on the west bank, their brown, curly branches rising above the water, covered in a soft, green downy material. Next, the stone roller on the drying field appeared, half-buried in the mud, the other half sticking out, its surface covered in a thin layer of soft moss, like an old, tattered cloak. A few bare-chested men waded over to lift it, and with a loud "heave-ho", they raised it out of its original position, and a string of bubbles rose from underneath - it turned out that the foundation, which had been soaked by the water, was now slowly releasing the stagnant air it had held for many years. The terraced fields on the southern slope reappeared, layer by layer, the ditches winding like knife cuts, although some sections had collapsed, the overall layout was still intact. An old man stood on the slope, leaning on his cane, staring at his vegetable garden in a daze, then suddenly untied the gourd from his waist and took a big swig of wine, coughing repeatedly, and after he finished coughing, he grinned, "The scallion seedlings haven't drowned! Pinch some to stir-fry with eggs, just the right amount of salt."

The rotten wooden stakes of the ferry dock emerged one by one, and the boatmen counted them repeatedly, their muddy fingers pointing to the stakes: "seventy-two... no, the seventy-third stake is swaying violently." A woman next to them was washing rice, and the water splashed onto the stake, revealing the cinnabar character "ren" written underneath - a mark left by the craftsmen when the dock was first built, now a dark red color, like a drop of congealed blood. The children were the first to venture into the newly exposed beach, picking up seashells, snail shells, and peach pits that had drifted ashore who-knew-when. A little boy held up a turtle shell, shouting, "This shell is older than my grandfather!" No one paid attention to him, but a cowherd on the shore was leading his cow to drink, the cow's nose buried in the shallow water, sucking for a long time, then lifting its head and shaking its mane, spraying a large sheet of crystal clear water vapor, and for a moment, a rainbow appeared in the sunlight. The breach upstream was gradually closing, no longer roaring, only a gentle trickle flowing along the new channel. The people followed the water, walking past the collapsed fence, around the tilted woodpile, and through the new marsh filled with cattails - the water formed three gentle whirlpools here, then meekly turned a corner and flowed into the deeper, wider old river channel.

In the evening, the cooking smoke rose from the rubble at the foot of the mountain like a thin, swaying silk ribbon, as if blown by the wind. The originally scattered smoke had now merged into a hazy blue mist, like the sky's clouds had been burned into a pale blue color. The villagers were busy cleaning up the remnants in the fields, the fires reflecting off the rooftops, the smoke carrying the fragrance of burning wood, like a warm cloak covering the entire village.

Someone was chopping on a wooden board at a nearby table, the rhythmic thuds like an old-fashioned drumbeat, steadily pounding on the wood, producing a low, rhythmic sound. The iron pot on the stove was boiling, the ladle clanging against the pot, making a crisp, metallic sound, like adding a metallic beat to the cooking. The dog's barking was sometimes sharp, sometimes short, like it was pulling the entire street into the rhythm, but unlike the desperate howls during the famine, it sounded more like the village's guardian was barking.

Overnight, the wild amaranth pushed out its tender green leaves from the cracks in the rubble, and the purple flowers clung to the damp ground, like scattering starry, pink petals on the soil. The plowshare lay quietly by the warehouse door, its surface rusty, like an old soldier's armor. The owner squatted beside it, holding a whetstone, the grinding sound mingling with the insect chirping, like a natural melody playing softly in the night.

The river water still flowed, but it was no longer as violent as before. It had become gentle, even a bit lazy, like it had drunk some cool tea and was slowly carrying the fallen leaves, duckweed, and the occasional struggling dragonfly larvae on its surface, drifting lazily towards the lower, flatter sea. The water's surface formed a series of small ripples, like a gentle drumbeat, lightly tapping against the shore's stones.

On the day the river finally reached the sea, Yu the Great sat on a reef, quietly watching the waves roll in from the distance. The surf crashed up, the chill feeling like hail rising from the deep ocean floor, soaking right through the soles of his straw sandals. Yu the Great slowly untied the hemp rope wrapped around his wrist, letting it scatter in the wind, as if releasing an old rope to dance freely in the breeze.

Aman crouched on the sand a few paces away, holding a bamboo slip in his hands. He rubbed the ink stains off with crushed salt grains between his fingertips. The sea wind made his eyelids sting, but he dared not blink—afraid he might miss the sight of the seawater swallowing the last thread of muddy current. Just then, a child ran across the beach, splashing through the mud and water. His laughter was crisp and short: "Uncle Yu, the water is so cold!" He reached out toward Yu the Great, trying to grab that coolness, but accidentally slipped his foot

"It's recorded," Aman said. He wrote from Gun's failure to control the floods to Yu the Great taking on the heavy responsibility, from Yu changing the strategy from blocking to channeling and leading the people to carve through mountains and guide the waters, to passing his own home three times without entering. He wrote of the people digging through Longmen and clearing the Nine Rivers, finally guiding the floodwaters into the sea and bringing peace to the Nine Provinces. Every stroke had its origin, and bore the fingerprints and footprints of many people. A group of young men carrying hoes passed by; the leader jerked his chin toward Yu the Great: "Going home for real this time?" Yu the Great nodded, stood up, and brushed the mud off his trouser legs. Only after Aman carefully put away the bamboo slip did he realize he had been clutching half a cold bun without taking a bite.

He broke off a small corner of the cold bun and tossed it to a seagull, then walked along the newly exposed path toward home. At the village entrance, a clothesline had been hung up again under the locust tree, the bedsheets still bearing mud stains that wouldn't wash out. As Yu the Great approached, a little girl sat on the threshold, hugging a faded cloth tiger. She looked at him for a moment, then suddenly turned and shouted into the house, "Dad's back!" The door opened quickly, and his wife stood inside holding a clay basin, her hands shaking so much that water spilled all over the floor. Yu the Great paused, but this time he did not walk past. He stepped over the threshold, first picked up the child, and then whispered to his wife, "I'm back." There was no feast in the house, only a pot of coarse porridge warming on the stove. The child complained that his beard scratched her face and pushed him away twice, but on the third try, she hugged his neck even tighter. Yu the Great sat by the stove drinking porridge, silent for a long time; his wife did not ask about those years, only refilled his empty bowl.

Aman stood outside the courtyard, not going in to disturb them. He filed "Yu the Great Controls the Waters" into *The Twenty Bamboo Slips of Mountains and Seas*, and thinking of the slip about the Foolish Old Man Removing the Mountains, he wrote in the corner of the page: "One made the mountains move aside, the other found a path for the water; only my shoes never walked a clean path in either story." He looked down at his shoes; the mud had dried into a hard shell, so he tried tapping them lightly against the stone steps. The mud shell didn't come off, but the sole cracked with a small split. He quickly stopped, looked around to make sure no one was watching, and quietly added, "This incident need not be recorded in the slips."

The next day, Aman spread the green bamboo slips on the newly exposed field ridge to dry, and a mud spot happened to cover the character for "channel." A teenager nearby peeked over and laughed, "Now it reads 'change blocking to blocking.'" Aman frantically tried to wipe it off, ending up smearing a streak of mud across his own nose, yet he said with deadpan seriousness, "A character can be blocked for a moment, but a river cannot." The villagers asked if, after following Yu the Great for so long, he was finally not afraid of water. He hugged the bamboo slips tight and muttered, "I am, especially afraid of water recognizing my handwriting." Before he finished speaking, a splash of clear water from the ditch hit his hem, and everyone burst out laughing. He waited for the characters to dry, solemnly tucked the page back into the slip collection, and finally closed the green bamboo slips, adding no more words. The clear water in the field ditch continued to flow steadily forward.
