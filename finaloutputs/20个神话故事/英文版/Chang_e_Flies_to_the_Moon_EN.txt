When Hou Yi returned from Kunlun, the mountain wind still clung to his clothes.

Chang’e met him at the door and noticed first the sandalwood box cradled in his hands. Usually, Hou Yi came back with bowstrings, animal hides, or that calm, "I didn't get lost, I just took a scenic detour" look on his face. But holding a box with both hands, walking steadier than someone carrying a full basin of water? That was a first.

"A gift from the Queen Mother of the West." He opened the lid only after stepping inside. Inside lay a glass bottle containing the Elixir of Immortality. "She said that since I shot down the suns that plagued the world and saved humanity from burning, she grants this medicine as a reward. Whoever drinks it will live forever, but they must also leave the mortal realm."

Chang’e didn't reach for it right away. She asked, "Is there enough for both of us?"

Hou Yi was silent for a moment. "Only enough for one person."

The room suddenly grew so quiet you could hear a single twig crack in the stove. Immortality was a blessing everyone dreamed of, but placing one bottle between a husband and wife turned that blessing into a cold ruler: who drinks, who leaves; who stays behind, who remains.

Hou Yi pushed the bottle toward Chang’e. "I have seen ten suns in the sky. I know that living long isn't always a blessing. If you are willing—"

"You want me to go to the heavens alone, while you age here on earth?"

Hou Yi pulled the bottle back. "Then I won't take it."

Chang’e looked at him. "If you won't take it, how could I bear to take it alone?"

The two of them pushed the glass bottle back and forth, very politely, as if immortality had suddenly become the last piece of flatbread on the table that no one wanted to be rude enough to grab. Finally, Chang’e pressed a finger onto the sandalwood box. "Stop pushing. If you keep this up, we won't eat the elixir, but you'll wear a hole through the bottom of the box."

Hou Yi took a small sip of wine. A smile bloomed at the corner of his mouth, but it faded quickly, like a night-blooming cereus. He placed his cup back on the wooden table, his voice low but still warm. "When I shot down the nine suns, the world grew dark. Now the sunlight has returned, and the people can farm again." As he spoke, his gaze drifted unconsciously to the newly tilled fields outside the window. Rice seedlings swayed in the spring breeze, and children's laughter drifted in like distant bells, reminding him that his glorious days were in the past.

Chang’e leaned sideways against the sandalwood box, her fingertips lightly tracing the patterns on the lid. A soft worry passed over her eyes. She gently curled the thin branches of the osmanthus sapling she had planted in the courtyard earlier, as if telling them about the coming spring rain. "This osmanthus tree won't bloom until next year, yet I must bury its roots in this bottle." Her words carried a faint sorrow, but remained gentle. She looked up at the eaves where the lamplight flickered and whispered, "The old spiderwebs under the eaves need mending tomorrow. If I don't fix them in time, the wind and rain will spill the newly brewed wine all over the floor." She chuckled softly, almost mocking herself for worrying about such trivial matters, yet afraid to unload all these thoughts onto Hou Yi.

The shadows under the lamp danced, casting their intertwined silhouettes. Hou Yi’s fingers tapped unconsciously on the table, like beating a drum in his heart, then stopped. He reached for the silk cloth at the bottom of the box. The cloth was embroidered with ancient patterns, symbolizing the preciousness and weight of the elixir. Hou Yi reminded her in a low voice, "If this elixir falls into the hands of the greedy, the world will know no peace." His voice sounded particularly steady in the lamplight, yet carried a lingering worry.

Hearing this, Chang’e gently placed the osmanthus branch back into the soil. Her fingertips touched the damp earth, as if feeling the pulse of life. She looked up, a flash of determination mixed with unease in her eyes. "If the price of immortality is separation," she said softly, her voice like the night wind brushing over a lake, "then let it live forever in the bottle for now." With that, her fingers lightly pressed the latch of the sandalwood box, applying just the right amount of pressure—neither shaking the bottle nor making the lid creak.

Outside, a few cheerful bird calls echoed, as if accompanying the couple's conversation. Aman stood at the door, holding green bamboo slips, quietly recording every word. Occasionally, he looked up at the lamplight, a faint, unnoticed smile on his lips. In his heart, he admired Hou Yi's clumsy attempt to hide the medicine, yet felt a touch of awe at Chang’e's decisiveness. Under the light, the elixir in the box shimmered in the dim glow, like a sleeping star waiting for fate's final decision.

"If the price of immortality is separation," Chang’e said, "then let it live forever in the bottle for now."

Hou Yi placed the glass bottle back into the sandalwood box and closed the lid. He did not treat the elixir lightly because it was a gift from the Queen Mother of the West, nor did he destroy it because it posed a difficult problem. The medicine was too precious to let greedy people have; their life together was too precious to sacrifice for one person's eternal youth.

They decided not to take the medicine for now and to find a safe place to hide it. Hou Yi carried the box to the wall, but felt it was too close to the door. He put it under the bed, but worried it might get bumped during cleaning. He lifted it to the roof beam, looked up for a long time, and realized that if he stared at the beam every night while sleeping, his neck would complain long before immortality arrived.

Chang’e took the box. "The man who shot down the suns finds it hard to hide a bottle?"

"The suns were in the sky. Big targets."

"This bottle isn't small either."

"But you can't shoot it."

Chang’e finally laughed out loud. She didn't decide on a hiding spot that night; she simply kept the box by her side. Before blowing out the lamp, they confirmed once more: neither would take the elixir alone. The sandalwood box was shut tight, sealing in immortality, and also sealing in a danger that had yet to reveal itself.

The next day, Chang’e chose an inconspicuous niche in the inner room. Old balls of yarn and two chipped wooden shuttles had been sitting there. She hid the sandalwood box behind them and covered it with ordinary cloth. Hou Yi tried to spot it from the doorway but saw nothing unusual. Even up close, he just thought the pile of yarn looked suspiciously tangled.

"If I were a thief," he said, "I'd probably be deterred by the knots first."

"If you were a thief, you wouldn't announce your identity upon entering."

To preserve the true origin of the elixir, Hou Yi asked Aman to record it. Aman entered the room holding the green bamboo slips. After hearing the story of the Queen Mother's gift, his pen tip hovered in mid-air. "So, the bottle holds immortality, the couple guards the box, and it's best to have someone standing outside who has absolutely no idea where it's hidden?"

"You only record the origin," Chang’e said. "Do not look at the hiding spot. Do not touch the medicine."

The young man immediately clasped his hands behind his back, standing as if both hands had suddenly committed a crime. "I fully agree. Before, when I recorded Hou Yi's nine arrows, at least the arrows didn't call out for immortality in the middle of the night. This bottle is hidden with such solemnity that I'm nervous for it. I'm afraid it won't be able to sleep at night."

Hou Yi said, "Record the first half clearly. Keep the second half to yourself."

He carved onto the green bamboo slips: "The Queen Mother of the West gifted Hou Yi a glass bottle containing the Elixir of Immortality. The medicine grants one person eternal life and ascension to heaven. The couple has decided not to take it for now." When finished, he handed the slip to Chang’e to read aloud. Chang’e confirmed it was correct and asked him to add at the end: "Do not take it out of greed."

"Carve that sentence deeper," she said.

He carefully deepened the grooves. "Deep enough that future generations will feel their hands itching just by touching it."

Footsteps suddenly sounded outside. Peng Meng, Hou Yi's disciple, stood in the courtyard, saying he had come to retrieve a dropped arm guard. His gaze swept over the bamboo slips in the young man's hand, then toward the half-open inner room.

"Master received a reward from the Queen Mother of the West?" Peng Meng asked casually.

Hou Yi closed the bamboo slips. "Just an old matter from after saving the land from drought."

"I heard Kunlun has a magical item that grants eternal youth."

"Kunlun also has very long mountain roads," the recorder interjected. "Whether you become immortal after the trip is unknown, but your legs will definitely behave themselves."

Peng Meng smiled and asked no more. He took his arm guard and walked to the courtyard gate, then turned back. "Such a precious item, Master. You must hide it well."

Chang’e watched his retreating figure. Only when his footsteps had truly faded did she close the inner room door tightly. The earlier lightness vanished, like a lamp shadow flickering in the wind. Peng Meng had not seen the glass bottle, nor did he know about the niche. But he had heard "Queen Mother," and he had brought up "eternal youth" himself. He was not entirely without motive.

Hou Yi wanted to move the box to a new location. Chang’e said that if Peng Meng was already suspicious, moving it hastily might only draw more attention. She checked the cloth in front of the niche and placed an empty wooden box in a more obvious cabinet, to buy a few precious moments if someone broke in.

They didn’t just take “I can’t see anything wrong” as a sign to relax. Chang’e sent Hou Yi out of the house first, then had him walk back in as if he knew nothing, pretending to look around. Hou Yi pushed open the outer cabinet and spotted the empty box right away. But as he reached for it, a dangling ball of yarn wrapped around his wrist guard. The harder he tried to gently untangle it, the deeper the knot burrowed into the gaps of his armor. Finally, he just stood there with his arm raised, stuck. “If a real thief came here,” he said, “they’d probably get caught by your yarn ball before they even got started.”

Chang’e came out from the inner room and picked the knot loose in a few quick moves. “Yarn won’t catch a thief,” she said. “It just tells me if someone’s been rummaging.” She tied a few different colored threads across the cabinet door and the corners of the box, so any opening or closing would shift them. Then she sprinkled a very thin layer of dry ash on the threshold of the inner room, so any footsteps would leave a mark. Hou Yi tried it again, only to discover he’d already left two perfectly clear shoe prints the moment he walked in.

Aman sat outside the door, keeping a log of who came and went. Hearing the sound of cabinets being rummaged through inside, he couldn’t help but peek in. “Should we put something heavy back in the empty box? If a thief picks it up and gets too excited, they might throw out their back.” Chang’e shook her head. “It’s only meant to buy us a few steps, not to hurt anyone.” Aman immediately shrank back and scraped the words “throw out their back” off the corner of his bamboo slip. “Then I’ll write: The greedy one gets happy for nothing.”

Hou Yi tried the test one more time. This time, when he saw the empty box, he checked the surroundings first. The threads still revealed that the cabinet had been opened. He walked around the outer cabinet toward the inner room, and the dry ash on the threshold left his footprints behind. But if someone really determined to search the house came along, these tricks could only serve as a warning and a delay. They couldn’t forever protect the Elixir of Immortality. Hou Yi’s brow remained furrowed.

Chang’e placed the real sandalwood box back into the wall niche and covered it with ordinary cloth. “So the most important thing isn’t the traps,” she said. “It’s knowing when you can no longer stay put and guard the spot.” Hou Yi looked at her. Neither of them voiced the worst-case scenario, but they made a new promise: if someone tried to take it by force, they would protect each other first, and the elixir must never fall into the hands of evil people. This promise later became the only thing Chang’e had time to hold onto when she had to make her choice.

He asked himself if he could help keep watch from outside the house.

“You can keep a record of who comes and goes,” Hou Yi said. “But you are not to keep the Elixir of Immortality for us, nor move it, and you must not try to fight anyone for it.”

So he sat under a tree outside the courtyard, recording the day’s visitors on the back of his green bamboo slips. Before leaving, he still wanted to say something to tell the bottle not to be nervous, but seeing Chang’e’s serious expression, he swallowed the joke back down.

That night, the real glass bottle lay quietly hidden inside the sandalwood box. Hou Yi and Chang’e had found it a safe place, but Pengmeng had also found an idea. The task of hiding the elixir seemed complete, but the shadow of the theft had fallen onto the threshold for the first time.

For the next few days, Pengmeng stopped asking directly about the elixir.

He practiced archery with unusual diligence. Often, after putting away his bow, he would wander over to Hou Yi and say things that seemed unrelated. “If a person lives forever, can they keep practicing archery forever too?” Hou Yi only corrected his hand position and didn’t answer. Pengmeng then asked Chang’e why she had been keeping the inner room windows closed lately. She replied that the weather was turning cool, without even stopping her steps.

When asking didn’t work, Pengmeng started watching. He watched when Hou Yi took his students into the mountains, when Chang’e was home alone, and when the recorder occasionally came to deliver bamboo slips but never took the sandalwood box away. He gradually confirmed that the Elixir of Immortality was still in the house, but none of the people who knew were willing to point out where it was hidden.

One morning, Hou Yi was about to lead his students into the mountains to observe the migration of animal herds. Pengmeng had been drawing his heavy bow in the courtyard late into the night, but at departure time, he was clutching his abdomen and leaning against the wall, claiming he had suddenly fallen ill and even found his quiver too heavy to carry.

One student asked, “You just added a second bowl of rice at breakfast.”

“The sickness came on fast.”

“If it had come a little later, would you have added a third bowl?”

Pengmeng turned pale and couldn’t speak. Hou Yi told him to stay and rest, instructing him not to wander around. After everyone left, Pengmeng lay on the bed for a while. Once the sound of footsteps on the mountain path faded away, he sat up, his complexion recovering faster than spring grass turning green.

He didn’t go straight to Hou Yi’s house. Instead, he first circled around to the stream to make sure no one else was nearby. Then he doubled back and deliberately knocked on the main gate of the courtyard.

Chang’e was organizing her threads. She opened the door and saw Pengmeng, her gaze dropping to the short blade at his waist. “Aren’t you sick?”

“I lay down for a bit, and the sickness realized it couldn’t keep me,” Pengmeng laughed. “Master’s wife, I’ve come to pick up the bowstring Master forgot.”

Hou Yi had clearly taken his bowstring with him. Chang’e didn’t expose the lie. She just said there were no spare strings in the house and told him to go back and rest. But Pengmeng stepped over the threshold, his eyes scanning the cabinet and the inner room. “Then I’ll just ask for a cup of water.”

Chang’e blocked the path to the inner room and placed the water on the stone table in the courtyard. “Drink it, then leave.”

Pengmeng picked up the bowl but didn’t drink. He started talking about the Queen Mother of the West, his tone still gentle. He said that since Master didn’t want to enjoy the immortal medicine alone, he might as well give it to someone braver. Chang’e asked where he heard such things, and he replied that rare treasures in the world always make their own reputation.

“The bottle didn’t speak,” Chang’e said. “It was greed that spoke.”

Pengmeng finally set down the water bowl. The bottom clinked against the stone, a crisp sound, like a lie cracking open. He took a step toward the inner room, and Chang’e shifted to the side, still blocking his way.

They were only a few steps apart, and neither reached for a weapon first. Chang’e deliberately cast her gaze toward the outer cabinet, and Pengmeng followed her look. He yanked the cabinet door open, and the pre-set threads fell lightly to the ground. When the empty wooden box was revealed, his eyes lit up for a moment, then he looked at Chang’e with suspicion. “If you’re willing to let me see it, it can’t be the real medicine inside.”

“If you don’t believe me, why bother searching?” Chang’e asked.

Pengmeng claimed he was just keeping it safe for his master, but his hand was already lifting the box lid. An old ball of yarn popped out, one end catching on his wrist. Annoyed, he tore it apart, and the threads scattered on the floor. Chang’e used that brief moment to retreat toward the courtyard door, trying to slide the bolt to alert the neighbors. But Pengmeng kicked over the stone stool, its leg blocking the door, cutting off her escape route first.

Several bolts of unfinished cloth were hanging to dry in the corner of the courtyard. Chang’e tore one down and held it between them, not to fight the short blade head-on, but to block his view. Pengmeng swung his blade to cut the cloth, but the first slash only opened half of it. Chang’e had already moved to the other side, near the door. But he grabbed the end of the cloth and yanked hard. The wooden rack fell, blocking the courtyard entrance. Cloth, spools of thread, and broken wood rolled all over the floor, and the water from the bowl he hadn’t drunk splashed onto his shoes.

If Aman’s remark about “the sickness came on fast” had been heard, he might have added, “The sickness healed fast, but the shoes got wet faster.” But there was no one around to make jokes now. Chang’e saw Pengmeng’s short blade was drawn, so she no longer pinned her hopes on opening the door for help. She led him toward the outer cabinet, waiting for him to lunge for the empty box so she could use those few steps to retreat into the inner room. Each pre-arranged trick bought her just a little more time, and she needed to decide what to do with the real elixir in those fragmented moments.

When Pengmeng realized there was no glass bottle in the box, his fake smile finally vanished. He flicked the broken thread off his wrist and pressed his short blade toward Chang’e. “My patience is limited.” Chang’e’s back was against the inner room doorframe. She heard the wind blowing through the osmanthus leaves outside, but she didn’t hear Hou Yi’s footsteps returning. She knew the next delay would have to be her responsibility to bear.

“I’m just keeping it safe for Master.”

“Hou Yi never entrusted it to you.”

“Then please, Master’s wife, bring it out and explain everything face to face.”

His right hand was already gripping the short blade. Chang’e saw him close the courtyard door behind him and heard no familiar footsteps outside. She understood that delaying him with questions was no longer enough. The empty wooden box was in the outer cabinet, and the real medicine was in the niche. If Pengmeng searched the whole house, he would find it sooner or later. As she backed away, she secretly calculated the distance to the side door, only to see Pengmeng step in front of it first.

Hou Yi, still in the mountains, knew nothing of the crisis at home. The purpose behind Pengmeng’s fake illness was now fully revealed. He drew his short blade, and his voice lost its politeness. “Hand over the Elixir of Immortality.”

Chang’e gripped the inner room doorframe and didn’t answer. She had to make a choice before Pengmeng found the glass bottle, and every choice would change the life she and Hou Yi had built.

Chang’e first opened the outer cabinet and carried out the empty wooden box.

Pengmeng reached out to grab it. He flipped open the lid, and inside were only a few strands of old thread. He threw the empty box to the ground. “You think this can fool me?”

“It already made you take a few steps.”

While he was looking down, Chang’e turned and ran into the inner room. She wasn’t going to hide; she was going to the niche. If she left the medicine where it was, Pengmeng would find it only a matter of time. If she ran with the sandalwood box, the side door was blocked. If she handed it over, a person with ill intentions who gained immortality would be beyond anyone’s control forever.

She ripped the cloth aside and pulled out a sandalwood box. Peng Meng lunged forward, eyes fixed on the box, and sprang at her. Chang’e shielded the wooden box behind her, sidestepping the loom. He grabbed the box’s corner, and the two of them locked in a tense stand‑off in the cramped room. The weaving thread slipped, coiling around Peng Meng’s wrist. He slashed it away, and the box lid popped open as they tugged.

The crystal bottle rolled to the edge of the mat, clear as a single drop of frozen moonlight.

Peng Meng let go of the box to snatch the bottle. Chang’e was quicker, bending low to seize it and retreating to the corner. The cork lay right under her fingers, while the door stood behind Peng Meng. She called out Hou Yi’s name, but outside only the wind rustled the osmanthus seedlings.

“Give it to me,” Peng Meng raised his short blade. “You can’t guard it.”

Chang’e stared at the crystal bottle in her hands. Hou Yi had once said under the lamp that no one should take the elixir alone, and she’d promised to stay by his side in the mortal world. If she swallowed it now, their promise would break and she’d be forced to leave; if she didn’t, Peng Meng’s next move would be to snatch the medicine, and what they were protecting was more than just a bottle between a husband and wife.

She didn’t pop the cork right away; instead she slipped the bottle along the wall. The loom blocked the middle of the room, its shuttle and broken threads scattered everywhere. As Peng Meng leapt over the frame, his shoe caught a rolling spool, wobbling his balance. Seizing the chance, Chang’e hooked the loom’s crossbeam with her foot and tipped the whole thing sideways. The heavy wooden frame missed him, but forced him to sheath his blade and bought her a couple of extra steps.

Two steps later they were still up against the wall. The window was too narrow to safely toss the bottle out. Chang’e shouted toward the courtyard; only the wind slamming into the door answered her. Peng Meng pushed the loom aside, pressing his left hand to the fresh red cut on his wrist while his short blade steadied in his right hand. “You won’t wait for Hou Yi,” he snarled.

Chang’e lowered her head and saw the medicine’s light flicker inside the bottle. That light cared nothing about good or evil; it simply granted immortality to whoever swallowed it. If Peng Meng got it, his short blade and greed would stretch across endless ages; if she drank it, at least no one could ever take the elixir from her, though its power would also pull her away from Hou Yi. She finally understood the choice wasn’t between “keeping a promise” or “breaking it,” but about which kind of loss she would have to bear herself.

Peng Meng lunged again. Chang’e first hurled the sandalwood box to the other side. The box hit the wall and bounced; Peng Meng instinctively turned his head, his blade veering for a heartbeat. In that instant she pried the cork open and gulped the entire dose of the immortality elixir, leaving no room for anyone else to fight over it. As the bottle’s rim left her lips, her first thought wasn’t about becoming immortal—it was to steady the loom, the last thing in the house she and Hou Yi had ever touched.

The medicine surged from her chest, but the mat beneath her feet gave a light, floating sensation first. She clutched the loom’s crossbeam; a corner of the wooden frame lifted, then slipped back down under its weight. The spools rolled across the floor, and the broken threads slipped from her wrist one by one. Peng Meng tried to grab her hem, but his fingers met only a sudden gust of wind.

As she leapt over the windowsill, she saw the osmanthus seedlings in the courtyard bent by the airflow, and she spotted the path leading to the Western Mountains beyond the gate. She knew Hou Yi would return that way, so she stretched desperately toward it, even as the medicine’s power lifted her toward the moonlight. There was no triumphant joy, no time to console herself with immortality; all she could do was keep shouting Hou Yi’s name as the roof seemed to sink and the mortal world slipped farther away.

The courtyard gate finally burst open with a gust of wind, scattering the dry ash that had been on the threshold into a perfect circle, clearly showing Peng Meng’s footprints. The water‑filled clay bowl still spun under the stone table, the empty wooden box and the broken threads pointed straight toward the inner room. Chang’e memorized every detail, hoping that when Hou Yi returned he could see through the chaos that she hadn’t taken the elixir for herself, but had blocked a robbery when there was no way out.

She asked, “What will you do once you have immortality?”

“No one will ever be able to press down on my head again.”

Those words erased the last hint of hesitation. Chang’e didn’t smash the bottle, nor did she wait for it to break on its own. She pried the cork open, saw the elixir clearly, and the moment Peng Meng charged, she tipped her head back and poured the entire dose into her mouth, swallowing it herself.

The medicine tasted sharply bitter for a split second. She swallowed, and the empty bottle slipped from her fingers onto the mat.

Peng Meng surged forward like a starving wolf, his eyes fixed only on the prize. His fingertips were about to brush the crystal bottle when, at the very moment his finger reached the rim, Chang’e’s hand gently clasped the bottle as if an invisible grip had seized it.

“Wait!” Chang’e’s voice trembled slightly but stayed calm. Her fingers twitched, and the bottle rang a clear, tinkling chime in her palm, as if reminding everyone how precious that elixir was.

Unwilling to give up, Peng Meng jerked the bottle forward, trying to pour out its contents. He tipped the bottle upside down, expecting a burst of golden light or mist, but only heard a hollow echo from inside—nothing fell out.

He furrowed his brow, froze, then stretched his hand even farther, demanding, “What exactly are you hiding?” His voice trembled with anxiety and anger, as if he wanted to shatter the whole room.

At that moment Chang’e’s sleeve fluttered lightly, as if an unseen wind brushed past her cuff, though no dust rose. Her feet seemed lifted by an invisible force, her toes barely leaving the ground, as if cradled by a soft cloud.

Peng Meng’s mouth dropped open in surprise; the bottle in his hand shook on its own, and the crystal rim flashed tiny specks of light in the lamp glow, like stars trembling in the night sky.

“How…?” he stammered, his voice shaking with disbelief.

Determination flashed in Chang’e’s eyes. She rose slowly in the air, her sleeves billowing like a white crane spreading its wings. A faint sorrow lingered on her face, but not a hint of hesitation.

Peng Meng finally lost his balance; the bottle slipped from his grasp and crashed to the floor with a crisp shatter. The fragments scattered in the lamplight like bursts of starlight.

“You drank it?” Peng Meng’s voice echoed through the empty room, a mix of astonishment and terror.

By now Chang’e was no longer a mortal woman; her form blurred in mid‑air and turned into a soft, glowing light that drifted slowly toward the high, bright moon. Each step she took carried heavy longing and protection, leaving only a silent echo behind for Hou Yi and Peng Meng.

“You drank it?” Peng Meng could hardly believe it.

“You’ll never get it back.”

He tried to grab Chang’e, but she slipped behind the loom. She herself was startled by the transformation, first pressing against the table edge, her body growing lighter and lighter. The skein on the table rolled off her sleeve; the long thread that had once tangled her now dangled toward the floor, like the first thin rope the mortal world was trying to use to hold her down.

Seeing the elixir gone, Peng Meng turned, picked up his short blade, and, not daring to wait for Hou Yi’s return, kicked open the side door and fled. The courtyard was left with the overturned water bowl, the broken empty box, and a crystal bottle that could no longer hold immortality.

Chang’e clutched the table edge, her knuckles white from the strain. She wasn’t doing this to wander the moon, nor to hoard immortality, nor by accident in a farce. She had made the choice herself between handing over the medicine and swallowing it, keeping the elixir out of evil hands while immediately paying the price of that decision.

Her toes left the mat. Swallowing the elixir was now an irreversible fact, and the night sky above the roof beams was slowly opening up to her.

Chang’e’s hands gripped the table edge, and her body kept rising.

The table legs left short scratches on the floor before they could no longer resist the upward pull. She let go of one hand, reaching for a column; her sash and long hair floated upward. Everything inside the house stayed where it was: the half‑finished cloth, the pot of warm water by the stove, Hou Yi’s old wrist guard hanging back on the wall from that morning. The more ordinary it was, the less she wanted to leave.

“Hou Yi!” she shouted toward the door.

The mountain path gave no answer. Chang’e looped her sash around a column, trying to tie herself down. The sash tightened; she hovered in mid‑air for a heartbeat, then the knot slipped open under the upward force. She reached for the dangling weaving thread; the fine line slipped through her fingers, leaving only a faint sensation.

She rose beneath the beam, slipped sideways through the door Peng Meng had smashed, and stepped out into the courtyard. The osmanthus seedlings were knee‑high, their leaves rustling in the night wind. Chang’e crouched, gripping a tree trunk. The young tree bent with her, the soil beside its roots loosening. If she let go now, the only osmanthus seedlings they had planted together would be pulled up as well.

Chang’e slowly let go. The branch snapped back, but she had already cleared the eaves.

She watched Peng Meng flee into the shadows and didn't chase him, nor did she let him use her as a ladder to climb higher. Instead, she called out for Hou Yi toward the mountain path, over and over. Her voice bounced off the roofs and trees, startling the sleeping birds, but it never reached the deep mountains.

The house shrank beneath her feet. She remembered their promise by the lamp, and Hou Yi’s hand pushing the glass bottle toward her, then pulling it back. She had protected the medicine, but she had broken their vow not to take it alone. Yet, if she could do it again, with a short blade already at the bottle’s rim, she still wouldn’t hand immortality over to Peng Meng.

The moon rose in the east, its clear light piercing through thin clouds. The power of the elixir lifted her toward that bright orb. Chang’e didn't choose a direction; the moon simply seemed like the only place in the sky big enough to hold an immortal body. She soared past the treetops and the dark outlines of distant mountains, officially beginning her flight to the moon.

The wind grew cold at this height. She tried to swim downward, but her sleeves only flapped uselessly in the air; she reached out to grab a cloud, but it dissolved through her fingers. The smells of cooking smoke, barking dogs, and plants from the human world faded away, leaving only the sound of the wind in her ears.

Chang’e pressed her hand to her chest. There was no sandalwood box there, no glass bottle—only the surging power of the medicine and her rapid heartbeat. She forced herself to remember the layout of the house below: the cassia sapling by the east door, the old well outside the wall, and the western mountain path where Hou Yi always returned. She feared that if she rose any higher, the lights would blur into one, and she’d never be able to find her home again.

A line of firelight suddenly moved in the distant mountains. It might have been the returning hunting party, or just torches in the woods. Chang’e shouted toward it with all her strength, but the wind carried her voice higher, away from them.

The moon grew closer, the human world farther away. She could no longer return to the house, pick up the empty bottle to explain to Hou Yi, or finish the goodbye she’d left hanging. Flying to the moon wasn’t a light, happy flight; with every step upward, she lost something that had once been within reach.

The last thing she saw was the cassia sapling in the courtyard, bent by her weight, slowly straightening up. Then the clouds closed in, hiding the roof. Chang’e passed through the moonlight, carrying her choice to protect the medicine and the regret of not saying goodbye face-to-face, and continued her ascent.

When Hou Yi returned to the courtyard, the first thing he saw was the door hanging crookedly on its hinges.

He dropped his game and rushed inside. The empty wooden box lay shattered on the floor, threads scattered everywhere, and the glass bottle, now devoid of the elixir, lay by the mat. Chang’e was gone, and Peng Meng had vanished too. Hou Yi picked up the empty bottle, his knuckles turning white, but he didn’t crush it.

A disciple found Peng Meng’s footprints by the side door. Aman hurried over, still clutching the green bamboo slips that recorded the medicine’s history. He walked fast, but tripped over a stone at the threshold, making a loud *thud-thud* sound, like a drum. Aman looked up, frowning, and muttered, “This stone is harder than the slips I’m carrying.” He gently placed the slips on the threshold, his finger unconsciously tracing a small circle on the cover, as if marking a spot for himself.

Inside, Hou Yi was examining the empty bottle. A few shards of glass still clung to the rim, glinting like blood. Suddenly, he heard faint footsteps outside. The prints in the dust were clear—Peng Meng’s. Hou Yi’s brows furrowed, anxiety flashing in his eyes, but he kept his posture steady.

Aman stood at the side door, watching intently. He flipped the green bamboo slips to the page describing the elixir’s origin, his fingertip brushing the ancient text: *“This medicine was granted by the Heavenly Emperor. It grants freedom from death, but if it is lost, heaven and earth will mourn.”* He whispered, “Heaven and earth will mourn,” his voice trembling slightly, but he kept it low, afraid to disturb the man inside.

Hou Yi raised his hand and gently set the empty bottle on the ground. It hit the stone slab with a crisp *clink*. The sound echoed in the silent room, a reminder of how little time they had. Aman couldn’t help but laugh. “Teacher Hou, this bottle is noisier than my family’s pot!” His words were lightly sarcastic, but they eased the tension just a bit.

Hou Yi didn’t respond. He turned to the hidden compartment behind the house, his fingers searching the gaps in the wood. Behind a thick plank lay the secret spot he’d prepared for the elixir. Just as he was about to push the plank aside, he heard rapid breathing outside—someone holding their breath, waiting for the right moment.

Aman stood at the door, watching Hou Yi’s every move. The green bamboo slips trembled in his hand, pages fluttering slightly in the breeze. He murmured to himself, “If this medicine falls into the wrong hands, the world will fall into chaos.” His words carried deep worry, but he maintained the calm demeanor of a recorder.

Hou Yi finally pushed the plank aside, revealing a small hidden space. Where the elixir pouch should have been, there was only a thin layer of dust. Hou Yi frowned, brushing the dust away to reveal a tiny golden seal. Ancient runes were carved into it, glowing faintly, a reminder of the medicine’s value and its cost.

Just then, a disciple outside the side door cried out, “Master! Peng Meng’s footprints lead inward, like a dark tide approaching!” His voice was urgent, and his footsteps quickened. Aman looked up, panic flashing in his eyes, but he still held the green bamboo slips tight against his chest, as if trying to guard every word written on them.

Hou Yi took a deep breath, his gaze shifting to the dark corners outside, as if searching for the impending crisis. His fingers lightly touched the golden seal, and a sensation passed through his fingertips...

A muffled shout came from high above outside.

Hou Yi looked up. In the moonlight, a familiar figure was soaring past the edge of the clouds, sleeves billowing in the wind. He burst out of the courtyard and ran in the direction Chang’e had ascended. Rocks and tree shadows rushed past him, but the figure in the sky didn’t get any closer.

“Chang’e!”

She seemed to turn her head. Her voice fell from the vast sky, too distant to make out full sentences. Hou Yi could only pick out the words “Peng Meng” and “elixir” from the scattered syllables. Looking at the empty bottle in the house, he understood what had happened.

A disciple caught up, asking if he should get the bow. Hou Yi’s hand touched the bow, but he stopped. That bow had shot down the suns, able to pin distant targets with an arrow; but the person in the sky was Chang’e. An arrow couldn’t bring her back; it would only wound someone who was already forced to leave.

He placed the bow on the ground and continued chasing on foot. When Chang’e rose over a mountain, he crossed the ridge; when she entered a layer of clouds, he called out from beneath the cloud shadows. Finally, his feet reached the edge of a high cliff, with only the night ahead. He had to stop.

He followed behind, panting so hard he could barely hold his pen. In the past, he might have joked that he’d seen the hottest slip when shooting the suns, and now he was seeing the coldest slip of moonlight; but Chang’e’s figure was fading away, and any witty remark would feel too loud.

He knelt on the stone edge and laid the green bamboo slips flat. First, he carved: *Peng Meng faked illness, returned, and stole the medicine with a blade.* Then, he carved: *Chang’e swallowed the elixir herself to keep it from evil hands, and flew to the moon.* The carving knife moved lightly, as if pressing harder would push the shrinking figure in the sky even farther away.

Hou Yi looked up at the moon. Chang’e was nearing the brightest part of the moonlight. When she looked back, she could only see clusters of tiny fires from the human world. Both were searching for each other, separated by a distance that could never be crossed.

“She didn’t leave me behind,” Hou Yi whispered.

The recorder offered no comfort, nor did he explain more on Chang’e’s behalf. He simply recorded those words exactly as they were. Hou Yi asked about Peng Meng’s whereabouts, and the disciple said the footprints had vanished into the dense forest. That was an evil act to be pursued later, but it couldn’t change the separation that had already happened tonight.

Chang’e’s figure finally disappeared into the moon. Hou Yi stood by the cliff for a long time, until the dew on the ground soaked his shoes. He didn’t shoot an arrow, nor did he order anyone to call out in vain. He simply memorized the last place Chang’e had appeared.

On the way back, he closed the green bamboo slips. In the past, when recording Hou Yi “shooting nine and leaving one,” it was about power stopping before the final arrow. Tonight, Hou Yi also put down his bow, because restraint isn’t always about keeping the sun; sometimes, it’s about not letting longing become another form of harm.

The moon lit the way home, and also lit the direction Chang’e had reached. The separation of husband and wife was no longer just a rumor: Hou Yi stopped on the earth, Chang’e vanished in the sky, and the young witnesses saw with their own eyes how their two paths were now divided.

Chang’e passed through the last layer of moonlight, and finally, solid ground appeared beneath her feet.

She landed on a patch of silent earth. There were no insects chirping or cooking fires smoking like back home, only a pale, white light stretching out into the distance. She turned to look for the way she came, but the sea of clouds had already closed up, and her footprints didn't stretch back up into the sky. The moon had caught her, but it hadn't given her a way back.

Ahead stood a palace, with the characters "Guanghan Palace" written above the gate. Chang’e pushed the door open and stepped inside. The hall was vast and empty; her footsteps echoed down the long corridor, sounding like someone else was following her from afar. When she stopped, the echo stopped too. There were no servants to greet her, no banquets to celebrate, and certainly no one to tell her that guarding the elixir of immortality would come without a price.

She walked through several cold, quiet rooms and finally stopped at a window facing the human world. Outside, the shadows of an osmanthus tree swayed gently, even though there was no wind. The tree was much taller than the sapling in her courtyard, but it reminded her of Hou Yi bending down to pack soil around its roots, accidentally splashing water all over his own shoes. She had laughed for a long time then. Now, that laughter was something she could only pull out from her memories.

Chang’e reached into her sleeve. Aside from a small piece of weaving thread that had gotten tangled around her wrist during her ascent, she had nothing else. She placed the thread on the windowsill. It was too short to connect back to the human world, but it proved that before tonight, she had still been living a life in front of a loom with unfinished cloth.

Looking down from the Guanghan Palace, the human world was just a distant, dark expanse. The lights were so tiny she couldn't tell which one belonged to her own courtyard. Chang’e searched along the window, spot by spot. When she saw the shadow of a mountain range, she wondered if Hou Yi had already crossed the ridge. When she saw a faint point of fire, she thought, maybe that’s the lamp he lit when he got home.

She wanted to tell Hou Yi exactly how Peng Meng had tried to steal the medicine. She wanted to tell him she remembered his promise under the lamp, and that she knew swallowing the elixir alone would cause him pain. But when the short blade was pressed against her, she had to choose between two losses: losing their life together, or letting a villain gain immortality. She chose to prevent the latter, but she was forever left behind in the Guanghan Palace by the former.

The moon palace had no day or night noise; time seemed to freeze along with the light. Chang’e didn't need food and wouldn't age; the elixir had delivered on its power. But not aging didn't mean not missing someone. Every small thing in the human world that would wear out, break, or end became even clearer because of this: the stove fire would go out, the cloth would be finished, the osmanthus sapling would grow tall, and Hou Yi would change with the years.

She walked out of the palace gate and wandered along the moon's surface for a while, trying to find a way down from another angle. Ahead was still silver-white desolation; behind her were only her own footprints. She finally returned to the Guanghan Palace, because this moon palace was the only place that could hold her, even though it wasn't a home she had chosen for herself.

Chang’e sat down by the window, facing the human world. The distant shadow of the osmanthus tree fell on the ground, like a hand that could never quite reach down. She held that piece of weaving thread in her palm, waiting for the moon to rise again over the houses of the human world.

From that night on, Chang’e lived in the Guanghan Palace. The moon palace held her up, and a long, lonely residence began. The elixir of immortality she had guarded disappeared into her body, but her separation from Hou Yi remained, day after day.

The moon palace now had someone looking longingly at the human world, and because of that longing, it seemed even colder. Chang’e said nothing under the osmanthus shadows. She only cast her gaze toward the distant lights, trying to recognize the one lamp that Hou Yi kept burning among the countless fires of the human world.

When Hou Yi returned home, he didn't immediately tidy up the scattered weaving threads.

He placed the empty glass bottle back into the sandalwood box, then carefully untangled the threads that Peng Meng had cut. Chang’e's unfinished cloth still hung on the loom at the same shuttle, as if the owner had just temporarily stood up. The osmanthus sapling in the courtyard had been bent over, but its branches had straightened up again, with dew still clinging to the leaf tips from when she ascended.

When the moon rose above the courtyard wall, Hou Yi brought out a small table. He placed on it the cakes and fruits Chang’e loved, and poured a bowl of clear water. The offering table couldn't cross the vast sky, and he knew Chang’e in the Guanghan Palace probably didn't need food or drink. But this was what the human world could still do for her.

"I fixed the door today," Hou Yi said to the moon. "The osmanthus sapling isn't hurt. I didn't dare to continue weaving that cloth; I was afraid that when you came back, you'd blame Peng Meng first, and then blame me for mixing up the warp and weft."

His voice lingered in the courtyard. Inside the moon's glow, Chang’e stood by the window of the Guanghan Palace. She couldn't see what was on the table, but she recognized the direction of her courtyard, and she saw that cluster of lights that burned longer than anywhere else. She pressed her hand against the cold window, as if responding through the moonlight.

From then on, whenever the moon was bright, Hou Yi would tell her about recent events in the human world. In spring, the osmanthus sapling grew new leaves; in summer, the old well water remained clear; in autumn, new grain was stored in the barn; in winter, frost formed under the eaves. None of these were earth-shattering events. These were the small things that had once made up the life they had chosen not to give up.

Aman sat outside the courtyard gate. He wasn't nervous about the bottle like in the first half, nor did he make jokes about Hou Yi's poor hiding skills. He turned to a new page in the green bamboo slips and checked the whole incident from the beginning: The Queen Mother of the West bestowed the elixir of immortality; the couple hid it because they didn't want to be separated; Peng Meng faked an illness to steal it; Chang’e swallowed it herself to protect it; she fled to the moon and lived in the moon palace, while Hou Yi remained in the human world.

On the last line, he wrote very lightly: "Gazing at the same moon, sharing the same longing, yet unable to meet."

The carving knife paused after the word "meet." Aman remembered when Hou Yi shot down the suns, the sunlight was so hot it burned his hands on the bamboo slips. Now, the moonlight fell on the same volume of green bamboo slips, so cold it made the tip of his pen feel stiff. Between these two records lay many stories, but for Hou Yi, it was just a journey from a choice that left the sun for the human world, to a night where he couldn't keep Chang’e.

Later, when people learned the reason for Chang’e's flight to the moon, they also set out cakes and fruits during the full moon, gazing at the figure in the moon. Some missed distant relatives; others hoped for the return of those who had scattered. The offering tables were all different, but they all looked up at the same moon.

In the Guanghan Palace, Chang’e still kept watch beside the osmanthus shadows. She didn't forget the human world because of her immortality, nor did she find a replacement in the lively fairy realm. Hou Yi would grow old, the osmanthus tree in the courtyard would grow tall, and the green bamboo slips would be passed down to future generations. Only she remained in the moon palace, preserving the cost of that choice for a long time.

A night breeze swept by, and the lamp on the table flickered slightly. When Hou Yi looked up, he felt that the figure in the moon seemed to lean closer to the human world for an instant. He didn't say that Chang’e had returned. He just left a piece of cake in its place and continued telling the rest of the day's unfinished story.

The moon shone on the human world, and it shone on the Guanghan Palace. It couldn't bridge the gap between the two places, but it let Hou Yi and Chang’e know, every time they looked up, that the other was also under the same clear light. Thus, the moon was no longer just a bright wheel in the sky; it also carried the longing and loneliness of a love that couldn't be together, yet never stopped watching each other.
