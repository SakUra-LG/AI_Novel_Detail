Before the sudden rain fell, the tribe had already been arguing for half the day.

The net-weaver by the river said the swallows were flying low, which meant rain was certain, so they should pull in the nets immediately. The man drying grain looked up at the sky, saw light peeking through the cracks in the clouds, and declared it wouldn't rain today. The fire-keeper, meanwhile, said his leg ached yesterday, it didn't rain yesterday, and now his leg ached again, so it was probably just his leg being dramatic.

Fuxi didn't shout louder for either side. He first watched how the clouds in the sky gathered together, then looked at the undersides of grass blades flipping in the wind, and listened to see which direction the distant thunder was heading. The swallows were indeed flying low, but a colony of ants was still busy moving food; the surface of the river rippled eastward, yet the reeds on the bank bent westward. Each sign told a little part of the story, but no single sign told the whole truth of heaven and earth.

"So, do we pack up or not?" asked the net-weaver.

"Move the grain that fears rain to somewhere with a roof, and leave half the net out to keep watching," Fuxi replied.

The grain-drier was unconvinced: "Is that sunny or rainy?"

"It's 'if you wait for just one word, the grain will get soaked before you decide.'"

Just as everyone had moved the grain baskets into the shed, the rain poured over the ridge. The half of the net left by the river was pushed off course by the current, but the weaver pulled it back in time; if they had left the whole net, it probably would have floated down to the neighboring valley to fish for them. As for what it caught, they would have to wait for the neighbors to come and say thank you.

After the rain stopped, the arguing didn't end. The man who pointed out the low-flying swallows said he was completely right. The grain-drier pointed to the sun reappearing and said he was only wrong for the first half of the day. The fire-keeper found his leg didn't hurt anymore and solemnly announced that this rain was highly effective at curing ailments.

Fuxi wrote down each of their statements separately, then continued to observe heaven and earth. He watched sunrises and sunsets, cold nights and warm days, the sprouting and withering of plants, and the rising and receding of river waters. Daylight was bright and outward-facing, while night was quiet and hidden; wind made branches move, while mountains made paths stop; heat melted ice, while cold froze water. Opposite things were not forever separated; when day ended, night came; when water rose high, it would recede; and when stillness reached its extreme, movement would begin.

He also placed the same stone in two different spots: one in the sun and one in the shade of a tree. In the afternoon, the stone in the sun was hot to the touch, while the one in the shade remained cool; after nightfall, both stones cooled down together. The stone hadn't changed its name, but its exposure to light and dark, heat and cold, was changing. Fuxi therefore reminded everyone not to mistake what they saw at a single moment for the eternal nature of all things.

Usually, each tribe member stuck to their own experience, like each person only touching one corner of heaven and earth. Some saw clouds and said "rain," others saw light and said "sunny," but no one asked how the clouds and wind changed or what came first. Fuxi had them pair their judgments with the actual results. When the wind rose the second time, everyone noticed the swallows weren't flying low, but the ants were marching up the hill in groups; soon the river swelled, rain fell upstream, but the sky above them remained bright. If you only stared at the sky above your head, you wouldn't see the water coming from elsewhere.

Therefore, Fuxi stopped looking for a single sign that was always right. He observed states that came in connected pairs: up and down, light and dark, cold and warm, movement and stillness, advance and retreat. Although all things in heaven and earth seemed chaotic, changes often happened between two ends; when one end strengthened, the other yielded, and when one end reached its limit, it might turn toward the other.

The sudden rain didn't give him a ready-made answer, but it left a bigger question: Could he use the simplest shapes to capture these changes of opposites giving birth to each other, so people wouldn't have to start every time with an argument to understand heaven and earth?

After the rain, Fuxi walked along the river to check the water flow.

The river surface had been muddy, but when he reached a wide bay, the ripples suddenly parted to the sides. A divine beast, shaped like a dragon but with the body of a horse, stepped out of the waves, its black-and-white spotted pattern rising and falling with the shimmer of its scales. The people on the shore forgot to step back for a moment until the Dragon-Horse snorted loudly; the person closest hugged their grain basket tightly. He didn't know if Dragon-Horses ate grain, but protecting dinner seemed like a wise precaution.

The Dragon-Horse didn't speak, nor did it hand over a carved answer. It simply stopped in the shallow water, letting the pattern on its back show fully. White dots and black dots were either centered or arranged in the four directions, their numbers and positions echoing each other. Fuxi approached the riverbank, observing from different angles, refusing to take his first guess as the final conclusion.

Some said it was the Dragon-Horse showing its owner where it lived after getting lost. Another said it looked like a grain distribution plan: the black dots were his, and the white dots could be negotiated. A shepherd boy was even bolder, claiming it was just hoof prints on the horse, because the Dragon-Horse had accidentally stepped on itself.

"If it can step on itself like that," Fuxi said, "we should definitely learn its leg techniques first."

After the laughter died down, he put aside everyone's guesses and looked only at the relationships between the dots. The grouped shapes in the center looked like a fundamental root, while the black and white dots in the four directions each had their own increases and decreases; the adjacent spots weren't unrelated, but rather like a river turning from one bank to another. Black and white didn't swallow each other; instead, because they faced each other, they revealed an order.

Fuxi remembered his observations from the past few days: day and night alternating, cold and warm waxing and waning, light and dark on the water surface, movement and stillness in the river. If the white dots only represented some white object and the black dots only represented a black stone, the River Map would have nothing to do with heaven and earth; but if they represented two types of opposing forces, their directions and changes could align with the transformations of all things.

He traced a pattern of dots on the damp sand with a branch, then had people look at it from upstream, downstream, and the opposite bank. When the viewpoint changed, the surface seemed different, but the harmony between the center and the four directions stayed the same. Fuxi compared the River Diagram with the shifting sun and the rise and fall of the water, step by step: the sun rises on one side and sets on the other; water gathers in low places and then recedes with the seasons. The world isn’t a chaotic pile—it holds itself together through opposites, cycles, and transformation.

The dragon‑horse halted at the riverbank as the evening light slanted down, then turned and stepped into the water. Everyone rushed to ask Fuxi if he’d remembered the diagram. Some even wanted to wade in and fetch the dragon‑horse back, saying they’d been too busy guarding the valley and hadn’t seen which dark spot went where.

Fuxi stopped them. He had already sketched the River Diagram on the shore, and more importantly he understood that the diagram wasn’t a finished bagua nor a magic key to the world. It merely displayed the correspondence of black and white, directions and numbers, reminding the observer that seemingly opposite forces can depend on each other, and that change follows a traceable order.

The river quickly washed away the dragon‑horse’s footprints, but it left the dot pattern on the sand untouched. As night fell, Fuxi sat down and matched each line of the pattern with what he’d seen before. From those observations he inferred: if everyone is to grasp this relative change, they’ll need symbols even simpler than grouped dots—signs that can be recombined over and over.

Fuxi first tried drawing the sun, the moon, flowing water, and rocks.

The more he drew, the bigger the problem grew. The sun can be bright, rainy, or half‑hidden behind clouds; the moon waxes and wanes; a river can be a lazy ribbon or a raging beast. If he made a separate symbol for every single look, the symbols would become as cluttered as the world itself, and people would still miss the common thread of change.

He stripped away the concrete shapes, leaving only two lines. One ran continuously from head to tail, representing outward, bright, moving, and growing; the other was broken in the middle, representing inward, dim, still, and hidden. The whole line he called the Yang line, the broken one the Yin line.

Someone staring at the broken line asked, “Did you get tired halfway through drawing it?”

Fuxi replied, “If I were tired, the empty space would be lying down, not drawing.”

He didn’t prove yin and yang with a joke; he took the two lines back into the world to test them. At the first break of dawn, when the morning light just sliced through the night’s curtain, Fuxi stood on a mountaintop, gently brushing the two fresh thin lines—one whole long line for Yang, one short broken line for Yin. Aman followed close behind, carrying an old bamboo basket filled with last night’s collected dew and a few freshly snapped willow branches, ready to record every move Fuxi made.

Fuxi looked eastward, where the sun’s rays unfurled like golden silk across the sky. He lifted the whole long line high, its tip pointing at the rising sun, and said in a deep, resonant voice, “When the sun rises, Yang stretches out, revealing all things; the line’s unbroken flow matches that.” Aman couldn’t help laughing, “Big brother, are you trying to pull the sun down with that line?” Fuxi gave him no direct look, just a slight nod, signaling Aman to pour the basket’s dew onto the long line. The dew vaporized instantly in the sunlight, a fine mist forming a gentle haze that highlighted the line’s shine, as if Yang’s breath were flowing through the air.

Later, night fell and the moon poured down like liquid silver. Fuxi placed the broken short line on the ground, its tip pointing toward a distant valley. Aman leaned in, whispering, “The line’s broken—did you draw it wrong?” Fuxi smiled calmly, “When night comes, plants withdraw; the broken line expresses that hiddenness.” He traced the break with his fingertip; a breeze stirred, and dry leaves in the valley trembled, as if answering the “break.” Aman clapped, delighted, “So you let the wind help explain the ‘break’!”

Fuxi kept watching the river. In the heart of the current, water roared and splashed like a charging dragon. Pointing at the rapid, he said, “The fierce middle flow can be seen as Yang.” He tossed the whole long line gently into the water; it rode the waves yet stayed straight, embodying Yang’s steadfast vigor. Aman, feeling mischievous, handed Fuxi a willow twig, “How about using this as a ‘Yang’ flag?” Fuxi smiled without a word, slipped the twig onto the line, and let it sway with the current, casting a green flash that looked just like Yang’s lively energy.

Then he turned to a calm bend on the riverbank, where the water lay smooth as a mirror, reflecting the stars. “The quiet bend is softer, so it’s Yin,” Fuxi said. He set the broken short line lightly on a stone by the shore, its tip dipping into the water; the line’s gentle touch sent tiny ripples across the surface, soft and serene. Aman leaned close, amazed, “It looks like the line is taking a nap in the water!” Fuxi nodded, “Yin is gentle like water, yet it has its own force.”

But Fuxi didn't stop there. He looked up at the rock face above the rapids, where the shadows fell on the water, creating a pattern of light and dark. Fuxi explained in a low voice, "Even the rapids have shadows, and still water can be blown by the wind, which means yin and yang aren't permanent labels for all things." He crossed the long and short lines, forming a simple Eight Trigrams pattern, with the intersections of the lines matching the meeting points of the shadows and light. Aman couldn't help but burst out laughing, "Big brother, are you playing a 'game of light and shadow'?" Fuxi didn't respond, but tilted the bamboo basket in his hand, letting the remaining dew drop onto the intersections, where the droplets sparkled in the interplay of light and shadow, like the breath of the universe.

At this moment, the tribe gathered around, their expressions changing from confusion to surprise to sudden understanding. Someone pointed at the long line, saying, "This line is like the pulse of the sun, beating every day." An elderly man touched the short line, murmuring, "This broken line is like the gate of night, opening to dreams." Aman recorded this, his pen stopping occasionally as he chuckled.

Fuxi placed a bowl of water in the sunlight. As the water heated up and evaporated, it showed the transformation of yin to yang; when it cooled down at night and condensed, it showed the retreat of yang and the growth of yin. The tribe began to understand that a continuous line didn't equal all solid objects, and a broken line didn't equal all soft objects. They represented how things acted and changed at a given moment.

To test this, Fuxi asked two groups to observe the same field. One group recorded the dew and gentle breeze at dawn, while the other group recorded the sunlight and the stretching of the crops at noon. If they only asked whether the field was yin or yang, the two groups would argue until the next planting season; but if they described the time and changes, both judgments could be correct and connected as a process from yin to yang.

He then handed the continuous and broken lines to people who hadn't heard the explanation and asked them to choose between them at different stages: when the stove fire was burning, when it had just gone out, and when the ashes were cold. At first, some people thought "fire" was always yang, but later discovered that even after the fire was extinguished, the ashes still retained heat. Only by explaining which force was currently at work could the lines avoid becoming new, rigid symbols.

The person tending the fire used two sticks to illustrate his point, "So, was my leg pain yesterday yin or yang?"

"Pain is the result," Fuxi said, "First, explain when and why you were in pain."

"It hurt before the rain."

"Then go fix the roof, don't let yin and yang take the blame for your leg getting rained on."

Everyone burst out laughing, but also remembered: yin and yang aren't a universal answer to avoid observation. The simpler the symbol, the more it needs to be explained in the context of the real world.

Fuxi compared the black and white dots of the River Chart with the two lines. The relative positions of black and white, the increase and decrease of odd and even, and the resonance between the center and the four directions could all be reinterpreted through the relationship of yin and yang. The River Chart provided him with corresponding clues, and the world provided repeated verification; the yang and yin lines compressed these experiences into a basic, comparable form that anyone could draw.

Even with two lines, they could only express one layer of change. In the sky, there was wind and thunder; on the earth, there were mountains and swamps; and when water and fire met, new results were produced. Fuxi placed the yang and yin lines side by side, preparing to combine them to see if the same forces could express a more complex world through their positions and sequence.

Fuxi layered the lines on top of each other.

With only one line, you could see the two ends of yin and yang; with two layers, you could see different relationships like yin within yang and yang within yin; and with three layers, the positions of the lines could distinguish even more natural forces. Fuxi stipulated that they should be read from bottom to top, like a tree growing from its roots or a river flowing from its source, to avoid someone reading them upside down or sideways and mistaking a divination symbol for a row of clotheslines.

He first chose the most obvious example of heaven and earth to verify this. Three continuous yang lines represented the unending movement of heaven; three broken yin lines represented the earth's capacity to nurture all things. The tribe quickly distinguished between the two, but someone misunderstood "above" as "looking at the top first" and reversed the order.

Fuxi asked him to build a small earth platform. If he started from the top, the first scoop of earth would have nowhere to go, and he would have to hold it in his hands until the bottom was built. As everyone watched him stand there holding the earth, they finally agreed: reading from bottom to top wasn't Fuxi being difficult, but even the earth platform had its own opinion.

Water and fire were more complex. Flames appeared bright on the outside but relied on something beneath; water seemed soft on the outside but could penetrate and connect with the inside. Fuxi looked beyond their colors to their functions and structures. He drew the combinations on stone, asking the water carrier and the fire tender to explain them. In the first round, the fire tender mistook water for fire, reasoning that both could make people busy and sweaty; the water carrier countered that people who fell into the river usually didn't have time to roast by the fire.

Fuxi asked them what layer they had overlooked. The group returned to the stream and the stove, observing how water flowed smoothly yet hid dangers within, and how fire relied on wood to burn brightly and release heat outward. In the second round of explanations, similar combinations were no longer judged solely by their names, but by the positions of the lines, which could explain the differences.

Next came Thunder, Wind, Mountain, and Lake. Thunder rumbles below, waking all things on the earth; Wind slips between the trees, invisible yet everywhere; Mountain stands firm ahead, forcing travelers to change course; Lake sits low, gathering water and bringing joy. Each is made of the same Yin and Yang lines, but change their positions, and the relationships change too.

Fuxi handed the combinations representing Thunder and Wind to two people who had just returned from the forest. One guy only heard the thunder and insisted both symbols were "Thunder." The other pointed out that the treetops bent first, and the thunder came later, proving that the wind’s entry and the thunder’s boom were two different things. Based on this, Fuxi kept the two symbols distinct, allowing everyone to read the natural relationship for the first time by looking at what happened first and what happened second.

The tribe members noticed that eight combinations now filled the stone surface, and someone immediately declared that he understood it all. He pointed to the Mountain symbol and said, "Today is perfect for fishing in the river, because 'the mountain looks steady, so the boat must be steady too.'" Fuxi told him to go ahead and load the mountain into his boat first. The man stared at the distant peaks for a moment, decided to take back his claim of "understanding it all," and settled for saying he felt "slightly familiar" with it.

This misunderstanding confirmed for Fuxi that just having eight positions didn’t mean the Eight Trigrams were finished. The symbols had to match natural forces reliably, and the names couldn’t just be catchy rhymes. If people memorized the combinations without understanding how Yin and Yang waxed and waned across the three layers, they would still treat a mountain as a guarantee for catching fish when faced with reality.

He carved the eight three-line diagrams separately and paused his work. Next, he had to take them back to the actual sites of Heaven, Earth, Thunder, Wind, Water, Fire, Mountain, and Lake, letting each trigram withstand identification by those who didn’t know the answers. Only then could Fuxi personally name the eight relationships and complete the Eight Trigrams.

Fuxi carried the eight three-line diagrams and walked through the surrounding mountains and rivers.

He looked up at the vast sky, seeing Heaven’s vigorous, unceasing movement, and named the trigram of three Yang lines "Qian." He looked down at the earth, seeing its gentle, yielding capacity to bear all things, and named the trigram of three Yin lines "Kun." Qian and Kun were established first not because Heaven and Earth were the biggest and grabbed the names first, but because one revealed pure Yang momentum and the other revealed pure Yin virtue, setting the two ends for all other changes.

Spring thunder rolled as if from beneath the ground, the bottom line moving while the upper lines remained contained, so Fuxi named it "Zhen." Wind pierced through the forest, entering every gap, with softness below and Yang momentum above, so he named it "Xun." Water flows with hidden dangers, soft on the outside but solid within, so he called it "Kan." Fire shines bright on the outside but has something attached within, so he called it "Li." Mountains stop the path, halting travel to define boundaries, so he called it "Gen." Lakes gather water in low places, nourishing and delighting, so he called it "Dui."

Once the names were set, Fuxi gave the stone tablets to people who hadn’t accompanied him to identify them blindly. The first person received the Zhen trigram and said it looked like a sleeping beast just lifting its foot, accidentally capturing the essence of "movement." The second person identified the Xun trigram as three sticks of firewood blown crooked by the wind; although the description wasn’t elegant, at least he knew the wind wasn’t standing still.

When it came to Gen and Dui, misunderstandings arose again. One youth pointed to Gen and called it a Lake, because mountains can also hold water; another pointed to Dui and called it a Mountain, because mountain reflections are often seen by the water’s edge. Fuxi didn’t just correct the names; he made the two of them walk to the foot of the mountain and the edge of the lake. The main function of a mountain is to stop, forcing roads to detour; the main function of a lake is to gather and delight, collecting water and nourishing plants. Things can reflect each other, but the trigram emphasizes the dominant relationship in the present moment.

Aman watched from the side for a long time and finally couldn’t help but ask, "All eight diagrams only have three layers. Looking at them, it’s like a family swapping each other’s clothes."

Fuxi shuffled the stone tablets. "Then don’t look at the clothes. Explain why each line is in that position."

Aman stood beside Fuxi, gripping a thin branch he had just cut, but his eyes were fixed on the stone tablet that Fuxi had carelessly spread out. Rough scratches had already appeared on the stone, looking like the preliminary outlines of trigrams. Aman silently remembered Fuxi’s earlier words about "Qian and Kun," so he quickly shifted his attention to the upper left corner of the stone, muttering to himself, "Qian, Kun..." He raised an eyebrow, lightly tapped the horizontal line that varied slightly in depth, and identified it as "Qian."

Immediately after, his gaze slid to another diagonal line in the lower right corner. His brows knitted into the shape of the number nine, and he mumbled, "Kan, Li..." Aman’s fingers unconsciously traced a small arc on the stone, as if adding "feet" to these two lines. He connected them in his mind, imagining them as a complete picture, his brows tightening further as if trying to press all his confusion onto that stone tablet.

He suddenly pointed at the slightly thicker vertical line in the middle of the stone and announced loudly, "This line is water!" His voice carried confidence, mixed with the impulse of someone who hadn’t verified his claim. The tribe members nearby looked up and saw that the ends of the vertical line flared slightly outward, like two drops of water just about to fall.

Just as everyone was still pondering, Aman’s eyes jumped to another short set of horizontal lines on the left side of the stone slab. He suddenly changed his tone, grinning, “No, no, that’s fire!” He spread his palms and mimed flickering flames, drawing a few arcing strokes in the air as if he were really about to light something. The elders burst into laughter, pointing at Aman’s antics and saying, “Kid, are you drawing fire or trying to roast a steak?”

Aman’s face flushed a bit, but he wouldn’t back down, still insisting on his reading. He tapped a thin twig against the stone, producing a crisp sound, as if adding a beat to his “water‑fire” picture. Then he lifted his head, solemnly declared, “This hexagram shows that water and fire are battling inside me right now!” His voice echoed through the valley with a touch of childlike exaggeration.

The tribe burst into giggles, then turned their eyes to Fuxi. He raised an eyebrow, a glint of appreciation flashing in his gaze, but didn’t immediately correct Aman’s mistake. He gave Aman a friendly pat on the shoulder and signaled, “Go fetch a bowl of clear water from the stream, then bring a piece of firewood back to the hearth.” Aman scurried off, muttering, “Water and fire at war—first get the water, then light the fire, that’s the whole story.”

He crouched by the stream, fingertips brushing the flowing water, sending ripples outward like a mirror of the “water” line in his mind. Then, lugging a wooden bowl brimming with water, he staggered back to the hearth. Just as he was about to pour the water into the pot, the red glow of the fire startled him, and he sucked in a sharp breath of cool air. The dancing flames reflected in his eyes, sketching a fiery silhouette.

Back at the stone slab, Aman gently poured the water over it. The droplets traced the carved lines, forming tiny beads at the ends of the vertical stroke. Then he lit the firewood by the hearth; the blaze reflected on the stone, turning the once‑dim horizontal line a blazing red, as if real flames had ignited there.

Fuxi stood nearby, his gaze calm and deep. After watching Aman’s actions, he gave a slight nod, signaling him to continue the demonstration.

The crowd laughed. Fuxi had him fetch water from the stream and add a log to the fire. He’d actually seen the substance in the water and the illusion in the fire, and when Aman returned he finally sorted it out. The joke didn’t stick in his memory, but it highlighted where Aman had misread.

After many rounds of identification, a stranger could, without Fuxi’s prompts, name Heaven, Earth, Thunder, Wind, Water, Fire, Mountain, and Marsh, and explain why the yin‑yang lines were combined that way. Only then did Fuxi arrange the eight stone carvings: Qian, Kun, Zhen, Xun, Kan, Li, Gen, and Dui—the Eight Trigrams.

The Eight Trigrams weren’t a ready‑made picture delivered on a dragon’s back, nor a puzzle completed by someone else. Fuxi started by observing heaven and earth, was inspired by the River Diagram, grasped yin and yang, fixed the line positions, tested repeatedly, and finally drew them himself. Aman copied the eight names onto green bamboo slips, deliberately leaving a blank spot at the end.

“What’s missing?” someone asked.

“Missing is whether I’ll be able to understand what I copied after I go back home.”

Even though the Eight Trigrams were finished, if they could only be explained in front of Fuxi, they remained a personal diagram. Only when ordinary people receive them and use them to grasp change does the order of heaven and earth gain a shared language.

Aman went back, recopied the Eight Trigrams, and the next day brought a bundle of green bamboo slips.

He spread the slips out one by one. The opening Qian and Kun were still neat, but the later ones grew increasingly odd. Beside the Zhen trigram he doodled a startled bird, next to Xun he added two crooked trees; when he got to Kan and Li, fearing another mix‑up, he carved a water bowl and a fire basin respectively. At the end of Gen, a shoe appeared, because he remembered “mountain makes people stop,” but he couldn’t recall whether the shoe belonged to the trigram or to himself.

“Is this the Eight Trigrams?” someone asked.

He replied, “The first half, yes. The second half is the trail of clues I left when I got lost.”

Fuxi didn’t rewrite it for him; he simply removed the extra sketches, asking him to explain using only the three‑line relationships. The young man lost a bit of confidence each time a shoe disappeared, but later, by re‑examining the yin‑yang positions, he finally realized the Eight Trigrams stand on their own without any side drawings.

That afternoon a heavy wind rose in the valley. Some people, seeing the sky still bright, insisted on continuing to dry their nets; others, hearing distant thunder, wanted to douse every flame. Fuxi laid out the relationships between Zhen and Xun, Kan and Li before the crowd, but gave no direct orders.

Thunder rumbled far away, the wind had already slipped into the valley; upstream clouds darkened while sunlight still bathed the ground. If you only notice Li’s brightness, you’ll miss Zhen and Xun gaining strength; if you only fear Kan’s water and extinguish all fire, the rainy night loses both heat and cooked food. So the people split up: one group watched wind direction and treetops, another listened for how close the thunder sounded, a third checked the color of the upstream water.

Aman clutched his bamboo slips and followed, the wind flapping the pages so they slapped against each other. He hurriedly held them down, “If the Xun trigram really has to be demonstrated, could you at least not carve my notes so deep?”

The person watching the trees replied, “Your handwriting was already light; the wind is just helping you see where you need to carve deeper.”

Just as the laughter died down, news of a storm spread from upstream. The crowd stopped arguing over whether a particular divination was absolutely auspicious or inauspicious and instead took action: they first gathered their fishing nets and low-lying tools, moved their grain to higher ground, and protected the fires that had some cover, so the strong pre-rain winds wouldn't scatter them. After the wind and thunder passed, the rain entered the valley, and both the nets and the grain were safe, with the fire preserved under a stone lid.

This wasn't a case of the eight trigrams making an empty prediction of rain. Fuxi let everyone see that the trigram symbols put the relationships between thunder, wind, water, and fire in the same order, so people wouldn't just grab onto one sign. As reality changed, judgments also needed to adjust with the growth and decline of yin and yang.

After the rain stopped, Aman gently spread out the green bamboo slip that had gotten wet, his fingertips still feeling a bit cool. He first used a small knife to erase the bird shape that was already drawn on the slip, and then he scraped off the tilted tree branch, the overturned water bowl, the glowing fire basin, and the old shoe with a sole that was worn down to just a piece of leather. He left only the eight sets of neat, three-line symbols. Each line was like a fine bamboo texture, with faint ink marks that shimmered slightly in the post-rain air, like the gentle pulse of a breath.

Aman looked up at the tribe's leader, who was standing beside him with a slightly furrowed brow but still a patient expression. Aman took a deep breath and said in a clear voice, "These eight trigrams are arranged in this way because the energy of heaven and earth, during the alternation of yin and yang, first has 'Qian' above and 'Kun' below; then 'Zhen' on the left and 'Xun' on the right. This cycle can reflect the rules of the changing seasons and the generation of the five elements." His words were like linking the symbols on the slip together, one by one, forming an invisible thread.

Just as everyone was intently listening, the village's most mischievous kid, Ahu, suddenly rushed forward, reached out to lift a corner of the slip, and tried to pull out a piece of bamboo as a toy. Aman's eyes were quick, and with a flick of his finger, the bamboo piece snapped back, making a soft "thud" sound, like a bamboo leaf blown by the wind. Ahu was taken aback, muttering, "How did this slip do that by itself?" The crowd burst out laughing, and the laughter echoed through the valley, but no one truly understood the reasoning behind it.

Aman remained calm, still following the original order, and quietly added, "Even if one piece is missing, I can infer what the missing trigram should be based on the relationships of generation and opposition between the remaining seven trigrams." He closed his eyes, his fingers lightly tapping on the slip, as if reconstructing the missing relationship in his mind. After a moment, he raised his hand, pointed to the empty space, and said, "This gap should be 'Kan', because the water symbol must be paired with 'Li' at this time to form a complete cycle of yin and yang." The crowd looked at him in surprise, nodding in agreement, and even Ahu couldn't help but applaud, muttering, "It's 'Kan'! I thought it was 'Qian'!"

At this moment, another elder placed a round stone slab next to the slip, intentionally challenging Aman, saying, "Try to interpret this inverted pattern!" Aman didn't immediately try to guess the direction of the stone slab. Instead, he first looked down and carefully observed the patterns on the stone, then rearranged the eight trigrams on the slip from bottom to top, restoring the original order. He said slowly, "First, we need to determine the direction of observation. Only by finding the 'from bottom to top' sequence can we correctly identify the trigram symbols at each level." He lightly tapped each trigram with his finger, explaining layer by layer, and the crowd gradually understood that the order wasn't random scribbles but a reflection of the alternation of yin and yang in the universe.

Aman put away the bamboo slip, gently rolling it up and handing it back to Fuxi. Fuxi nodded, a hint of satisfaction flashing in his eyes, as if saying: from the chaotic rain to the complete arrangement of the eight trigrams, people had gone from not understanding to understanding, initially grasping the order of heaven and earth. The crowd discussed in hushed tones, their laughter mixed with a sense of awe for the new knowledge, while Aman recorded the process, a faint smile on his face, thinking to himself: even the most mischievous child could see the rules of yin and yang through correct observation and understanding.

Someone else placed the stone slab upside down to test him. He didn't force a guess along the inverted pattern but instead first restored the common sequence from "bottom to top" and then explained it layer by layer. This was exactly the method Fuxi had repeatedly emphasized: first determine the observation method, then identify the trigram symbols.

"Are you still lost?" Fuxi asked.

"There are still many paths," he replied, "but at least I know which line isn't the way."

He acknowledged that these lines weren't random drawings, nor was it the case that the more words, the easier it was to understand. He recalled the page in "The Twenty Bamboo Slips of Mountains and Seas" about creating characters: words allowed things to be recorded, while Fuxi's trigrams allowed opposite things to be seen together, revealing changes. The eight trigrams had helped a real judgment for the first time, and the eight relationships drawn by Fuxi himself had found practical use among people.

After the eight trigrams spread, the first new problem that arose wasn't that no one knew how to use them, but that some people used them too quickly.

A grain farmer saw the Kun trigram representing the earth that carries all things and declared that every field should be planted immediately. People at the foot of the mountain followed his advice, and people on the riverbank did the same. Unfortunately, the riverbank seeds were quickly washed away by the rising water. Another person, seeing the Gen trigram meant "stop," would halt completely whenever he encountered a mountain path. He refused to take a detour, eventually sitting at the crossroads and waiting for the mountain to move out of his way.

Fuxi called everyone together but did not announce that the trigrams were wrong. Instead, he asked the farmer if he had observed the terrain, the season, and the water levels. He then asked the traveler whether "stop" meant never moving again, or simply pausing to see the obstacle clearly before choosing a new direction.

"If Gen only tells people to sit down," Fuxi said, "then mountain goats probably understand it the least. They walk up and down the mountains every single day."

The traveler looked at a goat leaping playfully along the cliff edge and felt that his defeat was well-deserved.

Fuxi had different groups take the same trigram to different places to test it. The soil on the sunny slope was warm and drained well, while the soil on the riverbank was moist but prone to flooding, so the planting times naturally differed. Even with the same Kun earth, one had to watch how the yin and yang within it changed. On mountain paths, you stop for falling rocks, but once you spot a side trail, you can turn and go that way. "Stop" does not seal off action forever; it is just one part of the cycle of change.

Others used the Li trigram to observe fire and sunlight. Pottery needs fire, but if the heat is too strong, it cracks. Grains need sun, but if left in it too long, they lose their moisture. The Kan water can nourish, but it can also become a danger. The Zhen thunder makes people alert, but if you only feel fear without observing, you are just running away from the noise. The Xun wind can clear the way, but it can also help a fire spread. No single trigram is a command that ignores the situation.

The tribe carved down their findings and shared them with those who hadn’t been there to read aloud. One person recorded, "It is not suitable to live by the water," but people living on high banks objected. When they changed it to "Low banks are dangerous when the water rises," the conditions and consequences finally became clear. The eight trigrams helped them organize scattered experiences into relationships, but they did not spare the tribe from having to observe for themselves.

Farming seasons were no longer based solely on an old man remembering which year warmed up early, and traveling was no longer guided just by a predecessor’s vague advice to "head toward the mountains." People compared sunlight, soil type, wind direction, water flow, and terrain, understanding that the same force could produce different results depending on the context. When resolving disputes within the tribe, they began to ask: Who is advancing, and who is retreating? When should we move, and when should we stop? If one side becomes too dominant, will it force the other side to reverse?

Fuxi always refused to calculate a fixed, unchangeable destiny for people. He drew the trigrams to make change understandable, not to set up a stall that saved everyone from using their own eyes. When someone asked about the luck of a marriage, he told them to go home and talk it out first. When someone asked if they would find prey tomorrow, he told them to check if they had set traps today. The questioner complained disappointedly that the eight trigrams refused to let people be lazy. Fuxi replied, "Heaven and Earth haven’t promised to do it for you either."

Gradually, the tribe no longer needed Fuxi standing beside them to explain their judgments. They understood that Qian and Kun were not about one suppressing the other, and that water and fire were not eternal enemies. It was the waxing and waning of yin and yang, and the way opposites complemented each other, that kept nature and human affairs running smoothly.

Thus, the eight trigrams evolved from eight pictures on stone into a shared method for understanding order. They could not cancel out storms, dangerous paths, or arguments, but they allowed people to see conditions, relationships, and transformations within mixed experiences, rather than treating every change as a random accident with no cause.

When another mountain rain approached, Fuxi did not speak first.

This time, there were no thick clouds overhead, but low fog clung to the mountainside. The river surface was calm, yet the water birds by the marsh were all moving to higher ground. If they simply copied the conclusion from the first sudden rain, they might ignore the situation because the swallows weren’t flying low. But if they just memorized "Kan means danger," they would retreat from any water and miss the real threat: a mudslide coming from the mountain.

The tribe members laid out the eight trigram stones themselves. One person looked at the Xun trigram to gauge the wind in the fog and realized the wind was pushing moist air into the valley. Another looked at the Gen trigram to study the mountain’s shape, pointing out that a newly cracked slope had shifted from "stopping" to "moving." Those watching the water compared Kan and Dui, discovering that although the marsh surface was still, the water gathering from upstream was increasing. The fire still needed to be preserved, but it could not be left in the low sheds.

They did not wait for Fuxi to declare good or bad luck. One team moved the grain and tools from the low areas, another led the livestock away from below the cracked slope, and a third protected the fire with stone covers and dug shallow trenches around the sheds. The traveler, originally planning to take the old path to the neighboring valley, stopped at the mountain pass to observe, as Gen suggested. After spotting falling rocks, he actively took a detour instead of sitting and waiting for the mountain to move.

The mountain rain poured down, and the fog wrapped tightly around the slopes like heavy silk. Raindrops tapped against the stone slabs with a crisp sound. Fuxi stood on the high ground, his brows slightly furrowed, his eyes fixed on the distant valley. There, behind the fog, he saw the shape of the River Map; the outline of the Dragon Horse carrying the map appeared and disappeared in the rain curtain. Aman followed closely behind, gripping a rough bamboo staff. He occasionally looked up at Fuxi and muttered, "This rain is falling faster than my mom’s pot lid. Why doesn’t it just wash the mountain clean?"

The rain suddenly intensified, and muddy water rushed down the slope like a murky river charging toward the old shelter. The shelter had long been empty, its few collapsed wooden beams swaying in the wind with a mournful “creak.” The sludge surged into the low-lying marsh, churning up frothy bubbles that splashed onto nearby stones, leaving mottled stains. A flash of insight lit Fuxi’s eyes—he saw the water’s direction, speed, and where it met the mountain, and silently noted the alternating “Kan” and “Li” patterns.

Meanwhile, the tribe had already hauled their fire, grain, and livestock up onto the high rocky platform. Aman watched a sturdy adult scramble a sheep onto the platform, only to trip on the rope around his ankle and nearly tumble down. He shouted, half‑embarrassed, “Aren’t I just practicing the ‘Bagua steps’?” The crowd burst into laughter, then quickly settled the sheep in place.

The rain eased a bit, and the fog thinned, letting the valley’s outline reappear. Fuxi stood at the edge of the platform, tapping the ground with his finger, murmuring, “Yin and yang give birth to each other, strength and softness work together—mountain is firm, water is gentle, wind moves, marsh rests.” He sketched the river‑map symbols he’d just observed onto the rock with simple marks—first a curved line for the ridge, then two short intersecting lines for the stream, three dots for wind direction, and finally a tiny stone to mark the marsh’s center. Aman tried to copy it, but his pen slipped and drew a crooked horizontal line. He quickly corrected it, then chuckled, “My drawing looks more like the ‘eight’ in ‘Bagua’—guess I’m making everyone laugh!”

The tribe gathered around, first staring in surprise at Fuxi’s diagram. Someone pointed at the winding mountain line and asked, “Is that a mountain, or a dragon’s back?” Fuxi smiled without answering, simply nodding to keep the focus on the whole picture. Then he explained each part: “When the wind blows from east to west, the leeward side of the mountain gathers moisture and forms fog; rain runs down the slope into the low marsh; if we move our grain and livestock to the high, sheltered side, we’ll stay out of the water’s path.”

The people’s eyes lit up, and they all nodded approvingly. Aman saw a child waving a wooden stick like a wind‑vane, pointing east and giggling, “I thought the wind came from here, but it actually ran out from there!” Amid the laughter, everyone began rearranging according to Fuxi’s Bagua map: some hauled stone shields to the slope’s foot to block the mudflow, others piled grain bags on the mountain’s shady side to keep them dry.

When the rain stopped, a faint blue peeked through the clearing sky, the fog lifted, and the valley settled into calm. The tribe returned to the platform and shouted in unison, “Bagua saved us!” Then, one by one, they explained how wind, mountain, water, and marsh interacted: the wind drove rain onto the mountain’s back, the back guided the water into the marsh, and the marsh’s water flowed back to nourish the grasses at the mountain’s foot. Each person could clearly recount how their recent actions fit the pattern.

Fuxi asked, “What if the wind blows the opposite way next time?”

“Look again.”

“What if the river rises before the fog appears?”

“Look again, too.”

“What if someone says, ‘We did it like that last time, so just copy it now’?”

The fire‑keeper replied, “First invite them to see the shelter that got washed away. That shelter’s already gone with that excuse.”

The crowd laughed, then got to work repairing. Their laughter now came not from guessing sun or rain, but from finally understanding why copying blindly doesn’t work. The Bagua gives order, not a pre‑written answer that skips observation.

Aman recorded the events on green bamboo slips. He drew the eight three‑line trigrams, paused when he reached Kan and Li, and a nearby child promptly reminded him, “Don’t add a water bowl, and don’t add shoes.”

“I’m just letting the pen catch its breath.”

“A broken line gets a pause; a whole line can’t rest.”

Stuck by the rules he’d just learned, Aman hurried to finish the carving. At the end he wrote: Fuxi observed heaven, earth, and all things, saw the dragon‑horse bearing the river map, and drew the Bagua from yin and yang; the people used the Bagua to grasp nature’s and human affairs’ changes. A page in The Twenty Bamboo Slips of Mountains and Seas once showed him a shared shape for memory, and Fuxi gave those changes a common order to discuss.

Those lines were all derived by Fuxi himself from heaven, earth, and the river map. Aman’s job was only to note how people observed, tested, and learned to use them; drawing the trigrams and naming them remained Fuxi’s achievement.

After the rain, the clouds cleared, and mountain, marsh, thunder, wind, water, and fire re‑appeared across heaven and earth, with the cosmos still turning above and below. The Bagua didn’t lock everything into eight little boxes; it let people see how opposite forces depend on each other, how positions shift, and how rise and fall transform.

Fuxi put away the stone slab with the first drawing of the river map, leaving only the eight trigrams for everyone. From then on, when people encountered new changes, they no longer had to go back to the river to wait for another dragon horse, nor did they have to wait for Fuxi to redraw the trigrams. Instead, they would first observe the heavens and the earth, then distinguish between yin and yang, and finally explain their own choices. The chaotic experience thus became an understandable, verifiable, and transmissible order that could be passed down to future generations.
