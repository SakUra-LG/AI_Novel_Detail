When Cangjie arrived at the grain field, two men were each pulling on a rope, neither willing to let go.

The borrower of grain said, "A big knot is a basket, a small knot is a handful. I borrowed three baskets and have already returned two."

The lender flipped the rope over and said, "Big knots count the days, small knots count the amount. I’m counting that he borrowed ten days ago, so today he should return five baskets."

Cangjie asked, "So is it three baskets or five?"

Both men answered together, "Look at the knots!"

Cangjie stared at the tangled knots for a while. The knots were packed together, looking united, but each meant something different. He told the two men to let go, each taking the other’s rope and recounting. The borrower counted the other’s knots and claimed he owed four and a half baskets; the lender, squeezing the other rope, calculated that the borrower had actually over‑paid by a clay pot. The onlookers were thrilled—not because the truth was finally clear, but because a few more minutes of arguing might magically turn the grain debt into a whole cow.

"What does this thin rope mean?" Cangjie asked.

The lender replied, "It’s tied at the end, meaning the matter is finished."

The borrower immediately said, "No, it means ‘we’ll talk about it later.’"

Cangjie laid the two ropes side by side and asked, "If the knot‑maker isn’t there, can the knot speak for him?"

Everyone fell silent. The knots, of course, stayed silent too—yet they seemed more confidently right than the people.

The grain debt still unresolved, a shepherd boy ran up for help. Yesterday someone had asked him to drive a flock of sheep to the south slope, but he’d seen three tiny knots on the rope and thought he’d have to deliver them to three ditches. When he got to the third ditch there was only a fox, and the old man waiting on the north slope had a beard tangled enough to be a knot.

The shepherd‑caller lifted the other rope to argue, "This end’s knot means the north slope; that end means the south slope."

The boy asked, "When you handed it to me, which end was ‘this end’?"

The shepherd‑caller opened his mouth and realized both ends of the rope looked just like “this end.”

Cangjie told them each to tie a new knot. One used a big knot for the place and a small knot for the number of sheep; the other did the opposite. Some used the distance of knots to mark time of day, others used rope thickness to note weight. Everyone claimed their own system was crystal‑clear, then looked at the other’s and saw only a mess.

Cangjie didn’t guess for them. He first asked both sides to bring the people who’d been there that day, to re‑ask about the grain and the sheep, and he stored the two disputes in his mind. At dusk he hung the ropes on a branch; when the wind blew, dozens of interpretations swayed before his eyes.

Knot‑tying as record‑keeping can remind the knoter: there’s something here, something there. But it can’t name the people or objects, nor can it show how things happened. Memory follows people; as long as people are around, they can explain. If people wander, forget, or die, the rope is left with a row of silent knots.

Cangjie touched an old knot and whispered, "If meaning can only live in one person’s mind, what’s left isn’t a story at all, just a knot that teases people to guess."

The next day he asked three times each about the grain debt and the sheep saga. Every retelling missed something, added something else, like water slipping through fingers. Looking at the rope‑laden shelves, Cangjie for the first time felt certain: humanity needs something that lets even those who didn’t see with their own eyes know what really happened.

After a night of rain, a sack of fresh millet was missing from the grain field.

The grain‑keeper pointed to the soggy ground and said, "Probably a deer dragged it away." The hunter replied, "Deer don’t eat millet in bags; it was likely a thief." A few kids gathered around the messy prints, insisting a monster bird with human feet had passed. Cangjie didn’t wait to see who shouted the loudest; he crouched by the mud and examined each trace.

Two fine lines stretched toward the fence, each print splitting into three branches ahead and ending with a tiny dot. Cangjie scattered a handful of broken grain; soon sparrows hopped over, their claws landing exactly on the fine lines. Another set of prints split into two lobes, deep in the soft mud. Far off, a startled deer leapt over a shallow ditch, leaving hooves that also split into two, though narrower than the ground marks.

"Did you see that?" the hunter said, "A deer’s size varies; you can’t convict it just by a pair of hooves."

"So we have to see where it goes," Cangjie replied, following the hoofprints to a creek. The prints turned toward the woods, but the grains didn’t follow. The real scattered grains trailed a line of human footprints, looped around the fence, and stopped by a tipped wooden cart. The sack was wedged under a wheel; apparently the grain‑carrier had hidden from the rain, forgot to unload, and the cart slid down, dumping the sack into a grass ditch.

The man stared at his own footprints and sighed, "I meant to wait for the rain to stop before explaining, but the mud spoke for me first."

The man looked at his footprints and said with a sigh, "I was going to wait for the rain to stop before explaining, but it seems the mud has spoken up first." Just then, a young man with a green bamboo slip on his back peeked out from the side of the road - it was Aman. He saw the crowd gathered around the footprints and excitedly pulled out a bamboo slip: "I can leave my mark too. You see, this stroke is like a wave, this curve is like a mountain path, and this thing that looks like a fallen bug is where I slipped in the mud yesterday." Aman gently unfolded the bamboo slip and pointed to the marks, gesturing for everyone to follow along. An elderly man nearby shook his head and chuckled, "This stroke looks like rolling waves, quite good actually." Aman then slid his finger along the curve, indicating the twists and turns of the mountain path, and said with a wink, "This curve is like the winding path I walked on." Next, he placed his finger on the bug-shaped mark, looked up at the sky, and whispered, "This looks like a fallen bug, maybe it's where I slipped in the mud yesterday." The crowd passed the bamboo slip around, and someone said the stroke looked like a snake, while another said it resembled a bent vegetable stalk. As for the "bug," a shepherd boy guessed it had a stomachache, and a hunter thought it had been dead for three days.

The crowd passed the bamboo slip around, and someone said the stroke looked like a snake, while another said it resembled a bent vegetable stalk. As for the "bug," a shepherd boy guessed it had a stomachache, and a hunter thought it had been dead for three days.

Aman gently put the bamboo slip away, cleared his throat, and said, "It seems my meaning is still stuck in my own mouth, hasn't moved into the bamboo slip yet."

Cangjie didn't engrave according to Aman's marks, nor did he ask how future generations would write. He simply placed the bamboo slip alongside the footprints in the mud. The young man's curved line only made sense with his own explanation; bird tracks don't need birds to explain them, anyone who's seen birds can recognize them. Deer hooves and human feet are the same.

Cangjie asked someone to smooth out a small patch of mud, then had a bird walk over it again, a deer pass by, and three different people take a step. The crowd compared the new marks with the old ones, first distinguishing between birds, beasts, and humans, and then figuring out who came and went based on the direction. The children had been discussing the strange human footprints earlier, but now, following the bird tracks, they found some grains of food under the fence and finally admitted that the monster was probably just their own hunger.

Cangjie broke off a thin branch and, on the edge of the mud, sketched the most distinctive features of the bird tracks - three forks - and the deer's hooves - two lobes - and the human foot - a long shape. He didn't call them characters, but asked an elderly man who had just arrived to identify them. The old man, who hadn't seen the earlier marks, pointed to the shapes and said they were a bird, a beast, and a human.

Rain can wash away footprints, and things don't always cooperate by stepping into the mud. But on this day, Cangjie got a starting point from the bird and beast tracks: since all things can leave their identity through shape, humans can also take shapes from things and leave what they've seen for those who haven't.

He added a note to the green bamboo slip, but fearing others might misread it, he simply drew three feet next to it. Cangjie looked at it and asked, "Are you drawing a bird, a deer, or that monster from yesterday?"

The young man was silent for a moment, then gently erased the three feet: "This starting point, let's leave it to you."

Cangjie took away from the mud not a few footprints, but a question: what is the shape that each thing in the world cannot lose?

At dawn, he faced the rising sun and engraved a circle on a stone. The sunlight moved to the treetops, and the circle could still make people think of the sun; but when the moon came out at night, it was also a circle. If he drew a crescent moon as a thin arc, it wouldn't resemble a full moon. He observed the sun and moon for several days, eventually not trying to engrave every ray of light, but only keeping the essential features that could distinguish between the two.

Mountains were even harder. Someone drew an entire mountain slope, including stone cracks, tree clusters, and a sheep standing in the wrong place. The sheep's owner was very satisfied, thinking the shape at least proved his sheep had been to high places; Cangjie asked, "If every mountain needs to find the same sheep first, the mountains of the world will have to wait for it to finish."

He removed the trees and the sheep, leaving only the connected peaks. People who had seen mountains recognized it at a glance, and those who hadn't been to that mountain also knew it wasn't water. When drawing water, he observed the twists and turns of the stream and kept the continuous undulations; when drawing trees, he took the outline of the trunk, roots, and branches; when drawing grain, he added the hanging ears, making it more than just a thin tree standing in the field.

Birds and beasts loved to give him a hard time. Cows had horns when seen from the front, but only one horn from the side; sheep had their horns covered by their ears when they lowered their heads to eat. Cangjie didn't chase after each animal to change its shape, but instead chose the most recognizable features. A hunter brought a piece of turtle shell and said the patterns on a turtle's back could also distinguish the cracks like roads. Cangjie was inspired: shape doesn't have to be the same as the real thing, as long as it captures the key features that everyone has seen.

He also studied people. He watched them standing, sitting, and bending over to work, each in their own unique pose; he noticed how their posture changed when they held something versus when their hands were empty. Cangjie would repeat these movements for everyone to see, then ask them what they noticed first. Some people stared at his legs, others at his hands, and one person honestly admitted he was just wondering why Cangjie had suddenly started squatting in the yard, suspecting he’d been bitten by an ant.

After the laughter died down, Cangjie kept the basic shapes of the human body and limbs, but he looked for simpler ways to show changes in action, like walking or picking things up. He carved these candidate symbols onto wooden slats, stone surfaces, and turtle shells, comparing which lines stayed clear no matter where they were drawn. If a symbol was too complicated, one person might carve it looking like a mountain, while another made it look like a patch of thorns; if it was too simple, the sun, a mouth, and a pebble might all get squashed together into one confused shape.

Cangjie didn’t copy thousands of shapes from all things in a single night. He only organized a small batch each day: first observing, then stripping away the irrelevant details; first letting people who knew the object identify it, then showing it to those who had never seen the original. Symbols that were easily misidentified were set aside for now, while those that could be recognized consistently were kept.

Gradually, the yard filled with wooden slats of varying lengths. The sun and moon, mountains and rivers, plants and trees, birds and beasts, and humans all stood there, each represented by a simple symbol. They looked like simple shadows taken from all things—without fur or color, without sound—yet they kept their true identity.

Someone asked in surprise, “Are these already characters?”

Cangjie shook his head. “Right now, they can only introduce themselves individually. If you line them up, they still don’t relate to each other. At best, they’re just a group of mute people standing very neatly in a row.”

He flipped the wooden slats over, shuffled the order, and prepared to have people who hadn’t helped create the shapes try to identify them. All things had already handed over their appearances; now it was time to see if these appearances could hold onto their meaning once they left Cangjie’s hands.

Cangjie mixed the candidate symbols together and invited a few people who had never seen the character-creation process to try and recognize them.

The first person, an old man, picked up the slat representing the sun and studied it closely. “This is an open mouth.”

“Why a mouth?”

“It’s round and empty inside. If it’s not a mouth, what could it be? And if it’s the sun, why isn’t it hot?”

Everyone in the yard laughed. Cangjie didn’t say the old man was wrong; he just added a clear mark inside the sun shape and adjusted the mouth shape to better show it opening and closing. During the second round of testing, the old man correctly identified the sun, but then he placed the two shapes together and read it as “mouth swallows sun.” He asked Cangjie seriously, “Is it getting dark today because that mouth is doing it?”

Cangjie said, “If there really was a mouth that big, we should first record what it eats.”

Amidst the laughter, he realized that being able to recognize individual shapes wasn’t enough. If they weren’t arranged in a specific order, the meaning would still run wild. So he established a rule: everyone must read from the same end, in sequence. No one could start from the left, no one from the right, and no one could just pick their favorite shape to read first.

The next slat was for a tree. The tester saw branches on the trunk and correctly identified it as a tree. But when the shape for grain was shown, he called it “a tree broken by the wind.” A woman who grew grain objected, “The grain heads bow down because the seeds are heavy. If your tree bowed down like that every year, I’d be happy to wait under it for it to bear food.”

Cangjie placed the tree and grain shapes side by side and asked everyone to point out the differences. He kept the drooping shape of the grain head and made the branches and roots of the tree clearer. After the changes, even children could tell them apart. The tester still refused to admit defeat, pointing at the grain and saying, “Actually, I was about to say grain, but I was afraid it would get proud.”

The cow and the sheep caused another debate. Both had horns, and at first glance, their symbols looked like a pair of relatives who were a bit too close for comfort. A herdsman pointed out that cow horns spread out while sheep horns curve; Cangjie kept the difference in the horn shapes. In the second round, a young boy finally stopped putting the cow in the sheep pen, but he asked back, “If there’s a cow with very curved horns, doesn’t that mean it has to become a sheep?”

Cangjie took him to observe the livestock. A single shape doesn’t lock in every possible variation of a living thing; instead, it keeps the most reliable feature people use to identify it. When things look similar, you have to judge them along with the surrounding shapes and the context of where they appear. After the boy understood, he patted the cow’s horn and said, “So you’re still a cow. You nearly changed your family just because your horns were curved.”

After a few rounds of blind reading, the liveliest part of the courtyard wasn’t getting the symbols right—it was getting them wrong. The water shape got called three snakes, the moon shape was mistaken for a leftover piece of pie, and the bent‑over human figure was guessed as a broken bow. Each time Cangjie asked, “Why?” and hunted down the mix‑ups in the jokes: was it not enough distinctive features, a wrong direction, or the neighboring shapes lacking a clear order? Aman, perched nearby, squinted his eyes into little arches and muttered, “Isn’t that a ‘fish’?” He lifted a finger to the bamboo slip, tapped the wooden board lightly—like a tiny drumbeat—that made everyone burst out laughing. Cangjie looked up, eyebrows raised just a touch, and said softly, “First lock down the main stroke of the shape, then fill in the details.” Aman nodded, his fingertip gliding over the slip as if adding the finishing brushstroke. Someone had written “person” as a “bow”; Cangjie chuckled, “Your arrowhead is pretty straight.” Aman giggled, his pen jittered and a curved line swooped onto the slip like a willow branch caught in a breeze. Laughter rang out, and Cangjie kept probing, “Why did you draw the waist of the ‘person’ as a bowstring?” Aman blinked and answered, “Because I wanted the person to look like they’re pulling a bow.” Cangjie shook his head, his laugh tinged with a hint of seriousness, as if to say, “Alright, let’s nail that point before moving on.” He set his pen down and tapped the board again, sealing the rule. Aman lifted his head, eyes sparkling, and whispered, “Now I get it—‘person’ is just ‘person.’” The courtyard erupted in another round of giggles while Cangjie kept breaking down each shape’s backbone and its finer bits, piecing them together like wooden boards forming a complete picture. Aman took notes on the side, occasionally scribbling a wrong character, prompting a chuckle from his peers, “Your writing looks like a ‘fish.’” Aman lowered his head, muttering, “I wrote ‘fish,’ not ‘fish.’” He put the pen back and followed Cangjie’s guidance, slowly teasing out every key difference, as if dismantling a painting into countless tiny pieces and reassembling them one by one. Cangjie’s voice echoed through the yard, turning chaos into a rhythmic order. On Aman’s green bamboo slips, every word was copied with painstaking care, his eyes flashing with curiosity about what lay ahead. After several blind‑reading rounds, the courtyard’s buzz was still about the mis‑recognitions: water turned into three snakes, moon into a cut‑off pie, the bent human into a broken bow. Cangjie kept asking “Why?” to pinpoint the confusion—missing traits, wrong direction, or unordered neighbors.

He didn’t pile on a mountain of new symbols for every mistake. Instead, he erased the obscure scratches that only the writer could explain and added back the clear, universal differences anyone could see. The strokes gradually aligned, similar shapes were spaced apart, and the sequence of pieces settled into a fixed order.

At dusk, Cangjie brought in a fresh group and handed them the revised wooden pieces. The sun was no longer read as a mouth, wood and grain each returned to their proper spots, and the ox and sheep weren’t swapped on a whim. If a reader hesitated, they could now correct themselves by spotting the shape differences—no need to wait for Cangjie to reveal the answer.

The first batch of elders came back to look at the two pieces for sun and mouth, deliberately asking, “Does the big mouth still eat the sun today?”

Cangjie replied, “If it does, you have to read them in order. First spit out the sun, then open the mouth.”

The elders laughed and put the wooden pieces back where they belonged. The candidate symbols finally could leave their creator’s workshop and be reliably recognized by strangers. But Cangjie wasn’t done: naming the sun, trees, oxen, and sheep is just ticking off everything one by one; true record‑keeping means telling future folks who did what, where.

So Cangjie decided to document a real event that hardly anyone had witnessed.

When rain was looming, a young man named Shisheng hauled three baskets of grain from an open field into a cave at the foot of a mountain. He moved two baskets first, but the rope snapped on the way, so he went back for a new rope before carrying the third basket. Cangjie watched the whole thing from start to finish, carving the person, the grain, the mountain, the cave, the rain, and the sequence of actions in order. To keep the count from turning back into a tangled knot, he used the same grain symbol three times for the three baskets, and varied the arrangement to show that two baskets went first and one followed later.

He gave the wooden piece to someone who had never been to the field. That person identified Shisheng, the grain, the cave, and the rain, but read it as, “Shisheng in the rain took grain from the cave, took two baskets, then sent one back.”

Shisheng’s face twisted, “If you write it like that, I spent a whole day busy and still owe the cave a basket.”

The trouble isn’t the individual symbols—it’s the actions and directions. Cangjie re‑examined how a person lifts, carries, and sets down things, giving “take” and “place” distinct shapes, and placing the start and end points before and after the action. He had people act out the record: if a reader pulls the grain out of the cave, the order is still ambiguous; only when they can put the grain back into the cave does the meaning land correctly.

The second trial reading was underway, but the direction of the grain transport was correct, while the sequence of the rain was wrong. Some people thought the rain fell first, and then Stone was rescued; in fact, Cangjie wanted to record that he saw the cloud and moved the grain first, and the rain came after the grain was stored in the cave. Cangjie then put the marks indicating the sequence in a place where they could be easily seen, so that readers wouldn't have to guess to fill in the gaps.

He said, 'This is even more tiring than moving the grain.'

Cangjie replied, 'You're moving three baskets of grain, but I'm moving yesterday. If yesterday isn't moved properly, tomorrow it will roll out of the cave.'

After everyone had a good laugh, they continued with the trial reading. It wasn't until a latecomer, relying solely on the wooden slips, said: Stone saw the rain cloud and moved the three baskets of grain into the mountain cave in two trips, changing the broken rope along the way, and then the heavy rain fell - that's when the story was first told without relying on the mouth of an eyewitness.

Cangjie was still not at ease. He wrapped the records in animal hide, put them in a wooden box, and asked all those in the know not to discuss the matter that day. The next morning, he found an old man who had just returned from a neighboring valley. The old man didn't know what had happened the day before, but he recognized the candidate symbols he had learned a few days ago.

The old man read slowly, and at one point, he mistook the broken rope for a curved road. According to the meaning of the context, he corrected it himself: 'If it's a curved road, it shouldn't be followed by changing the rope; therefore, this says the rope is broken.' Then, he completely recounted the three baskets of grain, the two trips, the cave, and the rain. Stone immediately went to check the cave and found that the three baskets of grain were arranged as recorded, and the broken rope was hanging at the entrance.

The courtyard fell silent. Yesterday was no longer in the mouths of the crowd, nor was it hidden in someone's memory; it was carved into shapes that could be recognized together, connected in order, and after a night's time, it could still speak for itself.

Cangjie reorganized the individual symbols for objects, actions, directions, and sequences, and called these shapes that could convey meaning and be recorded continuously 'characters.' People said he had completed the creation of characters, but he handed the wooden slips to Stone again: 'You read them.'

After Stone finished reading, he intentionally sighed: 'Very good. I finally don't owe the cave a basket of grain.'

This laughter was different from before. Previously, people laughed because no one knew what the knot was saying; now they laughed because everyone knew the same thing. Cangjie looked at the row of characters and understood that the true completion of character creation was not that he could carve them, but that when he was not present, others could still read the meaning.

Characters had preserved yesterday. The next step was for them to leave the courtyard and go to the granary, the mountain roads, and among the medicinal herbs, to prove that they could relay the memories of more people.

As more people learned characters, misreadings also increased.

The old man in charge of grain learned the fastest. He carved the grain stored in each cave onto wooden slips and had someone else follow the records to retrieve the grain. The first time, that person returned empty-handed, saying the record had mistakenly written 'east second cave' as 'east two caves,' and he had walked back and forth between the two cave entrances, eventually suspecting that the grain had moved on its own.

Cangjie didn't just correct the old man's mistakes; instead, he had the two of them go together: one person followed the record to guide the way, and the other checked the location. The old man discovered that he had crowded the location and quantity together, so he separated them again. The second time, the person retrieving the grain accurately found the cave entrance and retrieved the needed grain. The old man stroked the wooden slip and said, 'This is better than knots. When knots are wrong, they remain silent, but when characters are wrong, they can still take me to the scene to settle the account.'

People walking the mountain roads carved down the forks in the road, streams, and independent large trees, and handed them to companions who had not walked that road. The companions followed the records and arrived at the neighboring valley, only stopping at one place: the wooden slip wrote 'cross the water and turn towards the wood,' but there were three trees in front of them. After returning, the recorder added the shape of the giant stone next to the tree. The next person to walk the road didn't have to ask about the three trees one by one.

When it came to herbs, caution was key. The herbalist carefully noted the leaf shape, the scent, the usable parts, and the bitter or spicy taste after a quick sample, so later generations wouldn’t just rely on vague notes like “that grass looked pretty lively.” Aman heard someone talking about bitter grass and hurried over, clutching his green bamboo slips. He stared at the newly carved symbol for bitterness, his brow furrowing deeper with every glance. “Why does this character look like a crying face? Does the grass make your mouth cry before your brain even knows what’s happening?” The herbalist chuckled, “If the grass heard you say that, it would probably get even more serious about being bitter.” Aman handed the slip to an elder nearby, who shook his head with a smile. “You’ve written it so it looks like the grass is crying. Better to just write the character for ‘bitter’ so people don’t think the plant is screaming.” Aman bit his tongue, thinking to himself: If I could make the character for ‘bitter’ look like a leaf, people would remember it better. He gently touched his brush to the bamboo, trying to connect the curves of the character with the veins of a leaf. As soon as he finished, a curious child leaned in and asked, “Uncle Aman, does this character mean ‘you’ll cry if you eat it’?” Aman shook his head with a smile. “No, child. It means eating it makes your heart feel sad.” The child blinked, grinning like a little bird, voice bright and clear. “Then I won’t cry when I eat grass!” Aman tucked the slip away, silently noting: As long as the character looks like the leaf, people will remember its shape, not just its meaning. The herbalist continued gathering plants, while Aman copied the new character onto his slips for future use. The herbalist added, “If the grass heard you say that, it would probably get even more serious about being bitter.”

The herbalist said, “If the grass heard you say that, it would probably get even more serious about being bitter.”

He remembered how, back when Shennong tasted the hundred herbs, he had recorded “bitter” looking exactly like “cry.” He immediately shielded his bamboo slips. “This isn’t entirely my fault. ‘Bitter’ and ‘cry’ only differ by a face that isn’t very happy.”

Cangjie had him taste a tiny piece of a bitter leaf that had already been confirmed safe. His facial features instantly scrambled in different directions, fleeing for their lives. The onlookers immediately understood exactly what “bitter” meant. With his mouth full of water, he mumbled, “Now I think this symbol is quite subtle. The real thing adds three extra strokes of pain.”

After the laughter died down, Cangjie asked the herbalist to place the symbol for taste next to the symbol for weeping, keeping the key distinguishing parts distinct. He read it again, and this time, he didn’t make the medicinal herbs feel unjustly sad. He didn’t teach Cangjie how to create characters, nor did he fix everyone’s misreadings. He simply added a single stroke to his own green bamboo slips and asked someone nearby to read it aloud. The bystander read, “This person eats bitter grass and cries with great scholarship.”

He protested, “You can delete the second half.”

“It can be deleted,” Cangjie said, “but you can’t change what already happened into ‘sweet grass’ just because you’re embarrassed.”

From then on, every type of record had to be cross-checked. The grain manager didn’t read his own grain logs, the traveler didn’t rely on his own drawn maps, and the herbalist let others identify the plants by the text. Errors would reveal themselves when someone took the wrong item, went the wrong way, or failed to recognize a plant, and then everyone would return to correct them. The characters didn’t rely on Cangjie standing by to explain, nor did they depend on the bamboo slips of future visitors for support. Once ordinary people learned them, they could use the characters to hand over their knowledge to someone else.

A few days later, three new records were tested simultaneously: the grain collector found the correct cave, the traveler arrived at the neighboring valley, and the student of herbs identified the bitter plant among several similar leaves. He only tasted enough to make him frown, avoiding any other trouble.

He solemnly carved into the bamboo slips: “Characters can record grain, record paths, and record herbs.”

A bystander took the slips, finished reading, and then pointed to the end, asking, “Does this part also say ‘you are afraid of bitterness’?”

He snatched the slips back. “That’s just a crack in the bamboo node.”

Cangjie smiled but offered no argument. Private explanations could protect one’s pride for a moment, but the shared characters had already entered the granaries, the mountain paths, and the herb gardens. They began to preserve knowledge for everyone, and they also began to preserve the truth, without much politeness.

On the day the characters spread, the sky suddenly changed.

Before the day was over, a deep rumble echoed from within the clouds. There was no fierce wind, yet fine grains of millet fell from the sky, first hitting the leaves, then scattering across roofs, roads, and fields. People reached out to catch them, recognized the millet, and cries of surprise passed from one place to another. Unlike rain, which scattered upon hitting the ground, each grain stayed on the soil, as if the sky itself wanted to leave a tangible mark for the new events in the human world.

Some were delighted and hurried to bring out containers to catch the falling grain; others were terrified, hiding the characters they had just learned behind stones, fearing that heaven and earth were scolding them. Cangjie walked into the rain of grain, picked up a single millet seed, and said neither that this was a reward nor that it was a punishment. He told everyone to first settle the elders, children, and livestock, and then to collect the fallen grain without fighting or trampling it.

After nightfall, sad weeping sounded again in the wilds.

The sound drifted near and far, seeming to come together from the shady sides of mountains, abandoned graves, and deep valleys no one passed through. People by the fires stopped talking. Some believed it was ghosts crying and argued for immediately grinding away all the carved marks; others clutched the wooden tablets inscribed with grain counts, unwilling to throw them away yet afraid to look at them.

Cangjie asked, “Before writing existed, you could hide a forgotten grain behind a simple ‘I forgot,’ blame a wrong turn on the messenger, and let a misused herb keep harming people after the knowledgeable one passed away. Now that these things can be recorded, there’s fewer places for deceit and forgetfulness to hide. If ghosts and spirits thrive on human ignorance, they’ll certainly weep.”

The crowd still had doubts: “If writing is that powerful, won’t people use it to tell lies and tarnish reputations?”

“They will,” Cangjie didn’t dodge the question. “A knife can cut grain and also wound a person; fire can cook food and also burn a house. Writing can preserve truth, but it can also keep falsehood alive longer. So once you learn to write, you must know why you’re recording and who you’re passing it to. Don’t abuse its power just because it’s strong, and don’t abandon it because you might misuse it and revert to the old days of guessing knots.”

After speaking, he carved onto wood the day’s observed grain rain, the night’s ghostly wails, and the people’s questions, one after another. Fear, awe, and the crowd’s debate stayed on the board, while Cangjie remained the patient observer of human traces, the careful maker of characters. The record spread among the people; later readers would know that the grain rain, the ghost cries, and the doubts all happened after the invention of writing.

Late at night the ghostly cries faded. No shadows slipped into the house, no one was hurt; the tremor of heavens and spirits left only a warning: from now on, human experience need not vanish with idle chatter, and deeds become far harder to erase completely.

At dawn the grains on the ground glittered in the rain and dew. The elders called this strange phenomenon “grain rain.” Someone retrieved the wooden slip hidden behind a stone the night before and brushed off the mud; the grain keeper etched what he’d gathered beside the fallen grain, the herbalist noted which grains got damp and couldn’t be stored long, and the mountain‑traveler recorded exactly which trail became slick from the grain rain.

Seeing that writing, even after a night of fear, still circulated in people’s hands, Cangjie realized it was no longer just the joy of invention. People had embraced its benefits and were now shouldering its weight.

The grain rain stopped, and the ghost cries ceased. Writing was not reclaimed by heaven or earth. It rested quietly on wood, stone, and turtle shells, neither deciding good from evil nor sparing anyone from consequences—just keeping what needed to be remembered from sinking away too easily. Cangjie placed the final record back into the crowd’s hands: “From today onward, be careful what you record, careful what you read, and careful what you pass on. Let future generations receive clear, useful knowledge, not an old rope tangled with new riddles.”

After the grain rain, Cangjie deliberately spent several days not carving anything for anyone.

He wanted to see how far writing could travel without him there. The grain keeper alone logged the newly harvested millet and the lent grain, then had someone who’d never entered the granary verify the numbers against the actual sacks—they matched. The traveler handed the fork‑in‑the‑road to a youngster; after the youngster went back and forth, he added the fallen stones from the post‑rain collapse to the end of the original record. The next reader, seeing that, avoided the danger and never again let that crooked tree—mistaken for a signpost—pull a prank three times.

The herbalist carved the bitter herb’s leaf shape, harvest time, and usage onto a wooden slip. An elder read it to the children, who then took the record to identify the plant. Aman, watching from afar, saw the bitter‑shaped mark, twitched his tongue in mock surprise, but didn’t mistake it for a cry. The plant never spoke, yet the ancestors’ knowledge leapt forward a few days and settled firmly in the hands of the next learner.

Aman looked up at the slip and saw the strokes forming a tiny “bitter” character. He chuckled, “So even ‘bitter’ can become a character—talk about finding humor in bitterness.” He handed the slip to a nearby child, who tried to trace the same shape with a finger but turned “bitter” into “cloth.” The child giggled, “I wrote bitter as cloth—can you eat cloth?” Aman shook his head lightly, laughing, “No nonsense, remember it’s bitter herb.”

Later the herbalist wrote the harvesting time as a string of tiny dots, like a timeline. The elder connected the dots into a line, and the children asked, “How did that line appear?” The elder smiled, “It’s the footsteps of time, like the footprints we leave when we walk.” Hearing that, the kids began drawing their own footprints on the wood, trying to record their steps with symbols.

Watching the children’s drawings, Aman recalled Cangjie’s story: people struggled to record events with knotted ropes, Cangjie watched animal tracks and the shapes of all things, organized symbols, and created writing. When heavens and spirits trembled or grain rain and ghost cries echoed, humanity began to log history and knowledge. So he placed his little wooden slip into a basket, ready to take this newfound “writing” back home so more people could try recording their own world.

Others began noting things beyond harvest. A white‑haired elder recounted how a flash flood rerouted the mountain stream and forced the clan to relocate; his granddaughter listened and carved the tale. The next day, a stranger who’d never heard the story retold it from the writing, and the elder only corrected one river direction. A child preserved a lullaby his grandmother taught him; though the carving was crooked, others could still read the order. Deeds done, paths walked, lifesaving herbs, and names that must not be forgotten—all migrated from mouth and ear into a form that could be passed on.

Cangjie would occasionally pass by, just looking, not rewriting. If someone missed a stroke, the reader would ask questions; if someone mixed two things together, a fact-check would reveal the contradiction. Writing didn't make people never make mistakes, but it gave errors a place to be found and corrected. In the past, a single knot could lead to three people winning an argument each; now, if a line of text doesn't make sense, at least everyone is arguing about the same meaning.

Aman sat under a tree, holding the green bamboo slips, and suddenly stared at them for a long time. He had been recording what he saw on the "Twenty Bamboo Slips of Mountains and Seas", often complaining that the bamboo was heavy and the engraving was slow, and grumbling that some slips were as crowded as the Eight Immortals standing on a single magical artifact. It wasn't until now that he realized: being able to complain about "crowded writing" was a luxury he had later on.

If Cangjie hadn't initially seen the connection between shapes and objects in the footprints of birds and beasts, if he hadn't revised those symbols that would misread the sun as something to be eaten, and if he hadn't sealed yesterday's writing to be reread the next day, the green bamboo slips in Aman's hands would be just a bundle of neatly cut bamboo. What really made the "Twenty Bamboo Slips of Mountains and Seas" understandable to later generations wasn't the color of the bamboo or the speed of the engraving knife, but the fact that many people agreed on the same shapes, the same sequence, and the same meaning.

Aman picked up his old pen and wrote on a new slip: "Cangjie observed the footprints of birds and beasts, took the shapes of all things, and created characters to record the human world."

A child nearby came to read it, and when they reached the end, they pointed to a place and asked: "Is this written as 'human world' or 'people idle'?"

Aman looked down and found that he had missed a stroke. He first thought of using the bamboo node's texture to argue, but he swallowed his words and seriously corrected it: "It's 'human world'. If it were written as 'people idle', later generations would think we spend our days sitting around waiting for characters to grow on their own."

The child said: "But you're sitting down right now."

"I'm bearing witness."

"Then write down the witness clearly."

Aman was left speechless by a child who had just learned to read and write, and had no choice but to properly engrave the word "witness". Cangjie, who was listening from the side, laughed and continued on his way, without coming back to help Aman finish.

From then on, the people who recorded grain continued to do so, the people who distinguished roads continued to add to them, the people who knew medicine passed on its uses to those who had never seen the plants, and the elderly passed on past events to their unborn descendants. Cangjie didn't need to stand next to every wooden or stone tablet to explain, and writing could be written, reread, and passed down independently.

Human memory was originally like footprints in the rain, which would be washed away by the next rain; from the time Cangjie created characters, it had a common shape. History and knowledge no longer only resided in someone's mouth, and could cross vast distances and wait out long years. He closed the "Twenty Bamboo Slips of Mountains and Seas", feeling that it wasn't just heavy – every engraving that could be understood by others started from the person who bent down to examine the footprints of birds and beasts.
