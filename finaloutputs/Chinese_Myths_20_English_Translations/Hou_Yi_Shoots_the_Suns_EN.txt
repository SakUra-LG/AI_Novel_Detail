On the day the sun rose ten times, it felt as if someone had lifted the lid of a fiery kiln. Ten suns pressed together on the sky, their beams jostling each other, heat waves pushing heat waves, even the mountain stones steamed and puffed out white vapor. The earth blistered, the people suffered, the furrows split into black cracks, the well rope sank to the bottom but only drew up a half‑pint of scalding water, an old ox stood in the shade of a tree whose shadow was as thin as a rope. The old baker at the village gate lifted his pie to look at it, sighed, and said, “I’m saving on firewood, that’s true, but my stall is about to be taken over by the heavens.”

Aman arrived at that moment, clutching a green bamboo slip. He was the little chronicler who recorded the “Twenty Bamboo Slips of Mountains and Seas” in the Jade Pool. He was supposed to write wherever he went, but today every step felt like it was about to cook. He draped the bamboo over his sleeve, gritting his teeth, and said, “I’m not afraid of heat; I’m just afraid the characters will learn to run away before they’re finished.” A child beside him asked if he could use the bamboo to shade his face, and Aman immediately tightened his grip: “This is for future generations to read, not to block the sun. Besides, if it could shield ten suns, I’d have already offered it to the heavens as a brother.”

The villagers gathered around Hou Yi. Some were so hoarse they could only point at the sky and at the cracked fields. Hou Yi didn’t ask much; he saw a child dozing under a eave, an empty clay pot by the well, and even the birds flying so close to the ground they seemed to be flapping against it. He unstrapped his bow, ran his hand over the string, and checked each arrow feather one by one. The hot wind made the feathers curl slightly; Aman leaned in, wrote carefully “Bow still usable,” and a bead of sweat dripped onto the “can” character, turning it into something that looked like “roasted.” He hurried to add a stroke, murmuring, “Shennong tasted a hundred herbs and I already wrote bitterness as a cry. If the arrow sequence is wrong again, this old pen should punish me first.”

Hou Yi didn’t immediately climb the mountain just because the feathers were still usable. He asked the villagers to bring the few damp cloths left, not to wrap themselves but to lay them inside the arrow pouch, so the feathers wouldn’t keep deforming while scaling the hill. A man brought a clay basin, its bottom only a thin layer of water, and he walked more carefully than if it were full. Hou Yi dipped two fingers in the water, ran them along the bowstring slowly, listening to each note for sameness. The string first gave a dull thud, as if it was too hot to work; he changed his grip and plucked again, and the sound brightened.

Everyone was busy. Some moved the broken stones off the mountain path, others twisted dry, cracked grass rope into thick cords and tied them to the steepest stone steps, giving the people who carried water and arrows a handhold. A boy tried pulling the rope, but the grass rope didn’t budge; instead he sat down in the dust. He got up, patted his pants, and said, “It’s sturdy, tested.” Someone nearby laughed, “You tested the rope, or the ground?” The boy rubbed his backside, still sounding stern, “I tested both; the ground is even sturdier.”

Aman hurried to write, “Villagers united to clear the path.” He felt calm and wanted to add, “The boy personally tested the ground,” but the boy, standing in the crowd, glared at him, “Don’t let the later part be passed on to future generations!” Aman hugged the bamboo slip to his chest and said, “The chronicler only records facts. If you want to change it, you should have sat on the soft earth.” Those words let the tense crowd exhale, and when the laughter faded, everyone went back to moving stones, twisting rope, never forgetting that ten bundles of fire still pressed from above.

Hou Yi reinserted the cleaned arrows into the pouch. He looked over the cleared mountain road, the secured grass rope, and the villagers’ faces scorched by the heat, then nodded to the crowd: “You hold the valley below; I’ll clear the path up there.” No one rushed forward again; they simply gave him the widest stone step. Aman put away his jokes, hugged the bamboo slip tight, and followed. He understood that what Hou Yi carried up the mountain were not just ten arrows, but the little bit of strength each villager had just freed.

At the halfway point, a new crack suddenly split the stone step. The crack wasn’t wide, but it kept spewing heat, and the tip of a shoe would burn if it got too close. Hou Yi didn’t risk leaping over it; he first laid the bow horizontally between two rocks, testing whether the footing would give, then walked along the crack’s edge to the narrower end. Aman mimicked him, stepping out, and his shoe instantly recoiled when it touched the stone: “I’ve already confirmed for future generations that you can’t fry a pancake here—because if you put a pancake down, there’s no time to flip it.”

Hou Yi pointed to a protruding stone and said, “Step there.” Aman lifted his leg as instructed, holding the bamboo slip like a water‑filled basin. He was halfway across when a stone edge snagged his sleeve, and he froze above the crack, neither daring to move forward nor backward. Hou Yi didn’t carry the slip for him; he simply pressed the loose sleeve with his hand, letting Aman step back to safety. Aman steadied himself, looked at the small hole that had been pricked, and solemnly wrote, “First danger of climbing: the mountain snagged my sleeve, perhaps to keep the chronicler as a witness.”

When he made his second leap, he first tightened his cloak, then followed Hou Yi’s footprints to set his feet down, and finally crossed the gap safely. The villagers below, who were holding the rope, saw him raise the green bamboo slip to signal safety, and they all tightened the grass rope a little more, leaving a safer path for those who would come after. Hou Yi and Aman then steadied the slip again before continuing their climb. That brief delay didn’t change his goal, but it added another safe landing spot along the mountain trail.

When Hou Yi heard the remark, he laughed softly. The laugh was faint, like a glimmer of light that briefly flickers over a cracked riverbed, vanishing in an instant. He didn’t move right away; instead he tightened the leather strap of his quiver twice around his waist, his knuckles whitening slightly from the effort, producing a soft “creak.” Then he lifted his head slowly, his gaze sharp as an eagle’s, cutting through the heat waves, staring straight at the ten suns hanging in the sky. The sun was blinding, yet he didn’t even blink, and said in a low voice, “Just remember, no need to worry about me.” Aman nodded immediately, his head bobbing like a chicken pecking at grain. Halfway through, he suddenly lifted his sleeve to wipe sweat, the wet sleeve clinging to his arm, refusing to come off. “I’m not worried,” he said, “I’m just concerned for the bamboo slip. It’s been climbing with you today, doing a lot of work, getting pretty cooked. If we keep roasting it, it’ll turn into a crisp snack. What will I use to record your deeds then? Will I skewer it on a stick?” The others’ hearts tightened, their throats felt like they were packed with fire. Hearing him mutter, they laughed softly. The laugh was light, hoarse, and dry, but like a drop of water rolling over cracked earth, it instantly soothed the tense nerves.

Hou Yi’s lips twitched slightly, then he turned and stepped onto the stone steps. He reached the highest ridge, each step steady and strong, as if the ground beneath his feet were solid earth rather than scorching rock. The mountain wind, heated by the ten suns, blew across his face like a rough hot cloth, searing his skin. Aman followed behind, taking a step, panting, his legs feeling like they were filled with lead; each lift required all his body’s strength. He recorded each stair as a hardship, sweat sliding down his cheeks, dripping onto the stone, turning into white vapor with a “sizzle.” He tried to write “mountain high” solemnly, but his pen trembled, his wrist weakened, and he ended up writing “mountain hot.” He paused, looked at the crooked word, and muttered, “It’s the truth. The mountain really is hot, hot enough to make your heart race.” He looked up at Hou Yi’s back; in the fierce sun it looked lonely and steadfast, like an unshakable peak. Aman hurried to shield the bamboo slip with his sleeve, fearing it would truly cook through.

He took a deep breath, though it was hot, and tried to appear calm, continuing to follow behind, one breath per step, one note per step.

Hou Yi stood still, the rock beneath his feet reddened by the sun. He nocked the first arrow. The ten suns hung in the sky like ten proud fire eyes looking down on the world. The villagers held their breath; Aman did too, but halfway through he remembered he still had to live to write, so he quietly took a breath. The first arrow struck the first sun. Hou Yi pulled the bow taut, his arm veins bulging like a mountain ridge. When the arrow left the string, its scream cut through the heat, straight into the eastern sun. The sun trembled, its fire like a cracked red shell scattering sparks, then trailing a long flame tail as it fell beyond the distant mountain. The heat on the ground eased a bit; some villagers couldn’t help but look up, but halfway they were forced back by the lingering glare. Aman crouched behind a rock and wrote: “First arrow struck the first sun.” After finishing, he blew on the ink, realizing his breath was also hot, so he added solemnly, “This arrow is steady, and I’m very hot.”

The second arrow struck the second sun. Hou Yi didn’t wait for cheers; the second arrow was already nocked. He shifted half a step, avoiding a crack in the rock below, pointing the arrow at the brighter sun to the south. The bowstring snapped, and the sky seemed cut by a black line. After the second sun was hit, it rolled and fell, the hot cloud pulled out a wide crack. An old villager saw one sun missing and stared, asking, “Can’t we keep it from breaking? We need it to roast grain in winter?” Others wanted to laugh, but remembering their own grain shortage, the laughter turned sour. Aman noted the line in the corner, muttering, “People are almost roasted dry, yet they still worry about grain. How they live.”

The third arrow struck the third sun. Hou Yi’s mouth was bleeding from the bowstring, the blood drying instantly in the hot wind. He bit his teeth, his shoulders heavy, and fired the third arrow. It pierced two overlapping golden lights, like carving a path out of a sea of fire. When the third sun was hit, it emitted a low roar, and the falling light painted the valley bright red. Aman saw the blood on Hou Yi’s hand, wanted to write “Hero fearless,” but felt it was too formal, so he changed it to “Hero hurts but stays silent.” Hou Yi glanced at him, and Aman immediately pulled the bamboo slip into his chest: “I’m writing reality, not tearing it down.”

After three days, the first genuine cheers erupted from the foot of the mountain. But the cheers only reached halfway before being pushed back by the remaining seven days. The people finally understood that disaster wasn't just a hot stone that could be moved out of the way; it was like seven drums still drumming away, still pounding away at their heads. A young man supported his elderly mother as they stepped into the shade, but the shade was so small it only covered half of one foot, so he pulled his foot back to let his mother stand further. Aman saw this and paused, erasing the witty remark he had written and simply writing: "Life is suffering, even a small shadow is precious."

Hou Yi didn't turn around. He could hear the cheers and the fear hidden within them. He carefully asked the wind beside him, "Do you still have the strength?" The wind, having been scorched by ten suns' heat, had lost its spirit and only blew his hair slightly. Aman heard this and couldn't help but chime in, "If the wind had the strength, it would have blown me down the mountain to cool off earlier." But he quickly added, "Of course, it's best not to blow, as I still haven't finished writing." Hou Yi was distracted by Aman's joke and his shoulders relaxed for a moment, but he quickly regained his composure. He shot the fourth arrow at the fourth sun. After the first three suns fell, the remaining sun seemed to finally know fear, its flames flickering wildly, but the heat waves grew even more intense, as if it wanted to press people back into the pot before it left. Hou Yi closed his eyes, listened to the wind, and shot the fourth arrow from his fingers, piercing through a fold of the turbulent flames. The fourth sun was pierced, and the light scattered into a million red scales, falling behind a distant mountain. Aman counted along, writing "four" when his pen slipped, and he quickly pressed it with his sleeve, "Don't run around, you're just a number, not the one being shot."

The flames from the fourth sun swept over the mountain slope, and the grass ropes tied earlier were snapped by the hot wind, making a loud snapping sound. The people guarding the mountain path immediately crouched down, using stones to press down the knots, so the ropes wouldn't swing out to the steep slope. A child mimicked the adults, hugging a stone and almost flattening himself. When the wind passed, he looked up with a face full of ash, asking seriously, "Did I press it down?" The adult looked at the stone, which was still and hadn't moved, and then at the child's two thin arms, "You pressed it down, at least you didn't fly away."

The child thought this was a reasonable answer and got up, wanting to find an even bigger stone. Aman called out from across the mountain slope, "Don't move it! If you roll down with the stone, I'll have to rewrite you." The child immediately hid his hands behind his back, as if the stone hadn't been his to begin with. Hou Yi didn't turn around, but he raised his left hand, signaling everyone to stay in the leeward position. He himself moved to a rocky outcropping, where there was no rope to hold onto, and rocks continued to fall from the ground.

The fifth arrow couldn't be shot from its original position. The fire clouds from the fourth sun were rolling over from the west, and if shot directly into the turbulent flow, the arrow's tail would veer off course. Hou Yi knelt down and picked up three pebbles, throwing them in different directions. The first one fell straight down, the second one was blown back to his feet, and the third one flew off to the east at an angle. Aman understood and wrote "wind blowing east," but when he looked at the word, he saw that he had drawn it like a crawling insect. He glanced at Hou Yi and then at the word, quietly adding two legs, "Now it at least knows which way to crawl."

Hou Yi moved seven steps east along the rocky outcropping, each step waiting for the falling rocks to stop rolling before he stepped down. When he drew his bow again, the laughter from below had stopped. The people held their breath, shielding the ropes, and the child also covered his mouth, peeking through the gaps. The earlier jokes hadn't made anyone forget the danger; instead, they had helped everyone find their correct position. Hou Yi listened as the wind evenly swept past the arrow's feathers, and only then did he string the fifth arrow.

The child beside him was holding his laughter so tightly that he let out a hiccup, and Aman said sternly, "Be serious, if I make a mistake in counting, the people in the future will have to endure an extra day of heat."

The fifth arrow shot down the fifth sun. Hou Yi lowered his bow arm and let out a long breath. His shadow on the rock was stretched out extremely short, as if it too was reluctant to stay in the heat. The fifth sun hung in the middle of the sky, with the light at its strongest, piercing the eyes of the onlookers. Hou Yi suddenly took a step to the left, using the mountain ridge to stabilize the arrow's tail, and the arrow light flew up, like an eagle that refused to close its eyes. When the fifth sun was shot down, people at the well's edge cried out in shock, and someone even felt a hint of moisture on the well's wall. Aman quickly jotted it down and whispered, "The fifth sun fell, and the well took a breath. I want to breathe too, but I'm still waiting in line."

after the fifth sun fell, a chill seeped out from a crack in the mountain slope. It was so light that it felt like someone had quietly pressed a thin leaf against a boiling pot, yet it was enough to remind everyone that the world hadn’t completely fallen apart yet. A little girl, clutching a clay bowl, ran up the mountain. The bowl held only two sips of water; halfway up she spilled one, and she was so frantic she almost cried. Aman waved her off, “Don’t cry. If you spill the water, we can still write a note; if you cry, I’ll have trouble telling which drop is more precious.” The girl was stunned by his words, burst out laughing, and handed the remaining sip to Hou Yi. Hou Yi didn’t take it. He glanced at the girl’s cracked lips and simply said, “Keep it for yourself.” The girl hugged the bowl to her chest, her eyes red. When Aman recorded that line, his fingers steadier than before. He suddenly understood that shaking beside the green bamboo slips wasn’t just to capture the noise of the day, but to remember these tiny, tiny choices. Otherwise future generations would only know that nine suns fell, but never that someone had handed the last drop of water to a hero, or that the hero had pushed it back into the world. The sixth arrow fell on the sixth sun.

After shooting five arrows, Hou Yi’s arm began to tremble, and each vibration of the bowstring felt like a hammer on bone. The villagers tried to urge him to rest, but the sun still burned in the sky, and no one could say “take a break.” Hou Yi chuckled, “I can still hold it.” Aman immediately wrote down “I can still hold it,” then looked up and asked, “Is that the truth, or are you saying it because you’re afraid we’ll cry?” Hou Yi didn’t answer. The sixth arrow already left the string, slicing through the swirling white light. On the sixth sun, the smoke from the ground fissures slowly fell, as if finally someone had put a lid on its temper. after the sixth sun fell, the sky finally gained layers. It was once a fierce white, now a faint hint of blue appeared within it—so pale that it made the people’s eyes linger. Someone reached out to grab that sliver of blue, but of course they could catch nothing but a palmful of sweat. Aman saw this and wrote earnestly, “Someone tried to scoop the sky with their hand, failed, but the attitude was commendable.” An elder beside him asked if he should record it too, and Aman nodded, “Yes. If people in the disaster still do foolish things, it means their hearts haven’t hardened by the sun.”

Hou Yi took a breath to steady himself. He relaxed the bowstring, then tightened it again, as if negotiating with his own bones. Every bone seemed ready to haggle, but he had no room to bargain. Aman, watching from the side, turned pale and touched the green bamboo slip. “Do you hear it?” he whispered. “His bones are about to start arguing. Don’t make a noise, or I’ll think you’re cracking.” The bamboo slip, of course, didn’t answer; only the edges of the bamboo pieces brushed against each other in the hot wind, producing a faint tinkling sound. Aman immediately pressed it down, “Okay, okay, you’re also upset. Go back and find some shade for yourself.”

At that moment, the air was so hot that even breathing felt like it carried sparks. Hou Yi’s sweat slid down his cheeks, evaporating before it could hit the ground, leaving behind white salt stains that looked like miniature maps. He took a deep breath, his chest heaving wildly, as if he were trying to suck in all the surrounding heat and then force it out. Aman felt his eyebrows curl like shrimp shells. He fanned himself with his sleeve while fiercely protecting the bamboo slip in his arms, terrified that the precious thing would also overheat and break. “Aman,” Hou Yi’s voice was hoarse, like sandpaper on stone, “don’t tremble. If the slip tilts, the characters won’t be straight.”

Aman froze for a moment, looked down at his slightly trembling hand from nerves, then at Hou Yi’s steady, mountain‑like back, and whispered to himself, “I’m shaking from heat, not from fear. If I were afraid, I’d have already hidden in the stone cracks, not here acting as your scribe.” After saying that, he glanced at Hou Yi, noticing a faint smile at the corner of his mouth, which eased his mind a little, but his hand didn’t stop moving. He kept fanning the bamboo slip with his sleeve, muttering, “If this slip could talk, it would surely be cursing this damned weather.”

Hou Yi fell silent, simply pulling the bow tighter. The bow body emitted a low hum, as if protesting the immense tension. Aman’s heart hammered in his chest. He remembered the power of the previous arrows and knew this one could not be missed. He gripped the bamboo slip tightly, his knuckles whitening, and whispered to himself, “Don’t break, don’t break.”

At that moment, a surge of heat hit him, nearly knocking him off his feet. He staggered, almost falling, but caught a rock in time. He dusted himself off and, pretending calm, said, “It’s fine, I’m steady. The stone is just too hot; it almost stuck to my shoe sole.” He looked down at his shoe, saw a small blackened patch, and sighed, “Looks like the stone wants a sunbath too.”

Hou Yi seemed to have heard Aman's complaints, and he slightly tilted his head, a faint smile flashing in his eyes. That smile was subtle, yet it was like a refreshing spring that instantly doused Aman's anxiety. Aman was taken aback for a moment, then looked down and quickly wrote on the bamboo slips: "Hou Yi smiled, though faintly, yet it was cool." He finished writing, looked up at the sky, and the remaining sun was still blazing, but he felt a newfound determination in his heart. He knew that Hou Yi's every breath, every draw of the bow, was for this land, for these suffering people. Aman took a deep breath, adjusted his posture, and prepared to record the glory of the next arrow. He gripped the bamboo slips tightly, his eyes fixed intently on Hou Yi's bow, and whispered to himself: "Come on, the seventh arrow, it has to be precise."

The remaining four suns were like four fierce beasts that refused to concede, their light intersecting and obscuring each other. The people couldn't see their positions, only that the sky was in chaos. Hou Yi, however, saw clearly. He knew that the seventh arrow couldn't be rushed, the eighth arrow couldn't be reckless, and the ninth arrow couldn't be driven by hatred. If the arrow was filled with anger, it would eventually set the world ablaze. Aman heard him say "can't be driven by hatred" and was taken aback for a moment, then quietly wrote: "The arrow has to be precise, and so does the heart." He finished writing and looked at the six characters, not a single smile on his face, only a small, skewed dot drawn beside it as his signature. The seventh arrow struck down the seventh sun. The remaining four suns simultaneously blazed with intense light, as if using their last burst of arrogance to intimidate him. Hou Yi's forehead veins pulsed, and the rocks beneath his feet were pounded by sweat, creating small, dark spots. As the seventh arrow was drawn, the mountain wind suddenly counterattacked, making Aman's bamboo slips almost fly out of his hands.

He first hugged the bamboo slips, then remembered to hug himself, muttering to himself: "This is my job, clearly defined – first, protect the text, and then protect the text's author." Hou Yi's hands didn't falter, and the arrow tip slightly adjusted before returning to its precise position. The seventh sun was struck down, and the hot wind rolled back, rustling the people's clothing and making their hopes shine brighter, yet unable to dispel them. The eighth arrow struck down the eighth sun. Hou Yi changed his stance, pressed his shoulders, and steadied his wrist, his movements slower but more stable than before. Dark blue crevices appeared in the sky, like the edges of a red-hot pot finally cracking open a bit of coolness. The eighth sun cunningly retreated into the shadows of the other two light circles, and the people could only see a dazzling white, unable to tell which one to aim for. A child counted the fireballs in the sky with his fingers, but when he got to halfway, he let out a belch. Aman corrected him sternly: "It's not three and a half, it's three." Hou Yi's arrow had already flown out, flashing suddenly in the place where light and shadow intersected, and the eighth sun fell from the clouds, as if someone had quietly opened a door in the sky. The ninth arrow struck down the ninth sun. The last two suns hung in the air, one still blazing fiercely, the other, however, seemed to have been intimidated by the previous eight arrows, its light somewhat subdued. Hou Yi knew that the true weight wasn't the arrows shot, but what was left after shooting. He strung the ninth arrow, his fingers numb, yet his joints steady, like they were carved into the bow. Aman originally wanted to say something witty, but the words got stuck in his throat, and he only pressed the bamboo slips against his knee, the pen tip frozen in place. The arrow flew, and the ninth sun was pierced, the fire exploding in the air before quickly fading. The distant mountains finally showed their clear outlines, and the people saw that only one sun remained in the sky, but no one immediately cheered, everyone waiting for Hou Yi's next move. He looked at the nine lines on the bamboo slips, and suddenly felt that each line was like an arrow pressing down on his knee. He used to complain that the bamboo slips were heavy, but now he realized that some weights weren't the weight of the bamboo, but the weight of human lives, the land, and tomorrow. Hou Yi pulled out the last arrow from the quiver, then stopped. The arrow's feather quivered slightly in the hot wind, like it was also waiting for an answer. Aman saw this scene and felt a pang in his heart, almost asking aloud, "Will he shoot again?" He finished writing and wanted to smile, but the smile hung on his lips, not daring to fall. Because Hou Yi's face didn't show a victorious smile, only a deeper contemplation than drawing the bowstring. After Hou Yi shot the ninth sun, the world didn't immediately calm down. The last sun hung in the air, its light softened, yet still made people feel uneasy. Someone shouted, "There's one left!" and someone whispered, "Is it because he's missing one arrow?" The crowd’s voices split quickly into two groups. One side urged “shoot now,” fearing that the last sun might only be a temporary lull; the other side stared at the already darkened fields, unsure where the seed would sprout after ten suns had vanished. Aman lowered his head to look at his record. The first nine lines were “draw the bow, aim, release, the sun falls,” written in a clear order even if it was risky. By the tenth line, his pen hovered in midair, unsure which word to write first. The last sun seemed to feel the mountain’s silence too, and the light dimmed a layer more. People who had been curled under eaves in the distance slowly lifted their heads, no longer squinting from the sting; a fallen wheat shoot was lifted by a breeze, half a leaf unfurled, its veins still dry but no longer smoking. Hou Yi saw this tiny change and his breathing steadied. Hou Yi’s arm shook, the bowstring trembled, and the people’s hearts shook along. Once that arrow left, all the scorching would end, but every dawn and dusk would be shot down together. Aman also tried, and immediately wrote seriously, “The last sun’s temper has changed, it won’t bite for now.” After finishing, he felt the line was too solemn and wanted to delete it. Hearing that, Aman was stunned. He had always thought the “Twenty Bamboo Slips of Mountains and Seas” should be written with solemnity, each word like a monk in a temple. But today he saw people crying in disaster and laughing in disaster; he saw Hou Yi’s hand tremble in pain yet still accept a casual note. He suddenly understood that humor isn’t about turning suffering into lightness, but that people in hardship are still not flattened. So he didn’t delete the line “won’t bite for now,” but added four small characters beside it: “still respectful after a laugh.”

Down below, people began to stand upright. The first to straighten was a child, his cheeks flushed from the sun, yet he held his waist like a newly sprouted sapling. The people first froze, then some lifted their heads to test, finding the light no longer a blade but a hand that finally understood limits. The hot wind turned into a warm breeze, the well’s mouth filled again, cracks on the ridge seeped moist earth, and a few dead grasses slowly unfurled their curled tips. An old man touched the well water, so moved he scooped a handful onto his face, then remembered the water was scarce and awkwardly stopped his hand midair. Aman wanted to laugh, but only let out a soft chuckle: “Save some energy; this one of yours costs more than all my writing.”

Hou Yi leaned against a rock, his arm shaking so much that he could barely hold his bow. The villagers gathered around, eager to thank him, but also afraid of disturbing his injury. Hou Yi caught his breath for a while before looking up and saying, "Don't thank me yet, who has water, lend me a sip?" The crowd was taken aback, and then laughter and tears erupted together. Some people handed him water, while others offered him a half-dried biscuit they had pulled out from under a rock. Some people awkwardly tried to fan him, but when they realized the air was still hot, they blushed and put their hands down. Aman stood by, taking notes: "After saving the people, the first thing a hero wants is not to be worshiped, but to drink water. This is completely believable."

The water was eventually shared among the crowd, and Hou Yi only took a small sip before returning the rest. The young boy who had handed him the water grew anxious, saying, "This is for you!" Hou Yi shook his head, saying, "I just need to be able to stand up, you all need to go back to work." The boy held the bowl, unsure of what to say, and finally just nodded vigorously. Aman watched the exchange and started to write a grand phrase, but then stopped himself. He thought that grand phrases were too smooth and might gloss over the difficulties, so he rewrote it: "Water is scarce, words are scarce, but the heart is not."

The villagers didn't wait for the land to recover on its own. Before the sun had completely set, a few people were already walking along the dry watercourse, searching for the places where the rocks had blocked the flow. Others were placing broken buckets at the edge of the well, first patching the smallest cracks with wet mud. The man who had previously tried to fix the leaky bucket had applied too much mud, and when he lifted it, the entire patch fell off onto his foot. He stood there for a moment, then announced, "This was just a test for my foot, the second try will be for the bucket."

The people around him laughed and handed him a piece of broken pottery to scrape off the excess mud. The man didn't try to be stubborn this time, and after scraping off the mud, he poured in half a bowl of water to test for leaks. The water swirled around at the bottom of the bucket, and eventually, none of it leaked out. The crowd's laughter turned into a brief cheer, lighter than when they had watched the sun set, but just as real. Aman wanted to record it, but he realized he had been holding his pen behind his ear, and his hand was actually grasping a dry blade of grass. He stared at the grass for a moment, then nonchalantly tucked it behind the other ear: "One person handles the important tasks, the other person handles the trivial ones, clear division of labor."

The children were responsible for collecting the scattered wooden pegs from the well. One child brought over three stones that were completely useless for blocking the leak, but he was confident saying they "looked particularly good for stopping water." The adults didn't laugh at him, but just asked him to arrange the stones in order of size. When they finally found the useful one at the end, the child was so proud that his nose pointed straight up, but when he turned around, he tripped over his own stones and had to jump three times to steady himself. He immediately scolded the stones, "Just stay in line, don't stick out your feet!" Even Hou Yi couldn't help but laugh.

Hou Yi sat on the rock, slowly regaining feeling in his arm, and told the crowd which sections of the watercourse to block off and which wells to temporarily seal to prevent landslides. He didn't finish the tasks himself, and couldn't finish them, but he explained the layout of the land to the villagers, and they each took on a small task. Some people cleared the watercourse, others patched the buckets, others harvested their crops, and others helped elderly people back to their homes. The crowd, which had previously only been able to look up and wait, was soon busy on the ground.

Aman watched the crowd's busy movements and added a line to his notes: "The arrow stopped the disaster, but the daily life was picked up by the people." He read the phrase to himself, thinking it was a rare, genuine sentence, and was about to feel proud when the man who had been patching the buckets called out from a distance, "Historian, if you have time, come and stomp on the mud!" Aman quickly bent down to look busy: "Who said I had time? The green bamboo slips can be my witness, I'm so busy I even used a blade of grass as a pen."

The night slowly crept up from behind the mountains, like a thick layer of gray gauze. In the past, people had almost forgotten what night looked like, but now that they saw the darkness spreading across the sky, they felt a sense of fear. A child clung to his mother's clothes and asked, "What do we do when it's dark?" The mother choked back a sob, and Hou Yi looked up, his eyes shining in the fading light, and said, "When it's dark, we sleep, and tomorrow will be bright again." This was a normal phrase, but in this moment, it felt like a new promise. Aman quickly recorded it, then muttered to himself, "I guess normal days are really that great, great enough to make people cry with just one sentence about going to bed early."

The wind finally cooled, no longer a scorching fire that could roast flesh. Hou Yi slowly lowered the bow that had once stretched to a full moon, and the “crack” of the relaxed string sounded especially crisp in the silent valley. He exhaled deeply, the breath carrying the sweat and dust of the day. Villagers gathered around, but kept their distance, as if the sun’s lingering heat still clung to him. Aman, clutching a bamboo slip, trembled in his calves—not from fear, but because the steep path he’d climbed with Hou Yi had nearly splashed his freshly mixed ink all over him. He stole a glance at Hou Yi and saw the hero wiping the black dust from his face with a sleeve, looking a bit disheveled yet unmistakably real. “Hou Yi, sir,” an old farmer whispered, his voice rough as sandpaper, “that… that last one really won’t come back to cause trouble?”

Hou Yi turned, his gaze sweeping over the scorched earth and the surviving villagers, and softened. “If it dares to misbehave, I still have arrows.” He said it calmly, but with undeniable authority. Aman nodded, thinking to himself that this was far more solid than the empty slogans of others. He glanced at his bamboo slip, where the words “Sun‑shot Success” were crookedly written, and a little sun had been accidentally doodled beside it, looking like a mischievous grin. He hurried to cover it with his sleeve, fearing Hou Yi would laugh at his messy calligraphy. Children began to cautiously step out from their cover, their little hands catching moonlight leaking through the leaves. The moonlight was cool and gentle, washing away the day’s heat and fear. A chubby kid stepped on a sun‑baked stone that had cooled but still hurt, shouting, “Ouch! This stone still holds a grudge!” The adults paused, then burst into a long‑suppressed laugh. The laughter started soft, like wind over dry grass, then grew louder and brighter, dispelling the last trace of gloom. Aman watched, his pen pausing on the bamboo slip.

He wanted to write grand words like “All the world rejoices” or “All things revive,” but seeing those real smiles, he felt those phrases were too airy. So he dipped his brush again and, stroke by stroke, wrote, “Night falls, people laugh, and life must go on.” After finishing, he looked at the lone, quiet sun on the horizon, slowly sinking behind the distant mountains, no longer raging but quietly guarding the land. Aman yawned, rubbed his sore neck, and thought, “Tomorrow I still have to get up early, but at least tomorrow will be a good day.”

When the heat faded, many things that were previously invisible appeared on the mountain. A half‑rooted grass sprouted in a stone crack, a cracked but intact moss stain clung to the rock wall, and a few round stones in the distant stream bed glistened wet. Aman squatted, touched a stone, and found it no longer hot, so he solemnly bowed to it. A child next to him asked why he was bowing to a stone. Aman answered straight‑forwardly, “It didn’t scorch me into a jump today, so it deserves a bow.” The child laughed, bending down, and Hou Yi chuckled softly. That laugh was light, yet more reassuring than any day‑long cheer. Evening finally took on its proper shape. The lone sun slanted across the land, its light gentle, the distant mountains showing layered green shadows. Children ran out from under the eaves, chased each other by the stream, their laughter bouncing off the stones. The crops were still yellow, but no longer seemed doomed to die immediately; people gathered seeds back into clay jars, straightened the well ropes, and nudged overturned barrels upright.

Hou Yi stood on the ridge watching all this, pale but steady. Aman touched the green bamboo slip, confirming it was no longer hot, before he dared to write the final lines more neatly. The villagers began to inventory what could still survive: half a bucket of water, three bags of seeds, two skinny oxen, a patch of leaf that hadn’t been fully scorched, and a group of people who had just learned to speak loudly again. Some reported these to Aman, who listened, then finally said, “Are you asking me to write the aftermath, or to open the granary?” An elder laughed, patting him on the shoulder, “Write both, so future generations won’t think the hero’s single shot solved everything.” Aman, no longer joking, solemnly added to the bamboo slip, “Life still needs people to lift it up slowly.” When Hou Yi descended the mountain, the villagers wanted to help him, but he waved them off, saying he could still walk. Just three steps later, his knee wobbled honestly. Aman, quick as a flash, steadied his bow without touching the quiver, only holding the loosened bow body: “I declare I’m holding the bow, not boosting your swagger.”

Hou Yi looked down at him, a rare smile spreading across his face: "Well, thanks to this little clerk who's only holding the bow." Aman immediately stiffened his face, as if his ears hadn't just turned red: "No need to thank me, holding the bow is a hazardous job – what if it gets annoyed with my sweaty hands?"

Down the mountain road, the little girl who had first brought water came running again, holding up the clay bowl for Hou Yi to see. The water at the bottom of the bowl was almost gone, but it reflected the only sun in the sky. She asked, "Will it come back every day, just one sun?" Hou Yi looked at the gentle glow of the sun and said, "When it's time, it will come." Aman chimed in, "If it comes back too often, I'll ask for a sick leave from writing on green bamboo slips." Everyone laughed. After they finished laughing, the little girl hugged the clay bowl to her chest, as if it was a new, clear tomorrow. As the crowd dispersed, a thin stream of smoke rose from the foot of the mountain, like a gentle mist. The smoke no longer stood straight like a white flag, but curved slowly, like a person who's finally able to stretch after being exhausted. Aman watched it and suddenly felt like adding this too. Because myths aren't just about heroes drawing their bows, but also about the first pot of soup simmering again after a disaster. The sound was small, but it sounded more like victory than any loud noise during the day. Aman listened and suddenly felt his stomach rumble honestly, so he counted that too as evidence of the world recovering. Aman held the green bamboo slips and wrote "shoot nine, leave one" in The Twenty Bamboo Slips of Mountains and Seas.

He added another serious sentence: "Hou Yi shot down nine days, leaving one day, and the world returned to normal, with all things revived." After writing this sentence, he stared at the four characters for a long time, and suddenly felt that they were heavier than the previous nine arrows. The nine arrows saved people, and one day preserved the morning of the world to come. Aman didn't immediately make a joke, but instead closed the bamboo slips, as if he was afraid to disturb the peace that had just returned to the earth. But the quiet only lasted for a short while. When Aman inserted this slip into The Twenty Bamboo Slips of Mountains and Seas, he still couldn't resist adding a small note in the margin: "Hou Yi's side has too many suns, so many that he almost burned the characters; if it's ever Kuafu's turn to chase the sun, I'll soak the bamboo slips first, so the characters don't get too dry before I even start writing." After finishing, he glanced at Hou Yi and whispered, "I remember this passage – the hardest part isn't shooting down the extra ones, but knowing which one to leave."

Hou Yi didn't turn back, but kept looking at the gentle sun. The wind blew from the foot of the mountain, finally carrying a hint of moisture, and blew over the bamboo slips, as well as the smoke from the countless households that had just recovered from the disaster.
