The first time Kuafu announced he wanted to chase the sun, his tribe was hurrying to pull the grain off the stones before night fell.

The sun was already slipping westward, and mountain shadows stretched into the valley mouth. Children clutched their grain baskets and ran home, while elders urged them to hurry, because as soon as dusk fell, distant beasts and the rugged mountain paths would slip into darkness. Kuafu stood on a high ridge, watching the sun light the fields each day and then rush away again.

“Why does it always go west?” he asked.

An old man answered, “The sun walks its own road, and people live their own days. If you think night comes too fast, harvest your grain earlier.”

“What if I could catch it?”

The elder paused his work. “Then first ask your own shadow if it’s willing. It’s been following you from dawn to dusk and is already tired enough.”

Kuafu looked down, and his long shadow was already stretching over a few grain baskets. The kids quickly moved the baskets aside, fearing the shadow might crush the grain and leave a giant’s outline instead.

The joke didn’t deter Kuafu. He kept watching the sun, memorizing its rise in the east and set in the west; then he climbed the surrounding hills to see where the western road led. The sun gives the world light and heat, and it also firmly separates day from night. Kuafu didn’t want to spend his whole life guessing where the sun went after sunset—he wanted to walk right to the place where it set and see if a person could ever catch that beam of light.

The tribe tried to counsel him: a giant may have big steps, but the world is bigger. Someone suggested waiting on a high mountain for the sun to return—at least he’d save his shoes. Another said he could shout, “Slow down!” to the sun; if it didn’t hear him, there’d still be time to chase later.

Kuafu didn’t argue. He whittled a sturdy wooden staff, tested it on rocks and mud to make sure it could bear climbing and long travel. He packed just enough food for the first stretch and stored water in a skin bag. He didn’t count miles, didn’t use any magical device to measure the sun’s speed, and didn’t recruit anyone to share the chase.

“If you catch it, will you bring the sun back?” a child asked.

“If one person could bring the sun home, everything else would fall into darkness,” Kuafu replied. “I want to catch up to it, not lock it at my doorstep.”

“Then what will you say to it?”

“First catch it, then think about the words.”

The child thought that was an honest plan, except the first part was a bit… huge.

The night before he set out, Kuafu walked the paths around the village once more. Hearth fires went out one by one, and darkness closed in over the valleys. He heard people inside planning tomorrow’s work, and he heard the wind whistling through trees where there was no light. Chasing the sun wasn’t a momentary temper tantrum with the sky; it was his stubborn quest to probe the very edge of light.

He also strapped the water skin to his shoulder and his waist for a test run. The shoulder bag bounced with each stride, the waist one stayed steadier, so he tightened the waist strap again. When the tribe saw him fuss over even a single water skin, they finally understood that “chasing the sun” wasn’t a post‑meal boast—it was a real, foot‑long journey.

At first light, the sun’s ray crept over the eastern peaks, and Kuafu gripped his staff tightly. He didn’t wait for the sun to climb overhead, nor did he hold a long farewell. With each footfall the ground gave a light tremor, and his massive silhouette crossed the edge of the village lands.

The sun moved slowly westward ahead of him, and Kuafu took long strides across the earth. The tribe watched him shrink into the distance until only dust rose on the open plain. Kuafu’s first step toward chasing the sun had landed, and from that moment every question would have to be answered by the road, the heat, and his own strength.

As Kuafu ran across the tribe’s plain, the sun had just slipped past the eastern mountain peaks.

He leapt over field ridges, and the river ditches below looked like shallow lines under his feet. At first some villagers followed, hoping to see him off to the old hunting grounds; halfway there they were left only with waving hands. Aman clutched a green bamboo slip and kept at it the longest, running while scribbling, “Kuafu heads west, strides huge, dust… the recorder can still—”

Kuafu’s next step crossed a ditch, and his silhouette was already far ahead.

The young man lowered his head and finished the sentence: “…still see it.”

He caught up to the ditch’s edge, then leapt over a stretch of forest. The bamboo slips slapped each other in his arms, as eager as their owner. He intended to note every step Kuafu took, only to discover he’d etched three long lines that looked both like a road and like the distance Kuafu had left behind him.

“Wait!” he shouted.

Far behind, Kuafu turned, thinking danger lurked. The youngster, panting, leaned on his leg and said, “No danger—I just wanted to check whether you have to chase the sun all in one day. The sun will be back tomorrow.”

“It comes tomorrow, and it goes today,” Kuafu answered, then kept heading west.

He watched the gap widen again and had to admit he didn’t even know if the sun ever gets tired; Kuafu clearly wasn’t planning to think about the recorder’s comfort. Light‑hearted talk stayed in the early stretch; the road under his feet grew serious fast.

Scrub and loose stones forced Kuafu to adjust his stride. If he took winding paths, the sun kept slipping west; if he rushed straight ahead, he’d break branches and scatter beasts. Kuafu used his staff to brush aside dead wood, leapt over crossable gullies, and skirted any nests or human dwellings. He was chasing the sun, not carving a giant‑only highway through the land.

One gorge was too wide to jump in a single bound. Kuafu laid his staff across the banks, swung his body onto the opposite side, then pulled the staff back in one smooth motion. When the followers later reached the gorge, they could only see the deep gouge left by the staff tip, confirming he hadn’t changed direction. That single crossing added distance and left a trace for others to verify.

Extra rope and thick hide on his shoulders began to slow him down. He left a bundle of warm hide at a stone shelter for later travelers, keeping only the water skin, food, and staff. Lightening his load, his pace quickened again.

The young man raced to the stone shelter following the giant footprints, expecting Kuafu to be resting there. Inside he found only a thick hide. He turned to the green bamboo slip and said, “Good news—caught his clothing. Bad news—clothing won’t let me visit.”

He recorded the direction and footprints on the slip and kept on. From then on Kuafu’s footprints grew farther apart; each landing left a clear dent. On a hard patch he lost his trace, so he climbed a rise to look for kicked‑up dust. By the time he figured it out, Kuafu had already passed the edge of the plain.

The sun was still ahead, its light shifting from pale morning to full blaze. Kuafu didn’t pause to re‑swear an oath now that he’d left the village, nor did he stop to wait for companions. He ran into the empty wilderness; the first leg was over, but the chase had only covered the easiest part.

Aman finally lost Kuafu completely for the first time. He carved “Kuafu entered the western wilds” onto a green bamboo slip, his finger trembling over the knife marks as if afraid the characters might slip away. When he finished, he sat down to catch his breath, leaning against a jutting rock, sweat beads rolling down his forehead like tiny pearl rain. In the distance, Kuafu’s silhouette was already swallowed by a scorching halo, the dust he kicked up swirling into golden whirlpools in the wind.

Aman looked up and saw Kuafu’s long robe glittering under the blazing sun, like a giant burning snake. The snake would lift its head now and then, its mouth seeming to shout, “Sun, hurry up, let me catch you!” Aman couldn’t help but laugh, muttering, “If he drags the sun home, we’ll be sun‑baked every day!” Before he finished, Kuafu had already left deep footprints on a sand dune, some of the stones in the prints turned red‑hot, like roasted chestnuts.

Aman stood up, but the scorching ground made his steps wobble; he braced against the rock, trying not to turn into a charred stick himself. He kept following Kuafu’s footprints, the sand sizzling softly with each step as if mocking his sluggish pace. “My speed would make a snail laugh,” he whispered. In his haste he knocked the bamboo slip against a stone, a thin crack spider‑webbing across the edge, as if the sun’s heat had baked a tiny bite into it. He quickly brushed the ink off the crack, muttering, “Even this slip wants to chase the sun with me—talk about bad luck.”

He kept tracking, his steps growing heavier, the hot air like a tangible rope tightening around his chest. Kuafu had vanished ahead, leaving only rolling heat waves that rose like massive tides. Aman looked up at the sky, saw the sun hanging high, its glare cutting like a knife, and grumbled, “This sun is ruthless—won’t even leave me a shadow.” He clutched the bamboo slip to his chest, afraid the wind might snatch it away.

Just then, a low, distant cry rang out—Kuafu’s voice, it seemed: “Wei River! Wei River!” Aman followed the sound and saw a wide river not far ahead, its surface glittering silver under the sun. Kuafu had already rushed to the bank, leapt forward, and gulped a huge mouthful of water, splashing everywhere as if he’d set the entire river ablaze. Aman stared in disbelief, then burst out laughing, “He looks like he’s adding firewood to the sun!” But right after he finished drinking, Kuafu’s brow was still furrowed, his thirst unchanged, even more exhausted than before.

Aman kept following, his pace slowing. The gravel under his feet was scorched by the hot air, feeling like stepping on a grill’s iron plate. His lips cracked, his tongue stuck to the roof of his mouth, and even his breathing hissed faintly. He muttered under his breath, “This thirst is worse than chasing the sun.” He looked up at Kuafu, who was staggering at the edge of the marsh, his wooden staff glowing faintly as if it were about to ignite the soil beneath his feet.

At last, something changed on the horizon.

a line of mountains stretched across the end of the plain.

The sun had risen higher, glaring down on the rocks. A detour would take a gentle valley, but it’d veer far south; going straight over the ridge would be shorter, but the slope and hot stones would sap more energy. Kuafu paused at the foot of the mountain, took a few sips from his water pouch, never gulping it all at once.

He chose to climb the ridge. First he lit the fissure with his staff, then stepped onto a sturdy stone platform. The giant’s size let him leap higher, but also made the loose stones more likely to tumble under his weight. Kuafu avoided the beasts and trees below, preferring to skirt a sheer cliff so the chase wouldn’t turn into a disaster along the way.

Before noon, the rocks were so hot that even lizards hid in the shade. Kuafu’s shadow shrank to his feet, as if the long shadow that had come from his homeland didn’t want to move further in the sun. His staff struck stone with a deep thud, each one slower than the last.

He paused under the shaded cliff, not to give up but to let his rapid breathing calm, so his next step could still be steady. His water pouch had lightened by half, and he’d only eaten a small bite. Wind from above blew in, carrying a dry western scent.

Kuafu looked up at the sun. When he was in the valley, the peaks seemed to block it; once he reached the ridge, the sun appeared whole again in the west. The obstacle didn’t stop the sun—it just made the chaser spend more time. He used his staff to push himself up and kept climbing toward the highest point.

A mountain hawk had been circling above for a long time, as if it wanted to see why the giant wasn’t taking the usual path. When Kuafu crossed the ridge, the hawk instead landed on the rock where he had rested, as if taking over a post it didn’t understand.

The slope on the west side was steeper than the way he’d come. Kuafu couldn’t just rush down by weight, or he’d lose his legs before reaching the open plain. He braced his staff against the rock, controlling each landing spot. A few shards of stone rolled to the bottom of the ditch, dust followed him down, but it didn’t topple him.

After descending, the sun had clearly tilted westward. The mountains didn’t block the whole path, but they left the morning’s vigor behind. Kuafu’s shoulders were soaked in sweat, his mouth was drying, and his water pouch was down to the last drop.

He looked back at the ridge and saw the fallen stones blocking a beast’s path, so he turned back a few steps and used his staff to pry them aside. The sun moved a bit further west. Kuafu didn’t think of the chase as a chance to crush everything; this turn made him realize that every choice was draining the chaser, not the sun.

Ahead lay a wider scorched land, no tall trees, few streams. Sunlight fell on the pale ground and reflected back, as if the sun was not only in the sky but also laying a hot blanket beneath his feet.

The mountain wind here was dry. Kuafu held the last drop of water in his mouth, didn’t swallow immediately, waited for his breathing to calm, then drank. A brief restraint didn’t erase the heat ahead, but it let him step steadily into the plain instead of exhausting himself at the slope’s foot.

Kuafu finished the water, tied the empty pouch to his waist instead of tossing it at the foot of the mountain. He knew he’d be thirstier ahead, but he also saw the sun getting closer to the horizon. It wasn’t that he had finally caught up; rather, both were inching forward and backward, pushing the chase to its toughest moment.

He gripped his staff again and entered the western plain. The mountains behind him faded into heat haze, the goal stayed the same; what changed was Kuafu himself. The early burst of speed had turned into a struggle against heat, fatigue, and dwindling water.

The western plain had no trees to shade him.

The sun tilted toward the horizon, its light closer to Kuafu’s line of sight than at noon. Each breath drew in dry, hot dust; the stones beneath his feet turned white from the sun. He braced with his staff for a moment, then kept running; his speed had slowed since dawn, but each step felt heavier.

Kuafu finally saw the sun’s lower edge approaching the distant land. It was no longer just an immeasurable wheel above his head; it seemed to sink slowly at the far west. He quickened his pace, leapt across a dry riverbed. At that moment, his huge shadow stretched back, and the light almost swallowed his outline.

He shouted to the sun, his voice echoing across the open ground: “Why do you leave us every day?”

The sun didn’t stop to answer. A wave of heat rushed forward, shaking the road ahead. Kuafu reached out; light slipped through his fingers. He could chase the setting sun’s direction, but he couldn’t grip it like a torch.

Moving forward, the ground grew hotter. His shoes wore thin, his throat felt like it was packed with hot sand. Kuafu didn’t dream of bringing the sun back to his homeland, nor did he achieve an easy victory. He got closer than ever, truly understanding that the sun gave light and heat but also possessed a blaze that any mortal could not endure.

Nearby, a few drought‑tolerant beasts had already slipped into rock crevices. Kuafu kept running the straightest route, neither circling to show strength nor stopping because no one watched. He dropped his staff ahead each time, marking the spot for his next step.

A horizontal fissure blocked the way. Heat accumulated at the bottom, and detouring would let the sunset disappear completely. Kuafu stepped back a few feet, launched his staff across the gap, then leapt over the fissure. He almost lost his balance on landing, but grasping the staff steadied him. The sun didn’t get any closer from this daring move, but his body paid another price.

The sunset touched the distant horizon. Kuafu accelerated again, but only a heavy breath remained in his chest. The water his body lost could no longer be replaced by will. His lips cracked, his tongue was heavy, and the bright light ahead split into several rings. The goal of chasing the sun was still there, but taking another step alive became even more urgent.

He knelt beside a huge stone, palm on the ground. Heat seeped from the earth, reminding him that he couldn’t stay long. Kuafu knew that a nearby spring, even if it existed, wouldn’t quench a giant’s thirst; only a distant river could save him.

He found a few drops of remaining water in a rock fissure, enough to wet his lips. As soon as the droplets entered, they felt like they fell on hot earth, unable to soothe the burning throat. Scattered water sources were useless; he had to turn to a real great river.

The last sliver of sunlight quietly sank into the western horizon, as if someone had gently lifted a curtain. That blazing column shrank on the horizon, leaving only a faint afterglow before vanishing into twilight. Kuafu’s feet still thundered across the scorching plain, sweat beads rolling off his forehead like tiny raindrops, evaporating in the heat with no trace.

He looked up; the sky had shifted from golden to dark blue, stars had not yet lit, only the distant mountain peaks still held the day’s warmth. Kuafu’s chest throbbed, his breath felt like a drumbeat scorched by flame, the loam under his feet crackled softly from the burn. Every step felt like stepping on hot iron, the sting at his soles made him grit his teeth, his mouth twitched unconsciously, as if whispering to himself, “No stopping, no falling.”

Just then, Aman—an unbound chronicler of the Jade Pool, not limited by mortal time, carrying a tattered bamboo scroll—was panting behind him, clutching a trembling pen tip from sweat, occasionally scribbling crooked characters on the scroll. Aman muttered, “My pace is way faster than that old turtle!” As he spoke, he accidentally stepped on a loose stone, nearly fell, and the scroll almost flew out of his hand—luckily he grabbed it hard enough to keep it steady.

Kuafu turned his head to look at Aman, who was standing behind him, using exaggerated gestures to shout, "Big brother, don't forget to bring something to shade yourself!" The sound echoed through the empty plains, carrying a hint of humor, but also made Kuafu's heart tighten - he hadn't had a sip of clear water in days, and the dryness in his mouth was like a burning fire, even making his tongue emit a faint hissing sound.

He endured the scorching pain in his throat and continued to run forward, his footsteps becoming heavier. The ground beneath his feet was cracked due to the sun, and occasionally, a few withered grass roots would poke out from the cracks, as if mocking his persistence. Kuafu's hand tightly grasped the sturdy staff, whose head was carved with ancient patterns, reminding him of his unyielding vow.

At this moment, a low rumble came from afar, like a massive water flow rushing through a valley. Kuafu's ears caught the sound, and he instantly understood it was the confluence of the Yellow River and the Wei River. A glimmer of light flashed in his eyes, as if he saw a life-saving spring. So, he slowed down his pace, supported himself with his staff, and turned towards the direction of the faintly audible water flow.

Aman saw this and quickly spread out his bamboo slips, hastily recording: "Kuafu turned towards the rivers, and his footsteps to chase the sun have temporarily stopped." He also muttered to himself, "My records are faster than the river water, haha!" After finishing, he accidentally dropped his brush into the dust, and in a hurry, he picked it up with his fingers, getting them stained with yellow soil, and the characters he wrote became even more slanted.

Kuafu's silhouette stretched out in the dusk, his staff swaying gently behind him, like a flagpole pointing towards hope. Although he had left the direction of chasing the sun, he still couldn't stop the thirst in his throat; the rivers ahead were in sight, but he still had to cross the scorching wilderness to reach them. At this moment, he was exhausted, and his footsteps were so heavy they almost merged with the earth.

He heard the sound of a large water flow coming from the northeast direction, which was where the Yellow River and the Wei River were. Kuafu supported himself with his staff, changed direction, and headed towards the rivers. The thirst accumulated from chasing the sun finally forced him to leave the sun's direction to search for life-saving water.

When darkness fell, Kuafu left the land of the setting sun. The extreme thirst was burning his body, and he had to drink from the rivers first before deciding whether he could continue towards the light.

Kuafu followed the sound of the water to the banks of the Yellow River.

In the night, the Yellow River continued to flow rapidly. Kuafu bent down, scooped up water with his hands, and took his first sip; the scorching sensation temporarily subsided. He drank again and again, the river water constantly replenishing his palms, and the muddy shore bore the weight of the giant.

This was not a rest by a shallow stream. Kuafu had been chasing the sun all day, and the heat of the open wilderness had driven the thirst deep into his body. He drank and drank until the water level of the Yellow River dropped noticeably, but his throat still felt like it had leftover fire.

He stopped to wait for the water from upstream to replenish, trying to slow down his breathing. The river breeze blew through his wet clothes, which should have brought coolness, but the heat deep within his body didn't retreat with the water. The Yellow River could temporarily soothe his parched mouth and tongue but couldn't restore the strength he lost during the day's chase.

"It's not enough," Kuafu said.

The Yellow River didn't have any more answers, continuing to flow from upstream. He followed the river, searching for a tributary, and turned towards the Wei River. The water of the Wei River was clearer and cooler, and he drank again. When the water entered his mouth, it could stabilize his breathing, but a short while later, the extreme thirst rose again from his chest.

Aman didn't catch up until then, following the footprints. He first saw the huge depression on the shore, then saw the drop in the river water, and stood there for a long time, holding the green bamboo slips.

"I was worried I wouldn't be able to catch up to you," the young man said, panting, "now I'm worried the river won't be able to catch up with itself."

Kuafu didn't laugh. He looked up towards the western sky, which no longer had the sun, his voice hoarse: "The water of the rivers still can't quench my thirst."

The recorder understood the weight of these words and stopped speaking. He wrote clearly on the green bamboo slips: Kuafu approached the sunset, due to the scorching thirst, first drank from the Yellow River, then drank from the Wei River, but the rivers still couldn't quench his thirst. The edge of the bamboo slips was dampened by the splashed water.

The water of the Wei River was cooler than the Yellow River, and Kuafu still drank it in several mouthfuls. He first sat on the shore to wait, then tried to walk, but the thirst quickly returned with his footsteps. The results of the two rivers were different, yet verified each other, finally proving that the problem couldn't be solved by just drinking one more mouthful.

The nearby fishermen, awakened by the commotion on the shore, told Kuafu that there was a large marsh in the north, with a vast water surface, where birds and beasts often came to drink. The journey was long, and it was hard to tell the direction at night, but it was the only water area nearby that could possibly satisfy this thirst.

"Wait until dawn to go," the fishermen advised.

Kuafu used his staff to support himself and stood up. The sun had already set, and he couldn't keep it; if he stopped by the river now, the next sunrise would only see an unfinished chase. He wasn't going to pretend to chase the sun in the dark but was going to find the large marsh to look for water that could sustain his life.

The young man wanted to follow but was stopped by Kuafu's shake of the head. He had been chasing from his tribal land to the rivers and was already exhausted; ahead, there was no clear road. He couldn't help Kuafu find the large marsh, nor could he change the outcome of this journey.

"I'll record where you're going," he said.

Kuafu took a step north. The river water he had just drunk allowed him to stand up again, but it didn't restore all his strength. His stride had shortened, and his staff fell to the ground with a heavier sound. The Yellow River and the Wei River were left behind, and the classic drink didn't turn into a magical recovery but became the starting point for the next crisis.

He stood on the shore, watching the tall figure - Kuafu - slowly step into the dusk of the northern wilderness. The dusk was like a thick, heavy velvet, pressing down on the gravel of the riverbank, and the sound of the river water echoed again in the silence. Kuafu's footsteps were heavy and rhythmic, as if every step was competing with the earth for breathing space. Just as he was about to leave the riverbank, Kuafu suddenly stopped, lowered his head, and wrote two powerful characters on the rough bamboo slips in his hand: "Great Marsh". The strokes seemed to be written in blood, cutting through the night's thin mist like a knife.

Aman, the recorder who always followed behind, panting and not willing to put down the green bamboo slips, looked up, his eyes shining with surprise: "Great... Great Marsh? Big brother Kuafu, isn't this about finding water to drink?" His words carried a hint of puzzlement, mixed with the curiosity and awe of a child. Aman's brush tip trembled on the slips, almost writing the characters "Great Marsh" upside down. He hastily corrected it, muttering, "My memory is like the river water - drinking it doesn't help me remember!"

Kuafu didn't turn his head, just lightly tapped his staff on the ground, producing a low, muffled sound, like a mockery of Aman, or a reminder to himself of his persistence. The wooden staff trembled slightly in the night wind, as if sensing the fatigue its owner was about to face. Kuafu's footsteps, already shortened by his previous thirst, now stumbled a few times, his ankles hitting stones, making a "creaking" sound, as if the earth was reminding him: even a giant can't escape the bounds of fatigue.

The sound of the river water became exceptionally clear at this moment, and the starlight floating on the water's surface was lifted by the gentle breeze, like numerous small lanterns seeing Kuafu off on his long journey. Aman stood on the shore, the green bamboo slips in his hand trembling in the wind; he raised his hand to block the wind but only caught a few falling willow branches, laughing somewhat awkwardly: "My hands can't even grasp the wind, they're lighter than Kuafu's footsteps!" His words echoed in the quiet night, carrying a hint of self-mockery and humor.

Kuafu's figure stretched out in the night, his footsteps echoing in the distant wilderness, like drumbeats striking between heaven and earth. Aman closely followed, his panting and footsteps intertwining into a discordant symphony. On his green bamboo slips, he had already written down every instance of Kuafu's drinking, every fall, and every time he stood up again. At this moment, his brush tip involuntarily drew a small smile above the characters "chasing the sun", as if reminding himself: even in the grand endeavor of chasing the sun, there are comedic moments worth remembering.

The night wind grew stronger, carrying away the coolness of the riverbank, but it couldn't take away the burning determination in Kuafu's chest. Aman's gaze remained fixed on the distance, his lips murmuring, "The great marsh, it's waiting for me." His words were mixed with reverence and nonchalance, as if he was telling himself: even if I don't catch up, I'll remember this journey clearly. The gurgling sound of the river faded into the distance, while Kuafu's footsteps grew heavier on the barren plain ahead, until they disappeared into the unknown vastness of the great marsh.

The riverbank was left with only the echo of the water's voice and the bamboo slips in Aman's hand, which looked a bit disheveled.

The road to the great marsh didn't have the accompaniment of a river.

After leaving the river Wei, Kuafu first headed north through the lowlands. At night, he couldn't see the sun, so he used the position of the stars and the terrain to guide himself. He probed the ground in front of him with his staff, circling around when he hit soft soil and shifting his weight when he encountered firm ground.

He was able to maintain a rhythm for the first few steps, but as he walked further, the brief relief brought by the river water quickly dissipated. His throat grew parched again, and his legs felt like they were still carrying the weight of the sun on the mountain. Kuafu didn't look back to drink from the river Wei, because he had already proven to himself that the water there couldn't quench his thirst; the great marsh was the only direction he hadn't fully explored.

On the barren land, there were occasional shallow depressions with only residual mud from the rain. Kuafu bent down to look, but he didn't consider the muddy water a sufficient source of water, nor did he waste energy digging for non-existent springs. He used each stop to confirm his direction north before continuing.

A flock of night birds flew in from the north, carrying a damp atmosphere on their feathers. Kuafu adjusted his course based on the direction of the birds and found fresh animal tracks on the ground. The tracks led in the same direction, indicating that the great marsh was not an illusion; however, this confirmation only provided direction, not the strength to carry him forward.

Once, his foot stepped on a loose stone, and his body leaned forward. His staff sank deep into the ground, supporting his massive frame. Kuafu slowly stood up straight, pulled out his staff, and found that the tip had cracked. He switched to the other end to support himself, but he didn't get a new staff that would glow, nor did anyone appear from the darkness to repair the road.

After walking a bit further, he fell to his knees for the second time. This time, his staff could only help him sit up, not immediately recover. In the distance, the night sky was slightly brighter, with the faint sound of water birds. The great marsh was in that direction, possibly just a few barren plains away, or perhaps still half a day's journey.

Kuafu placed his palms on the ground, feeling the faint moisture. The existence of the marsh was not a rumor, but knowing the direction and actually reaching it were separated by his exhausted strength. He dragged his staff forward, his steps changing from a run to a slow walk, and then from a walk to a pace where he had to stop after every step.

The eastern horizon began to brighten. A new day's sun was about to rise, but Kuafu only looked towards the great marsh. He had been chasing for a day, enduring the cost of approaching the light and heat; now, his only goal was to reach the marsh and drink water, to prevent his life from being cut short halfway.

When the morning light illuminated the distance, he finally saw a narrow expanse of water. The great marsh lay at the end of the plain, with water birds flying up from the reeds. Kuafu gripped his staff, trying to stand up straight once more.

His legs couldn't support him. He fell forward, using his hands to prop himself up, with his staff rolling to the side. The great marsh was in sight, but the water was still out of reach. Thirst, scorching heat, and the exhaustion from the long chase all weighed him down, and no river water could arrive in time to save him.

Kuafu was still conscious. He looked at the great marsh and also saw the sun rising in the east. The sun completed one cycle, but the pursuer had reached the end of his strength. This was not a temporary rest or a wait for rescue; Kuafu had exhausted himself on the way to the great marsh, and his body could no longer continue.

He stretched out his hand to touch his staff. The wooden staff had accompanied him across gullies, over mountains, and through the wilderness and darkness, but now it couldn't take him to the water's edge. Kuafu knew he couldn't reach the great marsh, and his only choice was how to use the little strength he had left.

The sun rose from the east, casting its light on Kuafu.

He lay on the barren land, able to hear the distant calls of water birds from the great marsh, yet unable to drink a single drop of water. His staff lay across his palm, the cracked end stained with mud. Kuafu tried to use it to prop himself up, but his arm only lifted halfway before falling back down.

When he first started chasing the sun, his tribe asked him what he would say when he caught up. Now, he hadn't caught up with the sun, and he didn't have the strength to return and answer. He saw his footprints stretching from the south, a series of deep impressions, one after another. If someone followed his footprints, they would also go through the same parched land.

The barren land had no shade. Even if someone walked slower than him, carrying more water, they might still be trapped by the sun and thirst when they reached this point. Kuafu grasped his staff, thinking that it had once helped him explore the road, but if it only remained in his hand, it would wither away with him.

He raised his staff forward. For the first time, he didn't lift it over his shoulder, and the staff fell back to the ground. Kuafu paused, gathering his last bit of strength from the depths of his body, and then gripped the staff again.

He thought of the children in his tribe who ran before sunset and the marks he had left along the way – the stone sheds, the thick animal hide, the gully edges, and the footprints behind the mountains. Any of these could only help those who came later walk a short distance; if the staff could grow, it might provide shade on the hottest roads.

This time, he threw the staff with all his might towards the land between the great marsh and his path.

The staff cut through the morning light, falling into the distant mud, its tip slanting into the ground. Kuafu looked at it, as if he still had one more step to take.

"For those who come later," he said in a low voice.

There was no one from his tribe nearby to respond, nor was there a divine promise of a miracle. Kuafu's hand slowly fell, his gaze still fixed on the sun and the great marsh. His chest heaved like a drumbeat, his footsteps leaving deep impressions in the scorching hot sand, and the heat waves made his robe emit a soft hissing sound. Behind him, Aman panted, following closely with the bamboo slips in hand, muttering, "My footsteps are like racing a turtle; how can I keep up with Uncle's pace?" He occasionally looked up, seeing Kuafu's silhouette stretched long by the sunlight, and couldn't help but laugh: "Uncle, your determination to chase the sun is like trying to pull it down to roast meat for us!"

Kuafu's throat felt like it was being burned by flames, his thirst so intense it was as if his tongue was being torn apart. He suddenly turned around, rushing towards the nearby river Wei, whose water sparkled like a flowing mirror under the scorching sun. Kuafu bent down, scooping up a handful of icy water, and gulped it down, the water splashing everywhere, even startling a few wild egrets standing on the shore into flight. Aman stood on the shore, holding the bamboo slips, and exclaimed, "Uncle, this water is indeed 'cool'! I thought it was hot broth!"

However, the water of the river Wei, although clear, was not enough to soothe his parched lungs, which felt like they had been roasted by the fierce sun. Kuafu drank an entire bucket of water but still felt dry and thirsty, as if he had poured sand into a furnace. Aman saw this and quickly flipped the bamboo slips to a blank page, hurriedly writing, "Uncle Kuafu, drinking water can't quench his thirst; he's as thirsty as a dying camel!" His words carried a hint of teasing, but couldn't conceal the pity in his heart.

Thus, Kuafu continued forward, his footsteps heavy as if he were carrying an entire mountain. His shadow on the ground was stretched long, swaying occasionally in the heat waves, as if trying to escape the scorching heat beneath his feet. Aman followed closely, the bamboo slips in his hand now dampened by sweat, the pages slightly curled. He occasionally looked up at the sun ahead, muttering, "Uncle, is the sun running fast, or are we running slow?"

As they stepped into the edge of the great marsh, the ground changed from hard, dry earth to wet, muddy terrain. Kuafu's every step sank deeper, his ankles grasped tightly by the sticky mud, as if invisible hands were holding back his forward momentum. Aman stumbled several times in the mud, almost falling, but managed to continue by grasping a fallen branch, shaking and swaying as he recorded, "Uncle's footsteps are like stepping on 'soft, fluffy cotton candy'; it's 'one step, one pit'!"

Just as Kuafu was about to reach the center of the great marsh, his legs finally gave out, his knees buckling like broken twigs, his body leaning forward, and the staff in his hand slipping, hitting the soft mud with a thud. Aman exclaimed, almost dropping the bamboo slips into a puddle, and hastily picked them up, shaking the pages and shouting loudly, "Uncle, don't fall! I can't write any more 'tales of chasing the sun' in this notebook!"

Kuafu’s eyes remained fixed on the blazing sun in the distance, a faint gasp escaping his lips. But his breaths grew shallower and shallower until, on his way to the Great Marsh, his strength finally gave out and he died. The wilderness offered no rescue; only the wind howled across the empty plains, carrying away

the last of his presence, sweeping over the spot where his staff had fallen. The cracked end of the staff touched the damp earth, its wood grain drinking in the moisture from below. First, a tiny tender sprout pushed up from the fissure, followed by roots stretching deep into the soil. By the time this transformation began, Kuafu could no longer see it.

The underground moisture came from the Great Marsh not far away. The roots didn’t magically transport the marsh water to Kuafu’s side, nor did they reverse death. They simply allowed his final wish to take root and continue in suitable soil. Cause and effect landed in the connection between the wooden staff, the taking root, and the wet earth—not in a sudden, miraculous appearance of a fully grown forest out of thin air.

His body remained in the wasteland, his giant figure never to rise again. The sun climbed higher, illuminating the trail of footprints he had left behind, and also shining on that staff now taking root. Chasing the sun hadn’t made it stop, but it had allowed Kuafu to leave his final act as a gift for those who had not yet reached this place.

The sprout grew new branches, its leaves unfurling in the hot wind. The roots dug deeper, drawing water from the underground near the Great Marsh. The staff, once just a wooden tool supporting a chaser, had now left his hand and begun to become a life form capable of surviving on the scorched earth.

The tragedy wasn’t that Kuafu defeated heaven and earth at no cost. It was that, knowing his strength was exhausted, he still threw his last shred of possibility forward. His chase ended in death, but his persistence left behind more than just the footprints of failure.

In the morning light, the shadow of the first leaf fell on the ground. The shadow was small, unable to cover the giant’s body, yet it promised that this place would no longer be forever without shade. The process of the staff turning into a forest had begun. The next wave of change would be completed by rooting, blooming, and the arrival of those who came after.

The branches and leaves born from the staff did not stop at just one tree.

Roots extended along the damp soil, and new sprouts broke through the earth one after another around them. As spring turned to autumn, the young trees grew tall, their canopies connecting with each other. After the flowers bloomed, they bore peach fruits, and the scorched earth gradually became covered with a peach forest. The forest provided shade, the fruit provided water, and the falling leaves softened the hard, dry ground.

When Kuafu’s clan members followed his footprints to find him, they first saw where the giant had fallen, and then they saw the patch of green ahead that had no business appearing in the wasteland. They did not find the sun brought back, nor did they see Kuafu wake from death. The result of the chase was a life that had reached its end and a peach forest born from a staff.

Later travelers passing through could hide from the midday heat under the trees and pick peaches to relieve their burning thirst. Some traveling from the He and Wei rivers to the Great Marsh used the peach forest as a landmark on their journey, so they wouldn’t face the final stretch without shade or fruit, just as Kuafu had.

Aman arrived in the forest with his clan, still clutching the green bamboo slips in his arms. He recognized the cracked old grain of the staff on the ground and saw that the final footprints stopped not far away. Earlier, when he had lost track of Kuafu, he could still joke about refusing interviews because he was busy holding his clothes. Now, he put all his lighthearted words away.

He began writing from the start: Kuafu set his heart on chasing the sun, crossing plains, climbing mountains, and entering the scorched lands of the west; as he neared the sun, he grew thirsty and drank the He and Wei rivers dry, yet it still didn’t quench his thirst; he turned toward the Great Marsh in the north, but his strength failed him and he died on the way; in his final moments, he threw his staff forward, and it took root, transforming into a peach forest.

He wrote every event in the order it happened, without suddenly adding a forest at the end, and without deleting the fact that Kuafu died of thirst. Aman specifically noted: the peach forest was not a victory prize awarded by others, nor was it a new glowing cane. It was the ripple effect of the old staff and his final wish, growing out of the earth.

A child picked a peach and asked, “Kuafu didn’t catch the sun. Does that count as losing?”

The old man looked at the bright light outside the forest. “He didn’t keep the sun, and he didn’t make it back alive. If you only ask whether he caught it, the answer is clear. But the peach in your hand is what he left for those who came after, in his very last moment.”

The child shared the peach with his companions and didn’t press further about winning or losing.

At dusk, the sun still set in the west. The shadows of the peach forest stretched long on the ground, like the giant shadow that had once crossed the clan’s land on the day Kuafu set out. The difference was that these tree shadows wouldn’t follow one person away; they stayed on the road, shielding every traveler who came after from a moment of burning heat.

Aman carved the word “tragic heroism” at the end of the green bamboo slips, but felt that two characters were too light to capture it. So he added a note about the real ripples in the forest: travelers found shade, the hungry found fruit, the thirsty found water in the peaches, and the lost found their direction by the trees. Kuafu didn’t finish chasing the sun, but he ensured that the path toward the light was no longer just scorched earth.

Night fell, and the clan lit small fires under the peach forest. The sun would rise again the next day, and people would still have to face the light, the heat, the journey, and their own limited strength. Kuafu’s story didn’t teach people to pretend the cost didn’t exist. Instead, it showed them: life can exhaust itself, but the final choice can transcend death, becoming a path under the feet of those who come after.

The wind rustled through the peach leaves, and the sound of the forest traveled from the direction of the Great Marsh toward the He and Wei rivers. Kuafu’s staff had already become a peach forest, leaving green shade and fruit along his footprints in the human world. Aman closed the green bamboo slips, said no more jokes, and quietly bowed in the tree shadows toward that giant footprint.
