The villagers’ biggest fear wasn’t a lack of grass and trees, but the mystery of which plant could actually save a life.

A child had been feverish for days; the family brought back bitter leaves from the mountain, boiled them, but nothing changed. An elderly neighbor couldn’t eat; a kind soul sent over fragrant roots, claiming the scent alone would do the trick. A hunter, after a rainstorm, accidentally ate wild fruit and was wobbling on his way home—no one could say what might soothe him.

Shennong went house to house and saw that the people weren’t refusing to try anything. They remembered that in one year, someone had eaten a certain herb and felt better, so they’d named all similar leaves the same. Others had seen birds and beasts munching on a fruit and assumed humans could safely eat it too.

“If birds can eat it, why can’t we?” the young hunter asked.

Shennong pointed to a bird pecking a fruit on a tree. “It has wings. Can you fly with it?”

The hunter shook his head.

“Body shape matters. What a bird can handle doesn’t mean you can.”

The messier problem was the village names. The same fragrant herb was called “Awakened Leaf” in the east and “Mountain Tea” in the west; two completely different bitter roots were both dubbed “bitter medicine.” When news traveled from one place to another, it often boiled down to “bitter can heal,” with no detail on what it healed or how to tell it apart.

Shennong lined up every plant the villagers had used on a clearing, stopping anyone from casually boiling them. He first asked where it was gathered, then examined leaf shape, stem color, and scent, and finally had the patient’s family describe any visible changes before and after taking it.

The bitter leaves the child had tried didn’t help, but they didn’t make things worse either. The fragrant roots the elder had smelled made him willing to eat, but that didn’t mean every feverish person should use them. The hunter’s wild fruit had to be stored separately from edible fruit right away.

A mother asked Shennong if he could simply clear all the bitter plants from the mountain.

Shennong shook his head. “Bitter isn’t always harmful, and sweet isn’t always safe. Pulling plants out just by taste could destroy the ones that truly help.”

He ventured into the nearby forest and found far more species than the villagers had brought. The sun‑facing slope, the wet valley, the stone cracks, and the water’s edge each had distinct leaves. Relying on a single accidental experience would never let you sort out this sea of plants.

Shennong decided on three tasks: first, record each plant’s true appearance to avoid name confusion; second, learn how it changes once ingested, distinguishing edible, medicinal, and dangerous ones; third, teach this knowledge to others so no one has to gamble again.

Someone suggested catching animals and testing the plants on them. Shennong refused. An animal’s reaction doesn’t directly translate to humans, and handing the risk to a creature that can’t explain its feelings won’t give a clear answer.

Another said the strongest young people should try it. Shennong again said no. “I’ll decide the risk, not you. Your job is to observe, collect, and remember, not to shoulder my danger.”

He told the villagers to stop using unknown plants for now, to keep accidental eaters in a cool place, and to separate the known harmless water foods. Then he grabbed a bamboo basket and an old knife, ready to start with the most common plants near the village.

The villagers’ ailments had already shown that rumors alone were insufficient. Shennong wasn’t looking for a single miracle herb; he was set to separate the medicinal, edible, and dangerous plants of the wide wilderness one by one.

Dawn came, and he headed to the first slope. The plants looked calm in the dew, yet they hid both life‑saving and harmful powers. Shennong had decided to take the risk himself.

He didn’t just see the plant; he put it in his mouth.

He first noted whether it grew on a dry slope or wet ground, whether insects or birds had eaten its leaves, then smelled it, touched its sap. That still didn’t prove it was good for humans, but it ruled out rotting, spiny, or obviously irritating parts, and it told future seekers where to find the original plant.

The first was a fragrant leaf by the river. Villagers said deer often lingered nearby, but no one had seen them actually eat it. Shennong took a leaf, sniffed it, then ran his tongue over it. No immediate reaction, so he tried a bite.

It tasted slightly bitter, leaving a cooling aftertaste. He sat there, waiting, and didn’t try a second plant. His companions complained the pace was slow and asked why he didn’t try several at once if none seemed bad.

“If you eat them all together, and something changes later, how will you know which one caused it?” Shennong replied.

That’s when they understood that rushing could mix the results. They followed Shennong’s instruction to note only the leaf’s shape, scent, growing spot, and the outward changes after tasting, without exaggerating its benefits.

The second plant smelled sweet, but its edges tingled your fingers. Shennong didn’t taste it; he put it in a marked basket, waiting until he was fully prepared. Trying it hard was not the same as swallowing it immediately.

The third was a spicy root from the sun‑facing slope. Shennong first pulled a root from a stone crack at the slope’s base, still coated in morning dew, sparkling like a bead. He snapped it into two tiny pieces, took the thinnest corner, and rubbed it in his palm, feeling a faint warmth, as if a tiny flame danced at his fingertips. Then he put that fine root fragment in his mouth; his tongue was instantly pierced by a sharp sting, as if a sprite had lit a spark on his tongue, and his eyes narrowed in surprise.

Aman stood nearby, holding a bamboo slip, his brow furrowed, but he muttered, “If this plant can roast my tongue, I’ll have to write ‘spicy’ as ‘roasted meat.’” After saying that, he hurriedly changed the written “spicy root” to “spicy root (roasted)” and then quickly erased it with a brush, fearing Shennong might misunderstand.

The sting warmed Shennong’s chest a bit, his blood felt like a gentle current, his body grew warm, and his breathing steadied. The heat didn’t vanish instantly; a few seconds later, tiny beads of sweat appeared on his forehead, like raindrops on stone. He steadied himself, closed his eyes, breathed deeply, letting the heat circulate until his whole body’s temperature stabilized, then nodded, signaling he was relatively safe.

At that moment, Aman spread the bamboo slip and hurriedly recorded: “Spicy root—take a tiny corner, taste is spicy, then body warms, no acute toxicity seen. Mark as ‘continue testing,’ pending further observation.” He was halfway through when his hand trembled, and the line he drew made “continue” into “continue—.” He looked up at Shennong, feigning nonchalance, and said, “Teacher, this plant’s name seems like it wants to keep growing.”

Shennong didn’t immediately proclaim the root a cure-all; he carefully returned the root fragment to the marked basket, tied its mouth with a red string, and fastened a small wooden plaque on the knot, engraved with “spicy root.” He whispered to Aman, “This plant warms the body, but we need more tests before letting the villagers use it.” Aman nodded, but a mischievous grin tugged at his mouth as he tucked the bamboo slip away, muttering, “When I write ‘warm body’ as ‘roasted body,’ I wonder if the teacher will laugh.”

Thus, on the sun‑facing slope, Shennong completed the first tasting and record of the spicy root, laying the groundwork for future observation and verification.

Back in the village, a rain‑soaked, shivering elder agreed to try the spicy root. Shennong told him it was a root he’d tasted and only seen a warming effect, still cautioning him. After drinking a mild broth, the elder stopped shaking, but the single case only hinted at potential usefulness, needing more watch.

Shennong added the results to the wooden plaque: “Spicy root from the sun‑dry slope, distinct scent, spicy taste, mild warmth after use, slight reduction of chill.” Each detail could be seen or recounted by others, avoiding vague “good medicine” statements.

Someone asked if the fragrant leaf could also be given to the elder. Shennong refused to mix the two plants. The elder was already improving; adding another would blur the experience and could bring unnecessary risk.

He also decreed that any plant not identified by him could not be tested by villagers. Collectors should bring the whole root with leaves and note the environment; caretakers of patients should describe the illness before and after, without risking themselves by swallowing unknown stuff.

Over the next few days, the bamboo basket split into three sections: those already tasted with no harm seen, those only observed but not yet ingested, and those visibly dangerous and isolated. They weren’t a final pharmacopeia, just the first clues for future identification.

Shennong also made a mistake in judgment. A leaf that smelled mild and felt gentle on the tongue left a lingering bitterness in his mouth. He stopped immediately, refusing to swallow just because he had started. In his notes he wrote plainly, “You can’t rely on scent alone,” turning the error into a future safety rule.

His companions gradually understood that his courage wasn’t about eating every plant without thought, but about knowing that life is at stake and still being willing to take the final step himself, while trying to make each step understandable to others.

When the first round of plant identification finished, Shennong only confirmed that a handful of plants were harmless for the moment and found a clue that the root of Xin might soothe a chill. From then on he began to link scattered tastes with the actual needs of the people, slowly figuring out the different medicinal properties.

Shennong brought the plants he’d already identified back to the village, placing each beside a whole specimen.

Keeping only a single dry leaf was risky; he recorded the root, stem, flower, and growing spot together so that the gatherers would know which plant to look for in the mountains. He first had a few people who walked the mountain paths identify them, and they pointed to three different bitter leaves, proving that the old names were indeed unreliable.

Shennong gave them new names. The fragrant leaves by the river were named after their scent and their riverside origin; the sun‑facing Xin root kept its spicy name and the slope feature. Another broad leaf tasted sour at the mouth and could coax people who had lost their appetite after a hot day to drink rice soup again, so it was called “Sour Leaf” and kept separate from the bitter herbs.

Aman held the green bamboo slips and came to copy them. Shennong said the first plant was “slightly bitter,” and his pen slipped, almost writing “slightly crying.”

“Will the plant cry?” a child beside him asked.

Aman quickly corrected the character: “It won’t, it’s just my old pen that’s hurting its tongue.”

Shennong read the record and told him to keep the joke in his head; the drug names and properties had to be written accurately. He nodded, then drew the character “bitter” with extra clarity, so clear that it seemed to make a face of its own.

A record can’t rely on taste alone. After tasting the sour leaf, Shennong first made sure he felt no discomfort, then boiled a light soup for a villager who was weak after a hot day. The villager ate, his spirit improved, and the sour leaf gained a visible use. Another elderly man who used it felt no help, so Shennong wrote down that the invisible sour could not be used recklessly.

The third herb had a fragrant stem; crushing it and applying it to a swollen sore gave some relief. Shennong first touched the juice to confirm it caused no discomfort, then let the patient use it. This medicinal property came from external observation; you didn’t have to swallow every plant, and tasting wasn’t the only step.

Aman asked, “If it doesn’t go in, does that still count as tasting the herbs?”

“Tasting is a way I take on unknown risk,” Shennong replied. “The goal is to identify the plants, not to chew every leaf.”

He recorded that sentence on the bamboo slips so future generations wouldn’t just mimic ingestion but would remember observation and responsibility.

A few days later, the villagers gave their first retelling. Shennong didn’t test them on the names; he handed them whole plants and asked them to describe the growing spot, scent, and known uses. A gatherer mixed up the sour leaf with a similar round leaf, and Aman immediately wanted to flip the slip for a hint, but Shennong raised a hand to stop him.

“Let him look again on his own.”

The gatherer compared the leaf backs, found one with fine white lines and one without, and finally corrected himself. Shennong added that distinction to the record. It came from a real misidentification and was more useful than simply adding adjectives.

The first batch of records gradually gained four parts: where it was found, how to identify it, Shennong’s own tasting or touch and the changes that followed, and the actual results of the villagers’ use. Cases with no effect were recorded too, to prevent people from only passing on success stories.

Aman spread the freshly copied page on a stone table, his brow slightly raised, tapping the three characters—“bitter, sour, spicy”—with his fingertips. He muttered softly, “This page makes the mouth busy before the tongue even starts.” Though quiet, the words echoed in a nearby child’s ears. The child blinked wide-eyed, leaned forward, and whispered, “Teacher, are there any sweet medicines?” Aman looked up, a hint of teasing in his eyes, yet kept a serious expression: “Sweet medicine? You’ll have to ask the Great Emperor Shennong.” He paused, tapped the slip lightly as if marking a heartbeat, “I only record the taste Shennong tried; how could I change a plant’s nature just because I love sweets?” With that, he pressed the pen tighter, drawing a fine horizontal line on the slip, noting the moment Shennong had just put a purple‑leafed plant in his mouth, his brow furrowed, his face slightly turning, and the slight sourness on his tongue—a mingling of bitterness and sourness. Aman stared at the words, his mouth twitched as if imagining that bitter taste swirling on his tongue. At that moment, Shennong coughed softly, pressed his chest, his brow furrowed even more, clearly the plant’s toxicity had begun to bite. Aman quickly added a note in the empty space: “Mild poisoning, tongue tip tingling, requires licorice for balancing.” He wrote swiftly, yet slipped in a joke: “If you’re a sweet‑food lover, you’ll probably have to sweeten your tongue first.” The child laughed, clapped, and said, “Uncle Shennong is like eating chili!” Aman smiled faintly, but his pen never stopped, continuing to record every change after each tasting, the degree of pain, and the specific antidote used—no detail left out. When finished, he gently closed the slip, looked toward the villagers still gathering herbs in the distance, and felt a quiet pride: this time the bitterness, sourness, and spiciness of all herbs were clearly written, so when the villagers later used them, they would no longer wander blindly.

After the laughter, Shennong handed the records to the village gatherers in turn. The knowledge of herbs was no longer confined to his memory. Several mild herbs had already helped patients, but the similarity of leaf shapes also revealed a risk of confusion.

At that stage, a seemingly ordinary new herb was brought in. It had no thorns, no strong scent, and its leaf color was even gentler than the sour leaf. By existing experience it seemed safe to test further. Shennong didn’t know it would bring the first real poisoning test.

The new herb grew in a shaded spot among the trees.

Shennong first smelled it, then lightly touched it with the juice—nothing obvious happened. He took a small piece to taste; it felt only mildly bitter, and he sat under the tree to wait.

At first everything was normal. After a while his face turned pale and his steps grew unsteady. His companions wanted to help him keep moving, but Shennong stopped everyone, forbidding anyone to touch the same plant again.

“This is poisoning,” he said.

His voice was lower than usual, but he first pointed to the original plant’s location, had people mark it with a stone so no one would accidentally step on or pick it in panic. Then he recalled the plants he’d identified before: Xin root only brought warmth, sour leaf helped eating, none matched the rapid worsening discomfort he felt now.

The bamboo basket still held tea leaves picked that morning. Shennong had seen villagers chew them, had tasted them himself, and confirmed the fragrance was harmless, though no clear use had been found. He put the tea in his mouth; the bitter taste gradually faded.

The discomfort didn’t vanish instantly. Shennong stayed where he was, avoiding further walking. His companions took turns watching his expression and responses, noting only external changes without exaggerating miracles, following the method he’d taught.

After a long time, his face slowly returned to normal and his steps steadied. The tea at least slowed the poison, giving his body time to recover. Shennong did not claim the tea cured all toxins; he simply wrote, “This herb’s poison is mitigated; other herbs still need separate identification.”

He had the poison plant brought back whole, wrapped in vine leaves layer by layer, separated from the medicinal herbs. After returning to the village, Shennong drew the leaf shape, shaded growth area, and mild bitterness on a wooden plaque and carved a striking warning.

Someone advised him to study only mild plants from now on, to avoid any possible poison. Shennong replied that if poisonous plants weren’t identified, villagers would still eat them by mistake because of their similar appearance. The real change needed wasn’t to stop, but to prepare known antidotes before each outing and to ensure companions could recognize the route and the original plant.

He also revised his old method. A mild scent and non‑irritating juice did not prove safe to ingest; waiting time could not be skipped. The rule of identifying one plant at a time became even more important, otherwise when poisoning occurred, the source would be impossible to trace.

Hearing that tea can neutralize poison, the hunter decided to brew tea with every mysterious wild fruit in his home and taste it. Shennong immediately stopped him: “Finding an umbrella doesn’t mean you can brave a storm. Tea is the clue to detox this time, not a green light to take risks.”

The hunter remembered his earlier mistake and hurriedly handed over the wild fruit for identification.

Shennong took a few days’ rest before climbing the mountain again. That pause was recorded faithfully: poisoning isn’t a one‑off scare that ends instantly; it steals time, and it can also leave those who were meant to help without a reliable backup.

When he returned to the woods, he first checked whether the poisonous‑grass stone record was still there, then taught the herbalists to distinguish the plant by the color of the leaf’s underside and its root. No one was allowed to taste it to verify.

The first poisoning cost Shennong a real price and brought two irreversible changes: a dangerous plant was clearly isolated, and tea became a known antidote; the method of testing herbs shifted from simple observation to pre‑prepared antidotes, single‑plant verification, and keeping a recovery window.

Shennong kept tea in a small pouch on his person. Ahead in the forest were more unknown herbs, some of which might not share the same toxicity. He had to keep searching for different solutions and make sure his notes could guide companions even when he couldn’t speak.

After the first poisoning, Shennong did not avoid that forest.

He brought the herbalists back to the poisonous‑grass stone record and fetched the acid leaf that looked most like it. The two leaves looked almost identical from afar; only up close did he notice that the acid leaf grew in a brighter spot, its underside had fine white lines, while the poisonous grass usually hid in damp shade and smelled acrid when broken.

Shennong let everyone take turns identifying, but no one was allowed to taste. One person only remembered “poison in the shade,” then turned and tossed another harmless plant that grew under a tree shadow. Shennong pointed out that a single trait could still lead to a mistake; you must look at the underside, the scent, and the growing environment together.

Next they encountered a cluster of slender‑stalked grasses. The left one tasted slightly spicy and had once soothed a cold‑suffering person; the right one had only a dark stripe at the root. After Shennong tried it, he felt dizzy.

He stopped walking immediately, pulled out the previously known soothing leaf that could ease such discomfort. Tea could only reduce part of the effect; the fragrant root of the other plant was what finally steadied his steps. Shennong kept this poisoning separate from the last: different source, different symptoms, different cure.

The herbalists whispered, “Great King, could we keep a few antidote herbs together forever and boil them in one pot so everyone can be saved?” Shennong frowned slightly, tapped his jade pendant against his chest, and answered in a low, unwavering tone, “What works once doesn’t mean it should always be mixed.” He turned and handed the root to Aman, the recorder, who hurriedly wrote on the bamboo slip, “This herb cures this poison.” The herbalists stared, eyes bright with hope, but Shennong’s gaze cut them off. “First identify which plant is the poison, then you’ll know which old trick to use.” He said, then carefully put the fragrant root back into the pile, lowered his hand to feel the slight coolness of another leaf. Aman looked up at Shennong, who inhaled softly, furrowed his brow as if tasting the herb. Suddenly his face changed, and a faint bitter smile crept to his lips: “This herb is as bitter as a stone dropped into a mountain spring.” He quickly recorded that, and the nearby herbalist laughed, the bundle of herbs swaying like a greeting. Shennong chuckled lightly, shook his head, and said, “The bitterness shows it can disperse poison, but if you boil it with others, the bitterness will mask the toxicity and mislead identification.” He then split the root in two, put one half into a small clay pot, and kept the other in his hand, signaling the herbalists to test separately. They nodded, poured each half into a tiny pot, flames flickered, steam rose. Shennong stood nearby, eyes blazing, watching the color of the liquid shift. Aman quickly wrote, “Segmented test—poisonous plant not mixed, only then can the medicine be distinguished.” At that moment, villagers gathered from afar, eyes filled with hope and worry. Shennong raised his hand to pause them, then handed the first sip of broth to an elderly man. The old man took a small gulp, his brow relaxed, a surprised smile spreading: “This broth isn’t bitter, yet it warms like a spring breeze.” The crowd clapped, laughter echoing through the valley. Shennong’s gaze returned, and he said softly, “Remember, you must separate the source of the poison; don’t mix all herbs together, or you’ll lose the chance to tell them apart.” His words rang in the hills, Aman copied them at the bottom of the bamboo slip, added a tiny smiley face, reminding future generations that even serious medicine can leave a touch of lightness.

The third group of plants was even more confusing. One plant had sweet fruit but poisonous leaves; another had leaves that made a gentle herbal soup, yet its fruit scared off birds and beasts. Shennong tested each part of the same plant separately, refusing to lump “one plant is good” into a blanket statement.

Every time danger appeared, he added a simple identification method that ordinary people could follow. A bright stone marker stood beside poisonous grass; after collecting it, he separated the herbs into baskets with different colored vines, and when teaching, he insisted on bringing the whole plant, not just a dried leaf.

Some worried the process was too tedious to remember. Shennong told them to start with the most common few near the village, not to memorize every herb. Each herbalist could become familiar with one mountain area, then cross‑check with each other; if a plant was unfamiliar, better not to pick it.

A few days later, a young boy returned with two baskets of herbs for Shennong to examine. The medicinal and poisonous plants were correctly separated, except for one plant that was left alone because the boy was unsure. Shennong didn’t scold him for hesitating; instead he said, “It’s better to admit doubt than to confidently send the wrong one.”

This mountain trip yielded three lessons: similar leaves can mean opposite effects; one cure cannot handle all poisonous plants; different parts of the same plant cannot be treated as the same medicine.

Shennong still took the risk of tasting himself, and the cost was clear. The second poisoning made him pause for several days, unable to visit the sick. He recorded that gap on a wooden slip so villagers would know that identifying herbs isn’t instant safety, and that chasing glory shouldn’t lead to reckless imitation.

The boundary between medicinal, edible, and poisonous plants gradually became clear. The records no longer listed only taste; they included the whole plant, visible traits, known uses, dangerous symptoms, and corresponding antidotes. Each new piece of knowledge cut one more chance of relying on luck in the wild.

When Shennong returned to the village, several herbalists could already point out common poisonous plants on their own. Next, he wanted these records to be tested against real illnesses, not just tidy notes in the woods.

A settlement upstream on the river sent word that many villagers were suffering from illness at the same time.

When Shennong arrived, he found they were not all the same case. Some felt cold after rain, others were hot and lost their appetite, and some had used spicy roots by mistake, turning red and parched. Yet the villagers had already set up a big pot, ready to boil all the herbs together for everyone.

Shennong immediately told them to stop.

“All of us are sick—why can’t we drink the same pot?” the old pot‑maker asked.

“Cold and heat are different, accidental ingestion and fatigue are different. If one herb could fix everything, I wouldn’t have to roam the mountains these days.”

He first separated the patients into ventilated, rain‑shaded spots, observing only the changes that could be seen on the surface or reported by the patients, not making the same judgment for everyone based on a single “unwell” feeling. Those who felt cold needed something different from those who felt weak after heat, and those who mistakenly ate spicy roots could not be given more warm herbs.

From his existing records, he pulled out three herbs that had been repeatedly verified. Spicy roots were given only to those who felt cold with no other danger signs; acid leaves were used for those who lost appetite after heat; cool leaves were offered to those who had mistakenly eaten spicy roots to ease the dryness. Each herb was first brought whole to the herbalists for confirmation, avoiding reliance on dried leaf color alone.

He did not claim instant cure. After taking the herbs, caretakers followed the agreed protocol to see if the patients would eat, sleep soundly, and regain their composure. If there was no change, they reported honestly and did not add more herbs on their own.

The cold‑shivering old man gradually warmed up and slept soundly that night; the people who felt weak after the heat drank rice soup and felt a little better; those who mistakenly used the bitter root calmed down after stopping it and being treated with plain water. The three outcomes matched the old bamboo‑slip records, proving that the recorded medicinal properties can work on real patients.

Another child was also feverish, and the family wanted to copy the cooling leaf remedy. Shennong noticed the child coughed violently, a symptom different from earlier cases, and told them not to apply the old herb just because of the word “fever.” Instead, they should rest, drink water, and keep observing.

“The record isn’t a command to grab a handful of herbs when you see a word,” he said, “it teaches you to recognize people first, and also to recognize herbs.”

The village’s herbal gatherers recited the differences of the three plants in front of everyone. One of them had mixed up the bitter root with the slender‑stem warm herb. Shennong took him back to the growing spot to compare root color and scent again. Only when the other could identify them independently was he allowed to hand the herbs over to the caretaker.

The people began to understand that more herbs don’t always mean more useful. The big pot that was originally meant for a mixed brew was repurposed to boil clear water, and several baskets of unidentified plants were sealed away for later identification. No one would again let a patient take the risk of mixing herbs just because “there’s always one that will work.”

After the illness eased, Shennong left a few locals who knew the mountain paths. He told them to keep recording whether the plants’ shapes changed with the seasons and to note which effects had not yet been verified a second time.

The results of the treatment were uneven: some people improved quickly, some only found relief, and others needed more rest and observation. It was these varied outcomes that made the knowledge of medicinal properties more credible.

Shennong saw that his own experience had finally turned from personal feeling into a medical practice usable by the people. Yet he also realized that if herbal gatherers only memorized the bamboo slips, they would still be helpless when confronted with a completely unfamiliar poisonous plant.

To finish the identification of all herbs, he set out again into deeper mountains. A local hunter had seen a vine that caused animals to collapse after eating it; the villagers called it the “stomach‑splitting plant.” Shennong had to leave a clear warning before the plant entered the gathering baskets.

The stomach‑splitting plant clung to the stone walls of a deep valley, its leaves not at all menacing.

It was as slender as a usable vine leaf and had a similar color. If you only heard the name, people would keep their distance; but in an unmarked valley, gatherers might not know which plant was the stomach‑splitting one.

Shennong first examined the vine’s stem and scent, noticing that the poisonous plant left a cloudy liquid when broken. He taught this trait to his companions, sent them all back to the windward side, and only then took a tiny piece himself to taste.

As soon as the bitterness faded, he sensed something wrong. The last two poisonings had progressed slowly; this time he lost strength quickly, and even forming a full sentence became difficult.

His companions wanted to bring all the tea leaves. Shennong shook his head, indicating that they should first follow the old records to identify the symptoms. The tea could temporarily blunt some of the poison, but it didn’t improve the condition. If everyone remembered only “use tea when poisoned,” they would miss the right cure now.

One gatherer flipped through the bamboo slip, recalling the fragrant root used on the second mountain trip. Another pointed out that the poison that time came from the slender‑stem plant, different from the vine now, so they couldn’t just copy it. The debate didn’t drag on; they followed the principles Shennong had set out and checked each antidote plant one by one from the ones already confirmed harmless.

Shennong pointed to a clear bitter leaf and then to a mild root that could protect the body’s essence. His companions did not add any unfamiliar material; they used only the two plants that Shennong had personally tasted and confirmed in different contexts.

The poison’s effect didn’t vanish immediately. The group moved Shennong to a shaded plain, kept him alert, and took turns watching. After a long time, he could finally sit up again, then gradually recover his speech and walking.

This poisoning was heavier than before and revealed a danger: all critical judgments rested in Shennong’s memory. If he could not explain, even though his companions held the herbs, they might not know how to choose.

The first thing Shennong did after recovering was not to gather more herbs, but to have everyone repeat the stomach‑splitting plant’s characteristics, the external changes after accidental ingestion, why the tea was insufficient, and which known antidotes were finally used. Someone omitted the cloudy liquid, so they went back to a safe distance to observe the original plant; someone reversed the order of the two antidotes, and they corrected it immediately.

He set up a bright stone plaque at the valley’s mouth and marked the danger with multiple vine knots. Nearby gatherers took turns bringing real specimens to identify until, no matter who led the way, they could separate the stomach‑splitting plant from similar vines.

“Will you still taste such herbs personally?” someone asked.

Shennong replied, “Unknown things always need someone to identify first, but you shouldn’t treat risk‑taking as the only solution. If you can confirm danger by appearance, old examples, and corroborating evidence, you don’t need to prove bravery by eating it.”

He made it clear that the stomach‑splitting plant was poisonous and forbidden as a normal medicine. That conclusion came from the cost of personal tasting, but it ultimately saved later generations from repeating the same danger.

The severe poisoning made Shennong rest for a long time; the planned mountain trip was postponed entirely. His companions, for the first time, truly took on the responsibility of memory and judgment.

When they left the deep valley, knowledge had shifted from “Shennong knows” to multiple people being able to recite it accurately. Among the herbs, some saved lives, others were poisonous if misused; antidotes were not just a single tea bag but required identifying the source and handling it cautiously based on prior experience.

Shennong looked at the bamboo slips on his companions’ backs and knew the final stage was here. He wanted people everywhere to take away this identification skill so that the benefits of tasting herbs would not disappear with one person’s silence or departure.

Shennong gathered herbal gatherers from all over at the same valley.

Those who came brought complete local plants and the orally transmitted names. The first round of verification ran into trouble: the “clear leaf” from the north was a different plant in the south, and the “bitter root” from the western valley was entirely different from the riverbank bitter root.

Shennong did not force the old names to stay. He had each person first show the growing spot, leaf‑stem scent, and known medicinal properties, then let others from different regions point out whether a plant with the same name was actually different. Unconfirmed plants were set aside; they were not added to medical experience just because the story sounded plausible.

Aman spread out a green bamboo slip to copy. Someone said a leaf was “bitter then sweet.” He paused, confirming, “Here it’s sweet, not dry, right? Shennong’s page already has a ‘bitter’ that almost turned into ‘cry.’ Old ink shouldn’t be altered again.”

Everyone laughed once, then checked each word carefully. Aman laid the freshly copied slip on the stone table, tapped each character with his finger, frowned slightly: “I just wrote ‘dry’ instead of ‘sweet’ in ‘bitter then sweet.’ If I don’t correct it, future readers will think it’s a dry plant.” He looked up at Shennong, who was lowering his head to bite a purple little plant, his brow furrowed, then his face suddenly changed, his tongue trembling as if tasting an extremely bitter medicine. Aman quickly stopped the pen mid‑air, whispered, “Here it’s ‘sweet,’ not ‘dry,’ right?” Shennong nodded, gently spat out the plant, his mouth corner tinged with bitterness, yet he kept recording.

At that moment, an old farmer standing nearby shook his basket and laughed, “If this plant really is ‘bitter then sweet,’ my old arm will have to suffer three days of bitterness first!” Everyone burst into laughter, but Aman made sure to note the joke in a margin: “The old farmer’s laughter is not due to the medicine.”

Then Aman wrote the confirmed content swiftly, steady on the green bamboo slip. After each line, he lightly rubbed the slip with his fingertip to ensure the ink hadn’t seeped and the characters were neat. Shennong stopped tasting herbs and simply observed, occasionally pointing to a leaf and indicating to Aman to record its shape and color. Aman nodded and quickly added, “Leaf is triangular, color like morning mist.”

Throughout the process, Aman never decided which plant could be used; his duty was only to faithfully record Shennong’s spoken names, properties, and the body’s reactions during testing. After finishing, he gently closed the bamboo slip, looked up at Shennong, and said, “All recorded. If there’s a mistake, please correct.” Shennong nodded, smiled contentedly, and the group folded the slip into the medicine box, ready to hand it to the people.

Thus, the names were short, but because they were matched with the original plant and its features, errors were unlikely; Aman’s meticulous verification left the most reliable medical experience for future generations.

Shennong divided the plants into several categories: those already helping the people and clearly identified; ordinary plants that could be eaten but whose effects shouldn’t be exaggerated; clearly dangerous poisonous plants; and unknown plants that still needed observation.

Every spot still had a record of the solution left behind. Tea leaves only corresponded to a few types of discomfort that they had previously alleviated, and were not a universal antidote; the broken intestine grass was marked with a clear warning that it should not be picked due to its mild appearance.

Representatives from all over would take turns recounting. Shennong deliberately pulled out a complete, unnamed grass, and had them identify it without relying on the simple text. Some people looked at the shape of the leaves, while others checked the scent and the soil it grew in, until they finally recognized it as the known bitter leaf, and not the deadly poison grass of the shadows.

Another group, however, could not come to a consensus. Shennong did not announce who had guessed correctly based on their feelings, but instead put it back in the unknown category, waiting to be re-examined later. Medical experience is not just about knowing what you know, but also about recognizing what you don't know.

The people began to transplant the confirmed grass plants to the village gardens, so that the harvesters would not have to venture into the treacherous terrain every time. The pictures of poisonous plants were carved into wooden signs at the crossroads, and even children could learn to avoid touching them from the warning signs.

Shennong also stipulated that learners had to be able to identify the plants before learning about their uses; simply remembering which plant could cure what was more dangerous than not knowing at all. Every harvester who returned home had to bring someone else to verify the information, so that knowledge would not be reduced to a single person's memory.

Aman recorded Shennong's experience of tasting all the herbs in the green bamboo slips: the people's suffering prompted him to set out; the first record allowed the properties of the herbs to be passed down; repeated poisonings forced out different antidotes and identification constraints; and real patients tested which experiences were actually helpful.

He finished writing and asked Shennong, "Can all the herbs be completely recorded?"

Shennong looked out at the endless mountains and forests: "You can't stop starting just because you can't remember everything at once, and you can't claim to fully understand just because you've recorded many things."

Aman added this sentence at the end without adding any exaggerated titles.

The harvesters gradually returned to their respective areas. Some taught villagers to identify the bitter root and similar poisonous plants, while others planted the bitter leaf in a special care area, and some even guarded the valley of the broken intestine grass to prevent accidental picking.

Shennong continued to observe new plants, but the people no longer had to wait for him to personally arrive before knowing the most common medicinal and poisonous plants. The risks that individuals had borne were finally transformed into collectively preserved, verifiable, and corrected medical knowledge.

As the sun set, the valley grasses swayed gently in the wind. They still had their bitter and sweet, medicinal and poisonous, and unknown aspects, and would not change forever just because they were written down. But Shennong's method allowed people to understand how to identify before using, how to record effects, and how to be cautious when facing unknown things.

Shennong's experience of tasting all the herbs had come to an end, and it had fallen to the people: it was not a magical prescription, but a set of medical experience accumulated through personal experience, cost, records, and transmission. From then on, the people would suffer fewer misadventures and recognize more useful medicinal plants, and the risks that Shennong had borne would not be in vain.
