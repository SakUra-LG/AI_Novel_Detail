The highest peak of the Kunlun Mountains is always covered in snow, but the Jade Pool never freezes.

In the morning, the clouds drift up from the waist of the mountain and get stuck at the edge of the pool, blocked by the peach trees. A few almost ripe peaches hang from the branches, their colors still pale, but their fragrance has already attracted the white cranes outside the forest. They dare not cross the jade boundary, so they pretend to admire the clouds. If any of them glance twice, the guardian birds will cough, and the crane will immediately look at its own feet.

The Queen Mother of the West walks along the pool's edge into the peach forest. She doesn't judge the peaches' ripeness by their fragrance, but instead examines the veins, flowers, and the clear air flowing beneath the trees. Peaches are not harvested every year; some trees take many generations to bear fruit. If she misses one peach today, the world might have one more dispute in the future.

In the stone room behind the forest, the fire in the jade stove has just gone out. A glass bottle sits in a three-layer jade box, containing the elixir of immortality. It's harder to refine than the peaches and can't be taken at will. Those who take the elixir can escape aging, but they must leave the familiar world behind; "immortality" doesn't mean keeping their old life unchanged forever.

When the Queen Mother returns to the Jade Pool's jade steps, Aman is already waiting there with a bundle of green bamboo slips. He's the Jade Pool's historian, and his old brush is stuck in his hair, with only two hairs left to do their job.

"Today, I'll remind you of three things," the Queen Mother says. "Peaches that aren't ripe can't be picked; the elixir of immortality hasn't been assigned to anyone, so it can't be opened; and those who remember these rules can't think they have a share just because they remember them."

Aman writes quickly, but when he gets to the third point, he looks up: "The first two seem like they're to prevent people from getting too hungry, but what's the third one for?"

"Because the white crane doesn't have a bamboo slip."

The white crane outside the forest lets out a cough at that moment. The historian has to write the third point more deeply and add a note: "Remembering the rules doesn't mean you understand them." He finishes writing and turns to the next page, only to find that it's already full of rules about the peach garden, the elixir room, attending ceremonies, and seeking the elixir. He can't help but move his hand.

"The winds on Kunlun are strong, and the rules are many," Aman whispers. "If I keep writing like this, my hand won't be immortal, and my old brush will have lived out its days."

The Queen Mother hears him but doesn't scold him. Instead, she hands him a single hair that had fallen off the jade steps: "First, pick it up. Immortal things can't be given carelessly, and a hair shouldn't be wasted."

This sentence makes the historian stifle a smile, and his shoulders shake with effort. He puts the hair back into the bamboo slip and continues checking. The Queen Mother orders the guardian birds to report the number of peaches, their ripeness, and the sealing of the elixir room. Each item has a guardian, and each boundary has a reason: it's not to make the valuable things seem more impressive, but because if the elixir of immortality falls into the wrong hands, the consequences will last for many years, far longer than a single dispute.

The checking starts with the peach forest. Two guardian birds report in sequence, one counting the trees and the other counting the ripeness. When they reach the seventh tree, their numbers suddenly differ by one peach. Aman immediately looks up at the white crane outside the forest, which is looking away, looking very innocent.

"We haven't figured it out yet, so let's not accuse the white crane prematurely," the Queen Mother says.

Aman erases the note he had written, "Suspected of being seen without a peach," and follows the guardian birds as they re-count. It turns out that the seventh tree has a branch that extends into the eighth tree's canopy, and the two birds had counted the peaches on the branch as belonging to different trees. Although there's no missing peach, there's a problem with ownership. The Queen Mother orders them to tie a green ribbon at the branch's base and register the peaches according to the tree they grow on, not according to which tree's shadow they fall under.

Aman looks up at the sky for a long time: "The peaches aren't even ripe yet, and they're already learning to cross the courtyard and visit each other."

The guardian bird reminds him to focus on the rules, and he writes down "Branches that cross boundaries, peaches belong to the roots," and adds a note on the side, "Visiting is not allowed to change your household registration." The white crane sees that no one is looking at it, so it takes a small step forward; the guardian bird next to the boundary coughs lightly, and the crane quickly puts its foot back down and examines a stone it's already looked at many times.

Next, they check the sealing of the elixir room. The seal on the three-layer jade box should be continuous, but one of the seals is off by half a finger. The attendant is startled, thinking that someone has opened the elixir box. The Queen Mother doesn't scold anyone immediately, but instead checks the jade case, the room temperature, and the seal rope. Finally, she discovers that the night wind had entered through the window and tightened the outer rope, causing the seal to shift slightly, but the inner two seals still fit perfectly.

She orders someone to fix the window and leave separate marks next to each seal. This way, if there's any shift in the future, they can tell which layer it's in, rather than being alarmed by a single change and disturbing the entire Jade Pool. Aman finishes writing the marks and his wrist is already sore: "It turns out that the first step in guarding immortality is not to be scared into living fewer years by a single tightened rope."

The Queen Mother doesn't argue with him. The purpose of the rules is not just to prohibit, but also to let the guardians distinguish between mistakes, carelessness, and real intentions. Although the peaches and elixir are still there, today's checking wasn't a waste: one peach that had crossed branches and one seal that had been affected by the wind have added one more practical boundary to the previously vague rules.

In the afternoon, a messenger bird flies over the snow-capped mountain and lands on the Jade Pool's railing. It brings news from the human world: the ninth day has passed, the earth is cooling down, and the hero who shot down the sun is still helping people rebuild their homes and dig wells. Some people say he will soon climb Kunlun to ask if there's really a way to live forever.

Aman's brush stops in mid-air: "He's done a great deed, so should I write this before or after the rules about seeking the elixir?"

"Write it as it is," the Queen Mother says, looking at the mountain below. "His deeds are real, and the rules are real. If we write them together, we'll see the difficulties."

She doesn't promise anything beforehand or refuse him beforehand, but instead orders someone to clean the seat by the pool. The peaches are still on the branches, the elixir is still sealed, and the order of Kunlun hasn't been disturbed by a single message.

But the Queen Mother knows that the real difficulty is not in following the rules when no one is knocking on the door. It's when a hero with great deeds and people who care about him stands in front of you that the guarding really begins.

The day Hou Yi climbs Kunlun, the wind starts to pick up in the valley below.

He carries his bow and walks up the mountain, step by step, from the rocky path. After crossing the narrow path by the Weak Water, the trees become fewer and farther between, but the snow becomes brighter and whiter. Ordinary visitors would turn back halfway up the mountain, but Hou Yi doesn't ride a divine bird or shoot an arrow to scout ahead. Instead, he uses his bow to support himself, testing each icy stone step before putting his foot down.

By the time he reaches the Cloud Gate, his grass shoes are worn thin. The guardian bird asks him why he's come, and Hou Yi puts down his quiver and bow on the stone table.

"I've come to seek the elixir."

When this message reaches the Jade Pool, Aman is drying yesterday's bamboo slips in the corridor. He rushes over, sees Hou Yi looking at his grass shoes and his big bow, and asks: "You've been walking up the mountain all this way?"

"Yes, I've been walking up the mountain all this way."

"Then your shoes are probably asking to be buried, and your bow is asking to be used to shoot the sun again."

Hou Yi looks down at his shoes, which are cracked open, and smiles: "If they can make it down the mountain, that'll be a good end."

The joke only lasts until the Queen Mother sits on the pool's edge, surrounded by unopened peach blossoms. Hou Yi bows, and she doesn't have anyone bring her peaches or use her shooting skills to spare him the questions.

"Why do you seek immortality?"

Hou Yi had prepared many reasons: there are still evil beasts in the human world, the land is still recovering from the disaster, and if he lives longer, he can help more people. He explains each one, and the Queen Mother only asks: "Is there anything else?"

He pauses for a moment before mentioning Chang’e.

After shooting down the sun, he walked through many villages that had been burned to ashes and saw many houses being rebuilt. Every night, when he came home, Chang’e would always help him hang up his bow. Those ordinary days made him realize that even heroes will grow old, and couples will have one leave before the other.

"I want to continue protecting the human world and not be separated from her," Hou Yi says. "If the Jade Pool really has the elixir of immortality, I'd like to get a portion that we can take together."

Aman had been writing "Protecting the human world" on the bamboo slip, but when he hears the word "together," his brush stops in mid-air. He doesn't add any comforting words, but writes down the original sentence exactly. The Queen Mother looks at him, and he retreats to the side of the corridor, taking his joke about the shoes with him.

The Queen Mother raises an eyebrow, as if taking a star from the sky and placing it gently on Aman's shoulder, signaling him to continue writing. Aman looks up, and a hint of unease flashes in his eyes, but he doesn't dare say anything more.

“The sun that fell from the sky is a great boon,” the Queen Mother said. “But immortality isn’t just swapping merit for years. If you only claim the medicine by merit, who gets it first—those who fix floods, quell uprisings, or save a village or a town? How do you decide the order?”

Her voice sounded like crystal water poured from an ancient well, echoing up the stone steps of the Jade Pool. Aman bowed his head, his fingers lightly rubbing the bamboo, as if trying to write his thoughts into the scroll.

“I want to keep protecting the mortal world, and I don’t want to part with her,” Hou Yi said. “If the Jade Pool really holds a life‑extending elixir, I ask for a dose we can share.”

Aman lifted his head, blinked, and his mouth twitched up a little, as if he wanted to hide a laugh inside the bamboo scroll, but the Queen Mother’s stern aura silenced him.

“When you say ‘share,’ who do you mean?” the Queen Mother asked, a light chuckle in her voice, as if to let Aman know that immortality isn’t handed out on a whim.

Aman tapped the bamboo with the tip of his pen, writing the word “share,” then added, “Do not take it casually.” He lifted his hand and gently tapped the stone slab beside his feet, the crisp sound a reminder that even footsteps in this mysterious Jade Pool must follow order.

“The sun that fell from the sky is a great boon,” the Queen Mother said. “But immortality isn’t just swapping merit for years. If you only claim the medicine by merit, who gets it first—those who fix floods, quell uprisings, or save a village or a town? How do you decide the order?”

Hou Yi couldn’t answer. One arrow can find its target, but it can’t decide everyone’s fate.

“Then give it only to those who save the most people.”

“Who will count? Will the one who saves fewer people age first?”

Hou Yi fell silent again. He finally understood that the Jade Pool’s dilemma wasn’t a trick. A seemingly fair answer, when applied to a specific person, could still become unfair.

The Queen Mother sent water, not peaches. After drinking, Hou Yi set the empty bowl upright on the desk. He was not angry about being questioned for his merit, nor did he turn Chang’e’s name into a bargaining chip.

“I can’t agree to that now,” the Queen Mother said.

“Then I’ll wait.”

“I might also wait for a refusal.”

Hou Yi tightened his worn‑out straw sandals. “Since I’ve come this far, I should also hear the reason for the refusal.”

The Queen Mother had the green bird arrange a stone cottage outside the pool and ordered the gate of the peach grove to open the next day. What needed to be decided next was whether Hou Yi wanted to take on a longer responsibility or simply avoid death.

The next day, the Queen Mother led Hou Yi into the peach garden.

The grove stretched from the western shore of the Jade Pool into deep mist, its branches quiet. Many trees bore flowers, some had no buds at all; ripe peaches were rare, each one glowing faintly.

Hou Yi asked, “With such a vast grove, why are there so few ripe peaches?”

“Because you’re looking at the same season, but not the same span of years.” The Queen Mother pointed to an old tree nearby. “When it last bore fruit, the mortal world you knew didn’t yet have the villages it has now. A mortal sees only one peach, but the Jade Pool guards its long wait and the even longer consequences that follow after taking it.”

Aman followed behind, recording everything. As Hou Yi passed a tree, a peach swayed in the wind; he sidestepped the branch. The young scribe couldn’t help but say, “It probably heard you asking for two people’s share and decided to hide.”

“Peaches don’t hide people.”

“Then I’m worried for it. The tree has waited so long, maybe it’s not yet ready.”

The Queen Mother turned back, and Aman immediately carved a joke onto another discarded scroll, leaving the original with only “Guests have not touched the fruit.” Hou Yi didn’t reach for it; he watched the peaches on different branches and, for the first time, felt that “two shares” was not a casual request.

At the end of the grove lay a jade stone room. After three nested doors opened, a glass bottle appeared. Its liquid was clear, only a thin layer. The Queen Mother told him this was the immortality potion. The amount available could feed one person; after drinking it, one would live forever and leave the mortal world.

Hou Yi instinctively asked, “Can’t we split it?”

“If you force a split, the power becomes unpredictable. I won’t gamble two lives on a chance.”

“How long to brew another bottle?”

“You must wait for unripe peaches, for many spirits to finish their cycles, and for someone to guard every mistake in the brewing. You can’t just shoot an arrow that always hits, and I can’t let impatient guests force time to produce another fruit.”

Hou Yi stared at the glass bottle. He had imagined staying young with Chang’e, never considering that one person taking the medicine would leave the other behind. If he took it, Chang’e would age; if he gave it to her, he would be left. No matter what, “shared immortality” would not emerge from this tiny bottle.

The Queen Mother asked again, “If I grant you the medicine for shooting down the sun, how will I explain it to the next worthy hero?”

“Let him prove his merit.”

“After proving it, what? The peaches still aren’t more, the potion still isn’t more.”

Hou Yi didn’t rush to answer. He finally saw that rules also require people to admit limited resources when facing those who deserve compassion.

Aman wrote until his old pen left a single hair. He squeezed the mostly bare pen shaft and sighed softly, “The potion’s destination isn’t decided yet, but my pen has already split.”

The Queen Mother said, “At least one strand is still in your hand.”

“So I should celebrate its reunion?”

Hou Yi laughed once, his tense expression easing a bit. Yet when he looked at the potion again, the joke didn’t erase the problem.

Before leaving the stone room, the Queen Mother resealed the three doors. She didn’t grant the hero’s honesty on the spot; she asked Hou Yi to return the next day to the water’s edge of the Jade Pool, where a ripe peach would be offered, but the decision was not yet final.

“I still need to see one thing,” she said.

“What?”

“When you say you’ll guard the mortal world, do you first think of a longer name, or the people who need you now?”

Hou Yi left the grove. He had feared he wouldn’t get the medicine, but now he feared another thing: even if he got the immortality potion, he might not get the life he wanted.

The water’s edge of the Jade Pool had no music or dance; only clear spring water, mountain fruit, and one ripe peach lay on the desk.

The peach sat on a jade plate in the center, its fragrance drifting on the wind. As Hou Yi settled, a white crane guarding the garden peered over the fence. Aman sat at the back, looking at the peach, then at the crane: “Both are calm, but who will blink first?” The crane’s eyes were like deep blue stars, trembling softly, as if weighing whether that calm was worth a laugh. Hou Yi furrowed his brow, his fingers lightly tapping the jade plate, a crisp sound like a heartbeat. Aman lifted his head, squinted, lifted his mouth in a slight smile, and whispered, “This isn’t an ordinary peach; we need to be careful.” The crane suddenly lifted its head, blinked, as if replying to Aman’s warning, then lowered its beak to peck gently, as if saying, “Don’t worry, I’m here to guard.” Hou Yi chuckled softly, his gaze warm yet wary, his fingers sliding over the jade plate, testing the peach’s spirit. Aman’s fingers unconsciously brushed the edge of the scroll, producing a faint rustle like a tiny wind chime. The crane’s feathers rustled in the breeze, a gentle hiss accompanying the silent conversation. Hou Yi lifted his head, a flash of humor in his eyes, and softly said, “If it’s willing to wait, it can wait until I leave.” Aman lifted his head, his mouth curving up, and softly replied, “Then let it wait.”

Hou Yi said, “If it’s willing to wait, it can wait until I leave.”

“Then it’s won for sure. The crane needn’t go home.”

The Queen Mother had not yet taken her seat when a green bird suddenly darted in from the mountain gate. Its wings were dusted with fine snow; after landing, it reported that the wind had changed at the mountain’s waist, and last night’s melt had collapsed a narrow path. Several herb gatherers were still on the mountain, trapped on the other side of a cliff, the stone layers below still shifting.

Hou Yi rose immediately.

A servant reminded him that the Queen Mother would arrive soon, and the decision about the medicine might be announced right there. Hou Yi looked at the jade plate, picked up the bow and rope lying at the foot of the stairs, and said, "The decision can wait, but the loose rocks won't."

He followed the blue bird to the waist of Kunlun Mountain. The stone path had been broken into two sections, with a deep ditch in between. The people collecting medicine were crowded on the narrow platform, with rocks constantly falling behind them, and they couldn't jump or wait for long.

Hou Yi found a sturdy old pine tree with a stable root system, wrapped a thick rope around the trunk, and tied the other end to a heavy arrow without the sharp tip, shooting it towards the stone pillar on the other side.

The arrow wrapped around the stone pillar, and the people on the other side tightened the rope knot according to Hou Yi's instructions, creating the first suspension bridge between the two walls. However, the single rope swayed too much, and the people still couldn't cross.

Hou Yi didn't urge the trapped people to take risks. He first asked the people on the other side to lower the baskets filled with medicine, and then tried sending a short rope to test the suspension bridge. The basket was barely off the stone platform when it started swaying left and right, causing the medicine leaves to fly out of the seams. Aman was watching from a safe distance and couldn't help but say, "The medicine hasn't even left the mountain, and it's already getting a check-up from the wind."

Hou Yi retrieved the basket, moved the heavy objects to the bottom of the basket, and added a forked branch to the rope knot, so the basket wouldn't hang from a single point. The second test sent the basket swinging less, but it still spun around in the ditch. It became clear that one rope couldn't transport people safely; Hou Yi had to add another rope from a higher position and keep the two ropes at a stable distance.

The second arrow with the rope head flew over the deep ditch, but it landed lower than expected. If they fixed it directly, the people crossing would have to bend down, and their balance would be even worse. Hou Yi didn't force its use just because the arrow had been shot. He asked the people on the other side to release the knot and retrieve the arrow, and then observed the wind again. Aman wrote on the bamboo slip, "The second arrow wasn't used," and then added, "Shooting the stone pillar doesn't mean finding a solution."

The wind lifted from below every now and then. Hou Yi waited for the wind to drop before shooting again, and the arrow flew around the higher stone corner, finally landing at the right spot. The people collecting medicine on the other side followed his instructions, wrapping the rope around the pillar twice and then reversing it, so the force wouldn't be concentrated on one spot. The two ropes were at different heights, and someone still had to test them first.

The youngest person collecting medicine volunteered to try, but as soon as he stepped onto the lower rope, he started swaying like a pendulum. He tried to grab onto the rope, but the higher rope started swinging wildly. Hou Yi told him to step back and not to rely on bravery instead of a method. The second time, he first tied the waist rope to the higher rope, and then moved one step at a time, stopping to adjust before taking another step. When he reached the middle of the ditch, the wind blew his clothes into his face, and he couldn't see the path, so he froze in place.

Aman shouted from the other side, "Don't talk to the wind about clothes, just stuff it into your waist!" The person collecting medicine couldn't free his hands, so he used his chin to press down his collar and slowly moved forward. As soon as his feet touched the ground, he said, "The wind in Kunlun is very affectionate, but its way of greeting people is a bit too enthusiastic." The people around him smiled briefly, and their tense shoulders finally relaxed.

After the test rope was successful, the young person returned to the other side and helped his companions tie the waist ropes in order. Hou Yi remained at the old pine tree, adjusting the tension of the two ropes, so whenever one side took a step, he would tighten the other side. The rescue wasn't just about shooting two arrows across; it was about testing the objects, adjusting the ropes, re-shooting, testing the path, and then helping each person cross one by one. When the last old person stepped onto the suspension bridge, the rocks on the cliffside were just starting to fall.

Hou Yi shot the second arrow again. The two ropes were at different heights, with a lower spot to step on and a higher spot to hold onto. He told the most stable person to try the rope first, and then go back to help his companions one by one.

When the last old person reached the middle of the bridge, a rock suddenly fell from the cliffside. Hou Yi tightened the higher rope, making the old person lean against the wall; the blue bird on the other side called out, reminding the people to step back. The rock brushed against the lower rope, causing the suspension bridge to swing violently, but the old person was finally pulled to safety.

After the danger was over, the people collecting medicine thanked Hou Yi with the medicine. He didn't take it, but told them to leave it for the people who needed it in the valley below. By then, the sun was already past noon.

Aman followed them all the way, recording everything from a safe distance, without touching the bow or replacing Hou Yi's arrangements for the rescue. When the people had finished crossing, he finally wrote on the bamboo slip, "The peaches at the banquet are waiting for you, and they're more patient than the guests."

"And what about the Queen Mother?"

"She's waiting too. But she's not as impatient as the white crane."

When they returned to the Jade Pool, the clear water was still full, and the peaches on the jade plate hadn't been removed. The Queen Mother sat by the pool, asking Hou Yi why he hadn't waited for her to make the decision first.

"If I had to listen to an answer about immortality, and let the people in front of me lose their lives," Hou Yi said, "I wouldn't know what to do with the years I'd gained, or who to protect."

"Don't you fear losing the medicine?"

"I do," he admitted, "but fearing loss doesn't make the broken path repair itself."

The Queen Mother didn't praise him on the spot, but told the people to collect the two ropes. Just as the decision was about to be announced, Hou Yi hadn't secured a place for his own immortality.

She pushed the jade plate towards Hou Yi. He still didn't take the peaches, but asked, "Have all the people who were trapped been rescued?"

The blue bird replied, and only then did Hou Yi take a few peaches, giving the rest to the blue bird, which was tired from escorting the people. Aman stared at the last peach on the plate, his pen tip hovering above the bamboo slip, but he didn't reach for it.

The Queen Mother asked, "Don't you want to try it?"

"I do," Aman replied honestly, "but I just finished writing 'the recorder has no share,' and the ink hasn't dried yet."

The Jade Pool had a brief burst of laughter. However, the actions on the mountain path still hadn't solved the Queen Mother's last problem: whether a hero who saves others should also get something that others can't have – eternal life.

That night, the Queen Mother didn't mention the glass bottle.

She led Hou Yi through the stone gate at the north of the Jade Pool. Behind the gate wasn't a medicine room, but a path leading deep into Kunlun, where many stone rooms, clear springs, and clouds that never dispersed filled the air. Many empty platforms followed the mountain slope upwards, disappearing into the stars.

"If you stay in Kunlun," the Queen Mother said, "you can choose not to take the medicine that grants immortality. From the initial breathing exercises, guarding the heart, and controlling the breath, through enough years of practice, you might be able to transcend mortal life. This way, you wouldn't need to break the rules of the medicine."

Kunlun did have practitioners who achieved enlightenment through long and arduous practice, and the Queen Mother was willing to open the mountain path for Hou Yi. This path wouldn't require the rare medicine.

Hou Yi walked along the stone platforms for a while. There were no barren land, ferocious beasts, or houses that needed repair on the mountain. If he stayed here, he might be able to avoid aging; many years in the mortal world would pass like a brief gust of wind.

But this was the problem.

He remembered that before leaving, Chang’e had sewn his quiver and reminded him not to eat less just to save time. If he stayed in Kunlun today, by the time he finished the long platforms, the mortal world would have changed with the seasons.

Hou Yi asked, "Can I go back and bring Chang’e here first?"

"She also needs to undergo her own practice and make her own choice," the Queen Mother replied. "If everyone brings the people they can't bear to part with, the boundaries of immortality will disappear."

Hou Yi didn't argue. He walked to the highest platform that could be seen, looking down at the valley below. The lights in the distance were from the villages in the mortal world, flickering like fireflies. Those lights would be extinguished, but new ones would be lit by later people. He had said he wanted immortality to protect them, but if the first step towards immortality was to leave them behind, his words would be just a pleasant excuse.

He looked up at the sky, and the stone platforms below him seemed to be a winding dragon, gently swaying, as if reminding him that the distance between the mortal world and the realm of immortals was not a straight line. Suddenly, a small squirrel jumped down from the vine beside the platform and landed at his feet, making a clear "click-click" sound. Hou Yi smiled and stroked the squirrel's fur, and the squirrel blinked its eyes, as if saying, "Don't worry, don't worry, don't worry."

He continued walking, and each step on the stone platforms seemed to strike an ancient drumbeat, echoing through the valleys of Kunlun. In the distance, the Jade Pool sparkled, with a full moon reflected on its surface, like the eyes of a guardian. Hou Yi's heart was filled with both a longing for immortality and a love for the mortal world.

When he reached the top of the stone platforms, the clouds below him seemed to rise like a thin veil, revealing a path leading to the Jade Pool. Hou Yi reached out to touch the path, feeling a warm energy, as if someone was whispering in his ear, "You can go, or you can stay."

He took a deep breath, his eyes determined but also hesitant. At that moment, the wind from Kunlun blew, carrying the fragrance of peaches from afar, reminding him that immortality came with a price, and he had to make a choice between the rules of immortality and human emotions.

He looked up at the sky, and in his heart, he whispered, "If I stay in Kunlun to practice, can I still visit the mortal world often?"

"Can I still go down the mountain often after I've finished my training?"

"At first, you can't. If you keep chasing after the outside world every day, you won't be able to keep up with your studies here."

"How long will it be?"

"I won't give you a false short time. Maybe it will be so long that the people you're familiar with will have already grown old."

Hou Yi sat on the stone steps, thinking for a long time. He was afraid of growing old, and he was also afraid that Chang’e would leave him first; but if he left her to avoid his fear, the long life he would gain would be the very separation he didn't want to see.

When it was about to get light, he walked back to the stone gate.

"I won't stay in Kunlun," Hou Yi said. "If the medicine can't be given, I'll go back empty-handed. Human life is limited, and it's even more impossible to spend a large part of it waiting for me to become immortal alone."

The Queen Mother of the West asked, "You're giving up a path that has a real chance of achieving immortality, won't you regret it?"

"I will regret it," Hou Yi still didn't say he was completely without weakness. "When I'm old, or when I meet people who can't be saved, I'll probably think about this day. But if a choice only counts if it's not regrettable, then there are no truly difficult choices in the world."

The morning light shone over the snow peaks. The Queen Mother of the West saw him pick up his bow again, but she didn't ask him to make a decision. Refusing to stay in the Jade Pool alone showed that he wasn't treating the human world as a temporary place to stay before achieving immortality.

However, the Queen Mother of the West didn't immediately give him the medicine. She couldn't automatically answer all future requests with her true heart. She had to judge not only one person's true heart, but also how many people would be led to the same door if one rule was bent for the sake of a true heart.

Hou Yi waited in the stone hut while the Queen Mother of the West went back to the medicine room alone.

On the jade table, there were four things: an old arrow taken from his quiver, representing saving the human world by shooting down the sun; a piece of broken shoelace, representing him walking through Kunlun himself; the rope used to rescue people on the mountain path, representing the current decision to save people first; and finally, three-layered jade boxes containing the only bottle of elixir.

If you only looked at the old arrow, the achievement of saving the world would be enough to move any listener. Ten days ago, he had risked his life to shoot down the sun that was causing disaster, saving not just one village or city, but the entire world. If he didn't acknowledge this achievement, it would be like a cold stone that only says "no" to order.

If you only looked at the elixir box, it would be easy to refuse. The elixir was only enough for one person, and Hou Yi had achieved great things today; tomorrow, someone else would come with their own achievements. If all good deeds were reduced to achieving immortality, people would start asking how many years of life they could exchange for their good deeds.

The Queen Mother of the West put the rope next to the old arrow. Hou Yi had rescued people while waiting to see if he would get the medicine, and he had refused to stay in Kunlun alone. This couldn't make the elixir increase, but it showed that there was already one bottle that wouldn't be given to someone who only wanted to escape death.

Giving him one bottle wouldn't satisfy his original request. Hou Yi had asked to share immortality with Chang’e, but the elixir's power only allowed one person. The Queen Mother of the West couldn't just say it without explaining the cost.

She opened the first layer of the jade box, but then stopped.

The shoelace showed that Hou Yi was willing to walk for his requests, but it couldn't be the sole reason for giving him the elixir. Otherwise, the person who was most willing to endure hardship would get the elixir before the one who should bear the responsibility. The old arrow, the rope, and his two choices had to be combined to justify one limited exception.

If the rule never allowed for judgment, it wouldn't need a guardian, only a door that would always be closed. But if the rule was changed with every tear, it would no longer be a rule. The Queen Mother of the West's decision had to fall between two options: acknowledging one great achievement that could change the fate of many people, and limiting exceptions to the existing one bottle; or acknowledging Hou Yi's emotional attachment, but never creating a non-existent second fulfillment.

The elixir could be given to Hou Yi, but the reason wasn't "all those who have achieved great things can ask for immortality." It was because he had stopped a great disaster, and in his new choice, he had proven willing to bear the consequences. The elixir still only had one bottle, and it wouldn't be accelerated for his wishes, nor would it be divided because of his deep love for Chang’e, nor would it be hidden after he took it. The person who accepted it would also accept the responsibility of guarding, choosing, and possibly being separated.

The Queen Mother of the West drew the boundary on the jade leaf: refusing completely would be unfair to people's emotions, giving without limits would destroy order, and limited giving would leave the choice honest for the recipient.

She opened the three-layered jade box and took out the crystal bottle. The pale light of the elixir shone on the jade table, but it didn't illuminate the entire stone room, and it didn't make a second bottle appear.

The Queen Mother of the West didn't immediately take the bottle out of the medicine room. She first checked the decision on the jade leaf against the record on the bottle: one bottle, only for one person, cannot be divided, after taking it, one will ascend to the heavens, and the recipient must be aware of the consequences. The green bird repeated the words, and Aman also repeated them according to his own record. Only when the two meanings were the same did they continue to seal the bottle.

The first layer wrapped the bottle with plain silk to prevent it from being damaged on the way down the mountain; the second layer put it in a sandalwood box, with soft wood blocking the bottle's neck; the third layer was the plain string seal. Aman watched the green bird wrap the string three times and asked, "Does wrapping it three times make it safer?"

"Wrapping it more times won't be recorded, but it will only make the next person who opens it think that the seal has been tampered with," the Queen Mother of the West replied.

Aman quickly retracted his hand, "I was just wrapping it in words." He wrote "three times" on the bamboo slip and drew a picture of the string being pressed on which knot. The green bird gently shook the box, and the bottle inside didn't rattle; it then tilted the box slightly, and the bottle remained fixed in the center. All the checks were only to ensure that the medicine could be delivered intact to Hou Yi's hands, and that it wouldn't change the fact that there was only one bottle.

The Queen Mother of the West took out two jade slips. One was left with the box, recording the elixir's power and prohibitions; the other was left in the Jade Pool, recording the reason for giving the elixir and the date of delivery. This way, if people in the future only saw Hou Yi holding the elixir, they wouldn't think that Kunlun could be arbitrarily asked for; if people in the human world said that there were two bottles, the Jade Pool would have the original record to verify.

Aman wrote "cannot be forcibly divided" and then asked seriously, "If someone says that one person one mouth is fair?" The Queen Mother of the West pointed to the unbroken elixir, "Fairness isn't forcing something that can't be divided into two parts, and then having two people bear unknown consequences. If the elixir is insufficient, it should be acknowledged as insufficient."

He wrote this sentence after the restrictions, without adding a joke. Just now, the box could be sealed with a few easy words, but when it really came to two lives, the words had to be clear. The Queen Mother of the West finally checked the seal on the box herself, confirming that any person who opened it would leave a mark, and then ordered the green bird to go and call Hou Yi. She was giving him not a prize without explanation, but a responsibility with clear origins, boundaries, and consequences.

The Queen Mother of the West sealed the bottle's mouth herself before ordering the green bird to go and call Hou Yi. After the elixir left Kunlun, order could define boundaries, but it couldn't eliminate consequences.

She had still made her decision.

This wasn't a case of the Queen Mother of the West yielding to human emotions or human emotions being chased away by rules. She had limited the elixir to one bottle, leaving a road that could be borne between the two: acknowledging the hero's weight in saving people, limiting the order of guarding the elixir, and the true cost of the medicine had to be heard by Hou Yi before it was delivered.

When Hou Yi walked back into the medicine room again, the crystal bottle was already on the jade table.

The Queen Mother of the West didn't say "give" first, but let him stand in front of the table to see the elixir's quantity clearly. The pale light was still the same as yesterday, not increased because he had saved people on the mountain path, nor divided because he had refused to stay in Kunlun.

"This elixir can make one person immortal," she said. "After taking it, the elixir's power will make the person ascend to the heavens. The people who stay behind will still grow old, and their original homes, friends, and daily life will not stop with the person who takes the elixir."

Hou Yi asked, "If I and Chang’e each take half?"

"The elixir cannot be forcibly divided. I cannot guarantee that the remaining elixir's power will bring immortality, nor can I guarantee that you two will still be able to stay in the human world."

"If we never take it?"

"It will remain a precious treasure that needs to be guarded. People who know that the elixir is in your hands may respect you, but they may also covet it. You will not only have the choice, but also the danger that comes with it."

Hou Yi could have taken the bottle immediately, but he still didn't stretch out his hand. The elixir in front of him couldn't fulfill his wish to be immortal with Chang’e, and it might even push them in opposite directions.

Aman stood by the door, holding the green bamboo slips. He had carefully written down "Only one person," "Ascend after taking," and "Cannot be divided against one's will," and also recorded the price of seeking the medicine. The old brush was unusually quiet, not even shedding a single hair, as if it too knew that this was not a place to escape the truth by pretending to be ill. He looked up at the Queen Mother of the West, a hint of tension flashing in his eyes, but he dared not say more. The Queen Mother's stern expression was like a towering mountain peak, and the water in the jade pool around her seemed to shimmer in her gaze. "Hey, Hou Yi, do you want to take this bottle of medicine?" The Queen Mother's voice was like a gentle breeze from a distant valley, both warm and authoritative. Hou Yi raised his hand, but it never reached out. The medicine in front of him couldn't fulfill his wish to stay young with Chang’e, and might even push them in opposite directions. Aman coughed softly, his voice like a small bird chirping in the bamboo forest, breaking the tense silence. "Aman, don't treat your brush like a magic wand, we're not playing hide-and-seek here," the Queen Mother smiled, her eyes slightly raised, as if reminding him that this was no game. Aman lifted his hand and tapped the bamboo slips gently, producing a clear sound, like a child playing a wooden flute, and a brief laugh rippled across the water in the jade pool. "I'm just recording, recording this rule," he whispered, his voice trembling with a hint of anxiety. The Queen Mother nodded, a hint of softness in her eyes, but her expression remained stern. "Hou Yi, do you still want to take it after knowing this?" she asked, her voice like a gentle breeze from a distant valley, both warm and authoritative.

The Queen Mother asked Hou Yi, "Do you still want to take it after knowing this?"

Hou Yi thought for a long time before saying, "I want to take it, but I won't take it in the Kunlun Mountains, and I won't deceive Chang’e by deciding for her. I'll take the medicine back and tell her everything I've learned today, and then we'll make a decision together."

"No matter who wants to take it," Hou Yi said, "we must first listen to the reason why another person wants to leave alone."

Hou Yi still hadn't solved the problem of who should live forever, but he no longer thought of the medicine as a shortcut for both of them to stay young, and he no longer made decisions for others in the immortal realm.

She lifted the outermost layer of cloth, pushing the crystal bottle to the edge of the jade table.

"I gave you this bottle of medicine because you shot down the sun to save humanity, and because you chose to save the people on the mountain path and at the stone gate, I broke the rules to give you this bottle of medicine," the Queen Mother said. "But this is the only exception. There is no second bottle, no hidden easy way to take it, and no promise that everything will remain the same after taking it."

Hou Yi took the small crystal bottle with both hands, holding it more steadily than a bow. Inside was both eternal life and the possibility of one person leaving and the other staying.

Aman came forward and asked him to repeat the warning. Hou Yi said it all out, and the small official finally wrote it down on the bamboo slips: the Queen Mother gave Hou Yi the medicine; the medicine could make one person live forever and ascend to heaven; Hou Yi had to go back home and decide with Chang’e together, and take responsibility for guarding it.

"Can't I leave out a single word?" Hou Yi asked.

"If you leave out 'one person,' people in the future will think the bottle can hold a whole family; if you leave out 'the price,' everyone will only remember how pretty the bottle is," Aman held the bamboo slips tightly, "My brush may shed hair, but I won't let a single word be erased."

The Queen Mother didn't delete any of the restrictions. She ordered someone to bring a wooden box, put the crystal bottle inside, and seal it with a plain rope.

Before Hou Yi left the jade pool, he bowed to the peach garden and the medicine room. He didn't become an immortal, and he didn't get a guarantee that he and Chang’e could live forever together. The Queen Mother gave him a gift with boundaries, and a choice that no one could make for him.

The cloud gate slowly opened, and Hou Yi walked down the mountain with his bow slung over his shoulder. The wooden box with the medicine was hidden deep inside his luggage, taking the problem of eternal life with him as he walked further and further away from the Kunlun Mountains.

After Hou Yi left the mountain, the jade pool continued to operate as usual.

The peaches didn't ripen prematurely because one bottle of medicine was given out, and the jade stove didn't immediately produce another batch. The green birds in the garden continued to inspect the branches, and the white cranes continued to pretend to watch the clouds from a distance. If you only looked at the pool and the peach garden, this decision seemed to leave no mark; but the medicine room was one bottle short, and the human world had one more choice that had yet to bear fruit.

The Queen Mother returned to the jade steps, checking the bamboo slips written by the small official.

The small official wrote down the previous events in order: on the ninth day, Hou Yi shot down the sun; he came to the Kunlun Mountains to seek medicine because he didn't want to leave Chang’e behind; he learned that the medicine could only save one person, so he saved the people on the mountain path first, and then refused to stay alone in the jade pool; the Queen Mother therefore limited the gift of medicine, and made Hou Yi understand the consequences of one person ascending to heaven.

He also left a line in the empty space of The Twenty Bamboo Slips of Mountains and Seas: if the bamboo slips led him to the East Sea to see Jingwei carrying wood, he would first have to understand why she insisted on doing so, and not just count the number of stones.

"What should I write at the end?" Aman asked, "Write 'the hero returns with a full load,' it sounds like he's carrying a basket of peaches; write 'the medicine is obtained,' it sounds like all the difficulties were for nothing."

"Write the truth."

"The truth is a bit long."

"Your bamboo slips aren't short either."

He had to continue writing. When he wrote "eternal life has a price," the old brush shed a single hair. The small official picked up the hair and sighed, "It probably understood the price and decided to demonstrate it itself."

The Queen Mother had a hint of a smile in her eyes, but it quickly returned to calmness. She told him that after the medicine left the Kunlun Mountains, the jade pool couldn't follow and clean up the consequences of every choice. Hou Yi might not take the medicine for years, and people with greed might come to hear of it, and Chang’e might face a decision that no one had ever anticipated in a moment of crisis. If the record only said "the medicine was given," people in the future would think that eternal life was a reward at the end of the story, and forget that the bottle had always contained separation.

Aman stopped joking and rewrote the five characters more deeply.

He asked again, "Since that's the case, why not let me stay in the jade pool and lock the medicine box far away?"

"Because the medicine has been given to the one who should bear it, and you cannot take responsibility for others," the Queen Mother said. "Besides, you only recorded the rules in the Kunlun Mountains, and might think that all choices in the world can be made clear on the jade steps. From now on, go out and see the world, and record how people make choices in different places, under different circumstances."

The small official was taken aback, "Is this an assignment?"

"It's a chance to see the world," the Queen Mother said. "If you only complain in the hallway, at least you should know how many things in the world are worth stopping to complain about."

The small official looked down at the big bundle of bamboo slips, "Can the world arrange its own order? I'm afraid that by the time I get halfway, the bamboo slips will be wider than me."

"No."

"Then it's just like the real world."

This time, the Queen Mother didn't answer, but handed him a green silk cord with a water pattern from the jade pool embroidered on one end, and a blank space on the other.

Before he left, he looked back at the peach garden. The peaches ripened extremely slowly, the medicine was limited, and the endless years of life might become an eternal separation for another person. Without order, treasures would be fought over; without compassion, the rules would reject both merit and attachment.

The Queen Mother used one bottle to set a boundary, acknowledging Hou Yi's weight in saving humanity, but also making him clearly understand his responsibility.

Aman placed the bamboo slip with this story at the front, and carried the rest of the slips to the cloud gate. He only recorded the path, boundaries, and consequences of every choice, leaving the decision and the medicine to the protagonist of the story.

Outside the cloud gate, the wind from the Kunlun Mountains rustled his clothes. He pressed the old brush and muttered, "Don't drop any more, there's no peach leaf to cover you up in the human world," and then followed Hou Yi's path to the human world.

The jade pool returned to its usual tranquility. The Queen Mother still guarded the unripe peaches, the closed medicine room, and the order that wouldn't automatically please people. Eternal life has a price; true guardianship is to see the consequences in the most moving moments of compassion, and to make decisions with restraint in the most weighty moments of order.
