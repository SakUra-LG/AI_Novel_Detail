As summer arrived, the wetlands where medicinal herbs grew were becoming increasingly scarce.

A few children had cut their feet on the shallow shore, and the elderly people used the stored leaves to dress their wounds, saying that if they encountered the same injury again, the leaves wouldn't be enough. Someone mentioned the distant land of Leize, where the water and grass were lush, and reeds and fragrant herbs grew all year round. Others immediately added that the land was constantly filled with thunder, and people who had been there before would be caught by the cloud-dwelling Thunder God to guard the gates.

Huaxu asked, "Who has actually seen the Thunder God catch people?"

The people looked at each other, and finally a young man said that his uncle's neighbor had heard a passerby mention it.

"This rumor has traveled farther than our journey to Leize," Huaxu said.

She didn't laugh at the rumor or underestimate Leize. Thunderstorms, marshes, and getting lost were all real dangers, but "danger" couldn't automatically grow into a Thunder God who caught people to guard the gates. Huaxu found an elderly person who had reached the outskirts of Leize, asked about the mountain pass, river tributaries, and high ground where they could stop, and then had the companions prepare food, ropes, and baskets to carry grass and wood.

Someone asked if they should bring a shovel to search for treasure. According to rumors, where there was more thunder, the ground was likely to have glowing stones hidden beneath.

Huaxu handed him a small shovel: "Use it to dig reeds. If you find treasure, first ask if it can cure foot wounds."

The young man looked at the shovel, thinking that the treasure had not yet been found, but had already been assigned a very practical task.

This journey was led by Huaxu, but it wasn't a wild adventure to search for treasure. She wanted to confirm the route to Leize, collect the grass and wood that their tribe really needed, and observe the water conditions to see if they were suitable for future travel. The companions all knew that when they encountered thunder, they should retreat to the high ground behind the low trees, and when they encountered deep mud, they should use long poles to probe the path, without chasing after any strange noises.

Before setting out, Huaxu visited a few households, noting the shapes and growth locations of the medicinal herbs that were lacking. Someone could only say, "It's green, thin, and smells like grass," and she brought three leaf samples for them to identify. After they looked at them, they confirmed that the descriptions were enough to bring back the entire patch of grass.

"So when we get to Leize, we'll recognize it first, then collect it," Huaxu said. "We can't let every single blade of grass follow us home just because the road is far."

The sky didn't immediately rain, but the low rumble came from the ground's edge, with varying intervals. The farther they went, the wetter the soil became, and the more reeds they saw.

The group changed course to cross the river at the upstream rocky shore, and the empty baskets and food didn't get stuck in the mud. This small detour made the companions understand that the dangers of Leize weren't all from the thunder, and that the silent places on the ground also deserved respect.

If the adult footprint was just a giant old mark, entering it might not lead to anything; if it was connected to the Thunder Sea's mysterious phenomenon, this step would bring the unknown to her.

When they went to fetch water for the third time, the woman guarding the door asked, "Are you here to help with the birth, or are you going to turn this house into a temple for the Thunder God?"

They finally put down the bucket and went to add more firewood. The laughter was soft, and it wasn't mocking Huaxu or turning the birth into a comedy. It simply reminded the people outside to stick to their tasks.

As the night deepened, the thunder and the pain had their own rhythms. Huaxu focused on her own body, not letting the outside reactions disturb her. She knew that the life inside her came from the Thunder God's footprints and that what she really needed now was the experience of the birth attendants and her own determination.

As dawn approached, the signs of labor grew stronger. Huaxu entered the prepared birthing room, and the door closed behind her. The warm water, cloth, and attendants were all in place, and the tribe members retreated outside to wait.

The Thunder God's supernatural phenomenon, the tribe's long care, and Huaxu's practical preparations all converged at the same moment. The sound of the attendants' calm voices came from inside the room, while the people outside kept their positions, waiting for the new life to be born.

The long night was still going on.

Before dawn, the thunder rumbled again.

Huaxu could hear the thunder from inside the birthing room, but she didn't get distracted by looking for the Thunder God's direction. The pain grew stronger, and the older woman stood beside her, reminding her to breathe slowly and conserve her strength in the intervals. The people outside were quiet, with only the crackling of the firewood audible.

The birth was longer than anyone had expected. Huaxu gripped the rope, focusing on each time she was about to give up. The supernatural feeling of pregnancy hadn't spared her the real effort, and the awe outside couldn't help her complete this final stage.

Someone heard the thunder getting closer and wanted to push open the door to take a look, but the woman guarding the door stopped them. Huaxu had already asked for the birthing room to be quiet; respecting her choice was more important than watching the so-called descent of the divine being.

Just as the sky was starting to lighten, with a thin mist still lingering in the valley, Huaxu stood at the door of the simple birthing room, her hand tightly gripping the ancient wooden staff she had carved from the Thunder God's rock. Her eyebrows were slightly furrowed, but her eyes shone with a star-like determination – this was the "wait for the right moment" she had written on the green bamboo slips of Aman. Aman hid in the dark corner under the eaves, holding the bamboo slips and whispering a prayer, occasionally glancing up at Huaxu and muttering, "If I made a mistake in my notation, I might end up recording the Thunder God's footprints as chicken claw marks." His voice was carried away by the wind, but it added a hint of subtle humor to the tense atmosphere.

Suddenly, a soft light sliced through the clouds, like a silver ribbon, gently sweeping across the rooftops and landing softly on the stone floor inside. The light wasn't harsh, but it carried a warm, gentle feeling, as if the earth mother's fingers were softly stroking. Following close behind was a low, distant rumble, like the beat of an ancient drum echoing through the valley, rolling in without any flashes of lightning or eerie shapes. The thunder boomed against the rocky walls outside, producing a "boom-boom" echo, like the heavens were composing a prelude for the life about to be born.

Huaxu closed her eyes, took a deep breath of the mountain's fresh air, and then, following the midwife's instructions, slowly poured her accumulated life energy into her abdomen. Her hands rested lightly on her belly, and her fingertips trembled slightly, like a tiny stream flowing through a rocky crevice. She whispered an ancient incantation for nurturing, her voice low and rhythmic, as if calling upon the power of heaven and earth. Aman stood beside her, his bamboo slips trembling in his hand, and he whispered to himself, "If I write 'nurturing' as 'nurturing', I'm afraid I'll end up recording the Thunder God's footprints as footprints." As he spoke, he accidentally tapped his pen tip against the edge of the paper, producing a faint "click" sound, which startled the mice hiding in the walls, causing them to scurry out of their holes and disappear into the darkness, leaving behind a few faint squeaks, like a silent tribute to the sacred ritual.

The light inside grew softer, and Huaxu's breathing became more rapid and rhythmic. Her brow furrowed, but her lips curled into a faint, gentle smile – a mix of reverence for the life about to be born and determination to fulfill her mission. Her fingers pressed lightly on her abdomen, feeling the tiny kicks of the fetus, like tiny drumbeats resonating within her chest. Meanwhile, Aman's bamboo slips were filled with the words "footprints, thunder, light, nurturing", and he would occasionally look up, gazing at Huaxu's back, muttering to himself, "If I write 'footprints' as 'footpaper', I'm afraid even the Thunder God would laugh at me."

Just then, the stone floor inside began to tremble slightly, as if an invisible force was pushing from within. Huaxu's breathing grew deeper, and her voice followed suit, becoming lower and more resonant: "Heaven and earth unite, all things are born." Her hand rose slightly, her fingertips lightly touching her abdomen, as if guiding the force to emerge smoothly. The light at this moment became even brighter, yet still soft, like the first rays of dawn, shining upon her hair and casting a golden glow.

Finally, a clear, powerful cry shattered the valley's silence, echoing between the rocky walls.

It wasn't thunder, nor a dream omen.

Huaxu had given birth to a real, living infant. The midwife carefully wrapped the child, confirmed his breathing and crying were strong, and then placed him beside his mother. Huaxu saw the small face and clenched hands, and her long-held anxiety finally dissipated.

"It's a boy," she whispered.

This phrase was more important than any grand prophecy. The Thunder God's pregnancy, which had stopped at mysteries, dreams, and abdominal signs, had finally become a real life, born through Huaxu's genuine labor.

People outside heard the cry, and at first, there was silence. Then, some began to weep, while others released their tightly clenched hands. The distant thunder began to fade, and the morning light streaming through the clouds entered the courtyard. No monsters broke in, and no heavenly messengers took the infant away.

The guard first passed on the news to the waiting crowd, but didn't immediately let everyone rush in. Warm water and food were brought in, and the priority remained the mother and child resting together. The tribe's reverence was first expressed as restraint, rather than a desire to see the miraculous.

Huaxu fed the child his first milk, letting him rest in her arms. She was exhausted and needed rest; the infant needed warmth and care. The tribe was allowed to visit one by one, each staying only for a moment, without reaching out to touch the infant, as if waiting for him to display his divine powers.

A child asked, "What's his name?"

As she gazed at the infant in her arms, she remained silent for a moment. The child's name would be decided after the mother and child were safe and sound, no need to rush it before the first cry. For now, the very fact of birth was enough.

The midwife announced to the tribe that mother and child were safe. The child was indeed born of her own womb, born of her own hands, and the mysterious power of the Thunder God had finally found its complete expression.

As morning light crept in, the thunder faded into the distance. The mother closed her eyes to rest, the infant lying beside her, occasionally moving its tiny fingers. Civilization's roots lay not in the achievements of future generations, but in the simple, quiet moments shared by mother and child in this one night.

In her heart, she called him Fuxi, but she had not yet announced it to the outside world. She held the child's small hand in hers, still weary, and the tribe outside finally shifted from waiting for a miracle to waiting for her permission to come and offer their blessings.

A few days after the mother and child rested, she carried the infant to the courtyard.

The tribe had been waiting for a long time, but no one came to the door, asking about the thunder as they used to. Some brought soft fabrics, others repaired the roof tiles, and the child brought a beautiful leaf it had chosen, saying it was the only gift a baby could give.

She named the child Fuxi.

As soon as the name was settled, the infant opened its eyes. Everyone around immediately tried to read the future in that moment, some saying it would look up at the sky and be connected to thunder, others saying it would look to the left and grow up to walk the left path.

The mother gently turned the child back to her chest: "He's just waking up. If you all want to assign a whole life to every blink of his eyes, he'll be too busy to keep up."

The people laughed and stopped making predictions. Fuxi was now crying, sleeping, holding his mother's fingers, and when hungry, letting everyone in the house know. The ordinary moments of a real infant didn't diminish the mysterious origin of the Thunder God's power, but rather made it truly enter the world of human life.

Aman sat by the courtyard, opening the green bamboo slips. He first checked the adult footprints he had written down from the Thunder God's path: the soles, the arches, the direction, and the water ripples of the thunder; and the footprint of the woman, who walked the path once and then returned to the tribe, feeling pregnant, going through a long pregnancy, and giving birth to Fuxi herself.

He didn't explain the principle of feeling pregnant, nor did he write what Fuxi would do in the future. Someone came over to ask if he could add the phrase "This child will surely have a great destiny," but Aman shook his head: "My pen only saw him gripping his own fingers with determination. If I wrote any further, it wouldn't be a record, but stealing his future."

The mother heard and nodded at him. The green bamboo slips could record the miraculous birth, but not turn the infant into a symbol for people to predict.

The tribe rearranged their lives. Those taking care of the mother helped her recover, while others continued to gather grass, guard the water, and work. Fuxi's arrival hadn't stopped all their tasks, but had given them a new life to protect together. Those who had advocated for the mother to leave the tribe also brought firewood, and without explaining themselves, asked if the house was warm enough.

As dusk approached, a faint thunder echoed from the distance. Fuxi didn't show any divine power, but slept peacefully in his mother's arms. The mother looked towards the Thunder God's path, remembering the one water ripple in the adult footprints. The miracle was no longer just an unexplained mark on the riverbank; it had breathing, body temperature, and a name.

Aman wrote the final line: "Huaxu went to the Thunder God's path, saw the adult footprint, felt pregnant, and gave birth to Fuxi." He finished writing, and heard the infant's cry from inside, so he deepened the three characters "gave birth to Fuxi" once more.

As he closed the green bamboo slips, Fuxi woke up and cried once. Aman had initially wanted to record "The Sage opens his mouth," but heard that the cry was actually for his mother, so he changed it to "The infant wakes up." The mother took the child close, and the crying stopped soon.

The Thunder God's adult footprint still remained on the distant river, but Huaxu had walked the long path of pregnancy, and Fuxi had been accepted by the tribe as a real infant. The faint light of civilization had already begun to shine between the mother, child, and a tribe that had learned to respect and not be amazed by the unknown.
