The water in the Northern Ocean was so deep you couldn't see the bottom, and so wide you couldn't see the shore. A small fish swimming among the water grass for a day would think it had traveled a long way. If you asked it where it had been, it would point to the rock it had just left in the early afternoon.

The Kun lived in the deeper parts. When it swam through the dark water, the currents would change direction first. The small fish would see the water grass bend down in the distance and know the Kun was coming. By the time the water grass stood up again, the Kun's figure would still be passing by.

One small fish had swum along the Kun's back for a long time and, when it returned, told other fish it had found a moving mountain. Another fish asked, "Does the mountain have a tail?"

"This one does."

"Does it have eyes?"

"I didn't swim to that side."

"Then how do you know it's a mountain?"

"Because if I said it was a fish, it would mean I swam too slowly."

The Kun didn't argue with them. The Northern Ocean was big enough to accommodate a group of small fish who thought their limited experience was a unique landscape. It swam slowly along the familiar deep water, with only dim water light above its head and schools of fish flowing by its side.

One day, a long, unfamiliar sound came from above the water. The sound was not like waves or the noise of fish in the water; it was as if an invisible river was rushing by from the edge of the sky. The small fish hid in the water grass, saying the Northern Ocean above must be getting angry again.

The Kun stopped and listened for a long time.

After the sound passed, the deep water became quiet again. The Kun could have continued its usual days, but the next day at the same time, the wind sound came from outside the water again. On the third day, the wind sound changed direction, yet still stretched far away. The Kun began to notice where the light was coming from and discovered that the Northern Ocean's water flows, although many, mostly went back and forth between familiar sea trenches.

The Northern Ocean's water could suppress most sounds, but this noise penetrated the layers of seawater and still carried the power to go further. The Kun, for the first time, didn't turn along its old route but swam towards the place where the water color was brighter.

As it swam upward, the water flow became more turbulent. The frightened small fish scattered from its side, complaining that the "moving mountain" had taken the wrong path today. The Kun slowed down, letting the fish swim ahead, and then continued to rise. It wasn't chasing any prey, nor was it being driven out of the deep water; it just wanted to know where the sound came from.

Along the way, there was a dense patch of water grass. If the Kun had swum straight, the entire patch of water grass would have been displaced. Instead, it swam around the grass field, rising from a narrower deep trench. The small fish saw it making way for them and gained some courage, following behind, though still saying, "We're not curious, we're just afraid you won't find your way back."

The Kun didn't expose their lie. It knew that by "coming back," the small fish meant returning to places they could understand.

The water color changed from black to blue, and then from blue to bright. The Kun had never seen so much light before and initially thought it had reached the end of the Northern Ocean. But as it continued upward, it found that the top wasn't a stone wall, but a shimmering brightness.

It stopped just below the surface. The waves passed above, and the sunlight was broken and then reassembled. The long sound was outside the water, one wave after another, rushing towards a farther place from the north.

The Kun swam along the bright water surface for a while. The cloud shadows above moved in the same direction but were faster than any fish; after a moment, the clouds had already passed ahead, while the Kun was still in the water. The water surface wasn't the end of the Northern Ocean, but more like a door that separated two ways of traveling.

The small fish followed to the shallower area, dared not go further, and asked from a distance, "What's above?"

The Kun looked at the water surface illuminated by the light: "There's more."

"How big is it?"

The Kun didn't answer. It was still a fish in the Northern Ocean and couldn't know the boundaries of the world outside the water. But from that day on, the deep water was no longer all it knew, and the familiar old route was no longer enough to contain its vision.

The Kun stayed under the water surface for many days. When daylight came, it saw cloud shadows moving above; when night fell, it saw starlight flickering in the waves. The sky outside the water changed every day, but the old water routes in the deep remained in the same place.

The small fish initially accompanied it to watch, but later gradually lost patience.

"Clouds are just white shadows on the water surface."

"Wind is just water passing from above."

"The south is just another body of water. If you want to go, just swim there."

The Kun asked, "Have you been there?"

The small fish said in unison that they hadn't. After a pause, they added, "Exactly because we haven't, we save ourselves the trouble of going for nothing."

The Kun tried swimming along the water surface towards the south. It swam faster than the schools of fish and more steadily than the currents, but every time the sky wind passed from above, it clearly felt that although it was heading south, it was still being pressed under the same sky and earth by the water surface. The wind could cross the Northern Ocean, cross the distant mountains and waters that couldn't be seen; but a fish's body could only follow the sea.

It had once followed a cloud shadow to the edge of the Northern Ocean. The cloud passed over the mountains on the shore and continued south; the Kun, however, had to turn around in the water at the foot of the mountains. It wasn't that it disliked the slowness of the water path, but it was the first time it was certain that the direction it wanted to go didn't always have connected seawater.

It first poked its head out of the water. The waves fell beside it, and the long sky suddenly opened up completely. The north wind rushed past its eyes towards the south, and the clouds moved together at a higher altitude, without water grass blocking the way or sea trenches dictating the path.

The giant fish dove back into the sea. The little fish swam up to ask, "Have you seen enough?"

"No."

"Then look again tomorrow."

"What I see tomorrow will still not be where I want to go."

The little fish didn't understand. They thought the North Sea was vast enough, and the giant fish was big enough, so what more could it want? But the giant fish knew that its size was just its appearance in the water, and it couldn't cross the boundary between water and sky.

An old turtle said, "All things in the world follow their own paths, and fish should stay in the water." The little fish nodded in agreement, thinking this was a safe and easy way to live.

The giant fish asked, "What if our paths are just habits that have never changed?"

The old turtle withdrew its head into its shell: "That's still better than thinking too much."

The giant fish didn't ask again. Stability could explain why it stayed, but it couldn't explain why it always looked up.

It swam deeper into the North Sea. The little fish thought it had given up, so they celebrated in the seaweed, thinking their advice had worked. But the giant fish didn't return to its old route. It dove to a depth where it could gather all its strength, then turned around, heading upwards.

The water pressure was on its back, and the familiar ocean currents pulled it to the side. But the giant fish didn't follow. It gathered its strength, which it had used to swim forward, and stopped circling, no longer waiting for the currents to decide its direction.

The little fish realized that the giant fish wasn't retreating, but preparing for an unprecedented ascent.

"Are you going to break through the surface?" they asked.

"I'm going to leave the place where I can only swim."

"What can a fish be if it leaves the water?"

The giant fish looked up at the sea surface, which was getting brighter. It didn't have a ready answer, but it had made its choice: if its old form couldn't hold its new direction, it wouldn't cling to what it was used to.

It swung its huge tail and shot upwards from the depths of the North Sea, heading towards the light. The water closed behind it, and the little fish scattered. The sea surface was getting closer, and the sound of the wind above the water was getting clearer.

This ascent was different from its previous exploration. Last time, it had stopped at the surface; now, it didn't leave any place to stop. It put the south, the sky, and the distant lands that couldn't be reached by water all in the same choice, and continued upwards.

At this moment, the giant fish was still a fish. The next moment, it would answer for itself what it could become if it left the water.

The surface of the North Sea surged violently, like a submerged mountain rising into the sky. The waves retreated in all directions, and the giant fish's back was fully exposed to the sunlight for the first time.

He gently shook his hand, trying to retract the ink from the brush tip, but accidentally touched a small crab that had crawled up from who-knows-where. Aman's mouth was agape, and the bamboo slip almost fell into the sea. He quickly grabbed it with his other hand, and his fingertips were covered with sea salt, leaving a few small white dots on the bamboo surface, like starry marks. He looked up at the giant bird again, and saw that its chest fins had transformed into broad wings, with feathers shimmering in the morning light like the clouds in the sky set ablaze.

At that moment, the June wind howled from the distant horizon, whipping up waves and pushing the newly transformed giant bird. The bird's wings flapped gently, creating a series of wind sounds, and huge whirlpools immediately appeared on the sea surface, with water splashing everywhere, as if the curtain between heaven and earth had been lifted.

Aman's eyebrows relaxed, and a faint, barely noticeable smile played on his lips. He whispered to himself, "This 'having fish' is really something, and it can even turn into 'having a giant bird'." As he finished speaking, he gently placed the bamboo slip in his hand on a rock, and the tip of his brush accidentally knocked against a protruding seashell, producing a crisp "ding" sound, like a touch of childish music added to this magnificent scene.

The wind grew stronger, and the giant bird's back was fully exposed, its wings like clouds hanging from the sky, its chest feathers rustling in the wind, like the beat of a drum between heaven and earth. Its body drew a huge arc on the surface of the sea, then broke through the water vapor and soared into the sky.

Aman looked up, his eyes filled with surprise and awe, his hand holding the bamboo slip trembling slightly, the brush tip leaving an incomplete character "its" on the slip. He knew that the giant bird before him was no longer the Kun of the North Sea, but a bird with a back

Aman changed to another slip and added "of unknown extent". A small fish poked its head out of the waves and said, "You've written too little."

"I know."

"The bamboo slip knows better than I do; it's already run out of space."

The laughter had barely faded when the sea surface rose again. The Kun broke through the water, and the sky wind came face to face with it. It didn't fall back into the old water but instead continued to rise with the accumulated power. Its broad fins, like those that had opened up the deep water, unfolded in the long wind, transforming into a pair of wings that could support the sky; the shimmering scales on the water's surface turned into layered feathers, and its huge tail changed direction in the wind.

The transformation unfolded along the same upward motion, without stopping halfway to wait for others to marvel. The broad back, once used to push through the deep water, rose toward the sky, and its body, which had been close to the sea surface, gradually took on the contours of a bird. The North Sea supported it one last time and sent it completely into the wind.

This was not an uncontrolled accident, nor was it a matter of piecing together many strange things. Every upward movement the Kun chose was met with a response in its new form. The vast expanse that had grown out of the water did not disappear but was transformed from a fish body suited to the deep sea into a bird shape suited to the vast sky.

The seawater fell back into the North Sea from under the newly formed wings. The wings, now fully extended for the first time, blocked out a patch of sky, and the chatter on the shore and in the water came to a stop.

The Kun had transformed into a bird, named Peng.

On the surface of the sea, the wings, tail feathers, and bird shape pointing toward the sky were clearly visible. Later, even when the giant Peng returned to the water's surface to rest, this new form would not revert to its Kun body.

Aman held the remaining bamboo slips tightly, his old brush hovering above the slip without rushing to leave a mark. Just now, he had complained that the bamboo slip was too narrow, but now he felt that no matter how much he wrote, he shouldn't block the real change happening before his eyes.

A small fish poked its head out from behind the waves and asked, "Where did the mountain go?"

Another fish looked up at the sky and said, "Maybe it got tired of what we were saying and flew off to change its name itself."

The giant Peng heard this but didn't turn back to argue. Its identity had been established through its actions and didn't need the shore to name it or the small fish to agree. Above the North Sea, there was no longer a giant fish waiting for change; there was only a giant Peng spreading its wings for the first time.

It had not yet truly flown away from the sea surface or reached the south. But from this moment on, the Kun was finally no longer the Kun. All the winds, clouds, and distant travels would be faced by the giant Peng itself.

The nearby wind, however, only blew against the tips of its feathers, unable to lift its entire body. The wings rose and fell, and the immense power pushed the waves toward the distance, but it remained hovering above the water's surface.

"You looked like you could fly just now."

The giant Peng thus understood that a fish could swim in the water by its own strength, but a bird in the sky could not pretend the wind didn't exist. Its wings were so vast that ordinary small winds could only brush against the tips of its feathers; if it forced itself to chase the wind, it would only stir up the North Sea, creating waves, and still wouldn't be able to go very far.

It left the open sea and came to a high cliff at the edge of the North Sea. The wind would rise as it passed over the mountain wall, and then fall on the other side. The giant bird, Peng, didn't rush to charge into the clouds; instead, it stretched out one wing to feel the wind. When the wind dissipated, it retracted its wing and settled down.

"I'm waiting for the wind."

Peng looked at them as they nimbly turned around the cliff, but it didn't argue. The small birds' wings were suited for the valley in front of them, and a gentle breeze was enough to lift them. However, Peng wasn't heading for the nearby treetops or taking a short route that would bring it back after circling the cliff.

The small birds demonstrated how to use the wind between the treetops to turn. Peng watched carefully and learned to retract its wings to let the wind carry it, rather than just using its vast size to overpower everything. Its new form brought it strength, but not all the experience it needed; acknowledging what it didn't know made each attempt yield new results.

He didn't decide the bird's destination; he simply wrote what he had seen: the giant bird riding the June winds, its wings like clouds, flying south. After finishing, he tucked the old brush into the green bamboo slips, lest the last gust of wind blow his records away.

The bird rode the June winds, taking no orders from anyone, nor completing any task for others. The South Sea Heaven Pool waited in the distance, and the bird had set its own course.

The giant bird flew over the North Sea, heading towards the South Sea Heaven Pool. Behind it was the deep water it had lived in, and ahead was the unknown heaven and earth; this long-awaited journey had finally crossed the North Sea's boundaries.

After leaving the North Sea, the giant bird flew over the clouds for a long time. The June winds continued to blow from beneath its wings, and it didn't need to flap them every time; it only adjusted its direction when the wind changed.

At first, the North Sea was like a vast, clear mirror behind it. Later, mountains and rivers appeared, and the fish's lifelong reef, once a massive obstruction, became just a small dark shadow on the water's edge from up high.

The giant bird didn't think the things below were unimportant just because they had grown smaller. It was these rivers, valleys, and the lives within them that gave its journey meaning. The bird just no longer considered any one place the entirety of the world.

A flock of migratory birds, carried by the wind, saw the giant bird and asked where it was headed.

"The South Sea Heaven Pool."

The migratory birds said, "There's a lake not far from here, with clear water, plenty of fish, and a shore where you can take shelter from the wind."

The giant bird thanked them but didn't change course.

“Why fly so far?” the migratory bird asked, puzzled. “Don’t all the places we land look the same?”

“If all I want is a place to rest, I don’t have to leave the North Sea.”

The bird found that logic reasonable, but it also felt the journey was too long. So it decided to fly a bit and then head toward a nearby lake. Before it left, it warned the others that the southern clouds were thick and could easily throw off their sense of direction.

In the afternoon the clouds indeed closed in. The land and water were hidden, leaving only the sun above and the wind beneath the wings. A side gust then blew toward a closer valley. There were trees and a resting spot; if the great albatross followed, it could land quickly.

The albatross didn’t slam into the side wind to show off. It slightly tucked one wing against the wind, letting itself drift off course, then used the main June wind to glide slowly back south. The clouds hid the ground, but they couldn’t block the sun’s path, nor could they change the long journey of the wind from north to south.

After cutting through the clouds, a strange shimmer of water appeared ahead. The albatross didn’t descend right away. The South Sea’s sky pool was still farther away; every drop of water along the way could be a reason to rest, or an excuse to change course.

A flock of birds from the lake flew up to greet it, enthusiastically pointing out the fruit on the shore and the wind‑shielded caves. The albatross thanked them and caught a smooth air current above the lake, but it didn’t stay long. Accepting friendly gestures along the way doesn’t mean you hand over your destination to the first place you see.

It skimmed the sky for a moment, letting the wind carry it farther while conserving its strength. Freedom isn’t drifting aimlessly; it’s choosing a faraway spot while refusing to be pulled back by nearby safety—that’s the true liberty of this journey.

In the evening, the wind had weakened a bit. The albatross landed on a high mountain for a brief rest. Small birds gathered around, asking if it was a cloud that had fallen from the sky.

The albatross replied, “I come from the North Sea.”

“How far is the North Sea?”

“Only after flying over it do you realize how far it is.”

“Will you go back?”

The albatross looked south and said, “First I’ll reach the place I’m headed to.”

The next day a strong wind blew again. The albatross spread its wings from the mountain and continued toward the South Sea’s sky pool. The cloud sea opened behind it, and the shimmering water ahead grew wider; the South Sea was now clearly visible.

The migratory birds it had met earlier were no longer nearby, and the small fish of the North Sea couldn’t see this stretch. With no one watching its long trek, the albatross kept its course. It wasn’t flying higher to prove itself, just as it hadn’t left the deep water to make the small fish give up.

With both wings steady in the open sky, the albatross no longer looked back at the North Sea; it poured all its power into the journey ahead.

When the South Sea’s sky pool appeared beneath the clouds, its surface reflected the morning light. It was as vast as the North Sea, but with different winds, different shores, and mountains the albatross had never seen before.

The albatross didn’t circle in the high sky to show off. It folded its wings and, following the long southern wind, descended slowly. The clouds beneath it spread out, and the pool’s water rippled into wide waves.

While descending, a side wind from the South Sea pushed it toward the mountains. The albatross didn’t fight the wind; it looped around the ridges and then used the rising wind from the pool to lift itself back above the water. Reaching the shore wasn’t just about spotting a place name; only when its wings were folded and its feet touched the pool’s edge did the long voyage truly end.

The fish in the pool first saw a cloud shadow pressing down and hurried to the depths. When the albatross landed on a high shore, they realized that shadow had feathers and wings, and carried wind dust from the far north.

A fish asked, “Are you staying here, or just passing through?”

The albatross bowed its head and drank from the sky pool, replying, “First I’ll rest.”

“What after the rest?”

“I’ll decide then.”

The fish thought that answer was flimsy. It rolled in the water, its scales flashing rainbow shards in the light, and muttered, “If you’re staying, stay; if you’re leaving, leave. Better to decide your future today!” Two little stars twinkled in its eyes, as if it wanted to write all its doubts on the water’s surface.

The albatross didn’t build another wall for itself. Its back scales had turned into layers of light feathers, and its huge pectoral fins slowly unfolded, as if it wanted to capture the ocean’s vastness within its wings. The deep blue of the North Sea roiled behind it, and the sea surged with thousands of waves from its vibration. On the crests, a few curious small fish leapt up, looked up at the gigantic figure, and, amazed, opened their mouths into an “O” shape. Then one little fish couldn’t help but tap the water with its tail, making a “plop‑plop” laugh, as if saying, “Wow, this isn’t an ordinary fish—how did it turn into a flying bird?”

It soared from the far north, not to replace the old shackles with new ones. The hot wind of June was brewing on the surface of the sea, its sound like the ancient drums beating softly, gently tapping on its wings. The Great Peng unfolded its wings, the wingtips like clouds hanging in the sky, gentle and vast, instantly cutting the sea surface in two, with water mist drifting like white silk. At that moment, Aman stood on the reef of the north sea, holding a bamboo slip in his hand, his eyebrows furrowed, the tip of his brush scribbling out a few crooked characters: "Record—record—record—". He looked up at the Great Peng, muttering to himself, "This bird is so big, even the wind is afraid of it?" His voice was carried away by the wind, but a few gentle laughs remained, even the small fish in the sea shook their heads laughing.

The Great Peng's back was like Mount Tai, its wings like clouds hanging low, riding the June wind, carrying the sky on its back, flying towards the southern sea pool. Its silhouette drew a glowing arc across the horizon, leaving a trail of sparkling water vapor, and all living beings saw only a bird with wings like clouds hanging low, their hearts shocked and silent, completely acknowledging that it was no longer a bird, but a free Peng.

The June wind continued to blow over the sea pool, flowing further away. The Great Peng folded its wings and stood on the shore, seeing how the clouds rose in the south, and how the birds chose their own paths in the mountains. There was no imperial edict, no one holding a rein to control it, and no one dictating what it must do when it arrived.

The southern sea was not a prison to confine it, but a new world it had reached on its own; whenever the wind rose again, it would choose its own path.

The Great Peng stayed at the southern sea pool. It could land on the water's edge to rest, fly up high to watch the clouds, or take off again when the wind came. Resting did not mean being trapped, and setting off again did not require proof to anyone.

A few days later, the sea pool was blown by a strong wind again. The sea creatures thought the Great Peng would leave immediately, and swam to watch. But it still stood in a high place, only watching the clouds. The wind could bring opportunities, but it could not dictate when it should take off.

A fish asked, "Is this wind not good?"

"Very good."

"Then why not leave?"

"Because staying is also my choice."

The fish asked again, "You came from the north, were you always this kind of bird before?"

The Great Peng looked towards the north. The deep water was already far away, and the old form of the giant Peng was not following.

"I originally lived in the water."

The fish waited to hear a strange transformation, but the Great Peng did not retell its already completed transformation. It only unfolded its wings, and a shadow like a cloud appeared above the sea pool.

The fish looked up for a long time, "So what do you call yourself now?"

"The Great Peng."

The name fell, and the wings also lifted up in the wind. The Great Peng did not leave the southern sea immediately, but flew above the sea pool, looking back at the direction it had come from for the first time. The north sea, the June wind, the vast clouds, and the sea pool in front of it were connected into a path that it had chosen for itself.

It landed back in a high place, quietly folding its wings. Roaming was not forever without stopping, nor was it without attachment; it was able to choose to fly towards the sky in the deep water, to choose to fly far in the wind, and to decide when to take off again.

Later, the Great Peng sometimes flew over the southern sea mountains, sometimes still resting on the shore of the sea pool. The local birds and beasts no longer asked it whether it was a guest or a resident, because it did not need a fixed identity to authorize its every takeoff and landing.

The surface of the southern sea pool returned to calm. The Great Peng had completed its journey from the north sea giant Peng to the southern sea far bird. When the next suitable wind came, would it again unfold its wings like clouds hanging low, not bound by its old form, not bound by observers, and not bound by any command.
