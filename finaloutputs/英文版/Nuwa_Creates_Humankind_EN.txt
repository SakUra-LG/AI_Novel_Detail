After heaven and earth split apart, mountains and rivers found their heights and depths, plants grew with the turning seasons, and birds and beasts each found a place to call home.

As Nüwa walked through the mountains, she often heard flocks of birds calling to one another in the morning light. When she came to the river valley, deer led their fawns to drink; when she passed through the forest, monkeys plucked fruit from high branches for their friends; even tiny ants lined up in neat rows to carry food back to their nests.

The world was not silent, yet a silence remained that nothing could quite fill.

Nüwa sat on a mountaintop and asked the flying birds what kind of life suited the distant valleys. The birds chirped a few times and flapped their wings toward the direction of the fruit. She asked the deer why they always lingered by the water, but hearing a human voice, they cautiously stepped back, unable to answer her thoughts about home.

She did not blame them. Birds and beasts had their own ways; they could sense wind and rain, raise their young, and rely on one another. They just couldn’t discuss with Nüwa how to keep river floods from washing away their nests, couldn’t remember together which season brought the most fruit, and wouldn’t ask a follow-up question after she finished sharing her long stories.

One day, Nüwa rescued a small beast trapped under a fallen log in the woods. The mother paced anxiously nearby, and as soon as the cub was free, she rushed close, repeatedly nudging it with her nose to make sure it was safe. Seeing this care, Nüwa suddenly realized that the life she sought should be more than just something that obeyed commands.

New life needed to feel for one another and understand parting and reunion; it needed to use voice to express needs and understand others’ answers; and it needed hands to change its surroundings, rather than waiting forever for a creator to do everything for it.

She walked far along the river, imagining how such creatures, if they appeared in groups, would share food, hide from rain, argue, and make up. Thinking of people sitting together in conversation, the empty valley seemed to already have an echo; but when she stopped, there was still only the wind rustling the grass.

At night, the stars lit up the earth. Nüwa lit a small fire and tried telling her daytime questions to the flames. The fire danced, as if listening carefully, then promptly ignored her to burn through a dry twig.

“You’re quite good at nodding,” Nüwa said, “too bad you won’t say a single word.”

She chuckled, and her laughter quickly scattered into the night. This moment of loneliness wasn’t about having no living things around her, but about having no one else who could understand her smile and send the laughter back.

The next day, Nüwa came to an open plain. Here, water was plentiful, the soil was soft, and trees provided shelter. She decided to start her experiment here, creating a being that kept the vitality of natural life but could also think, speak, and work together.

She didn’t start right away. If she just patched together bird and beast features, the new life might still only follow instinct; if she made it too powerful without understanding how to care for others, the world would just gain another fierce creature.

Nüwa first observed how she herself walked, picked things up, and expressed emotions. She raised her hand and found her fingers could hold water and weave branches; she stood straight, which let her see farther and freed her hands for work. As for her face, joy, anger, sorrow, and happiness all left traces there, making it perfect for companions to understand.

The answer to what form the new life should take was right within herself. But form was only the first step; she still needed to find a material that could be shaped and yet sustain life.

At dusk, Nüwa walked toward the clear water in the center of the plain. The surface was calm as a mirror. There, she would see her reflection more clearly and look among the ready-made things of heaven and earth for a way to make her imagination stand firmly on the ground.

Nüwa bent down to look into the water, and a face appeared in the reflection.

She raised her hand, and the reflection raised its hand; she turned, and the figure in the water showed the outline of upright walking. Compared to four-legged beasts, this body could leave the hands free for labor; compared to birds, although it couldn’t fly far by nature, it could build bridges and rafts to find ways to reach the other shore.

Nüwa first tried using tree branches. Wood could stand, but it was too stiff; the bends broke easily, and it couldn’t show the subtle changes of a face. She then chose stones from the riverbank. Stone was sturdy, but carving an outline took a long time, and if she wanted new life all over the earth, she didn’t know how long she’d have to wait.

Water was the most flexible, but it couldn’t hold a fixed shape. As soon as Nüwa sketched facial features on the surface, the ripples moved and scattered everything.

She saw the damp yellow earth on the bank. After the river receded, the soil was neither as hard as stone nor as formless as pure water. Nüwa grabbed a handful and tried kneading it directly, but the soil was too dry and kept falling through her fingers; add too much water, and it became too soft to stand.

She left both failed lumps of mud by the river. The dry soil told her it needed water; the thin mud told her it needed enough weight to support itself. Nüwa added small amounts of river water one by one until the lump could be shaped easily without spreading out the moment she let go.

She molded a rough shape based on her reflection in the water: a base that could stand, hands preserved above, and a head facing forward to see the world. As soon as she placed the first outline on the bank, its stance leaned to one side, and it immediately tipped over.

Nüwa picked it up, widened the feet’s contact with the ground, and balanced the whole figure. The second time, it stood, but it looked like a quiet mud pillar, with hands pressed tight against its sides, unable to pick anything up.

She separated the shoulders from the arms so the hands could reach forward and upward, and work together. Then she left space on the face for eyes to observe, ears to listen, and a mouth to speak. She didn’t copy bird beaks or beast teeth, because the new life needed to exchange meaning with sound, not just catch food.

The reflection in the water swayed gently with her movements. Nüwa didn’t aim for every part to be exactly like hers. If all life had only one face, they would be hard to tell apart; she kept only the common basic form, leaving room for faces and heights to differ later.

The mud figure could finally stand steadily on the riverbank. It still had no life and wouldn’t blink when the wind blew. Nüwa walked around it for a long time, confirming that the yellow earth could hold the shape, the river water made it flexible, and her own reflection had given it upright posture and hands.

The next step was the hardest: turning the mud from a shape into a life that could feel and act on its own. If she breathed life into it too early, the unstable mud figure might collapse immediately; if she only kept polishing the outside, it would forever remain a quiet statue by the river.

Nüwa kneaded the rough figure back into a lump of mud. She had already determined the material and direction, so there was no need to keep a failed piece that still couldn’t move. Then, she selected fresh yellow earth, mixed it with clear water, and prepared to handcraft the first true human, from feet to face.

Sunlight fell on the river, and the inspiration from the water’s reflection was now clear. The yellow earth and water were no longer just random things she’d grabbed; they had become materials capable of carrying change. Nüwa sat on the flat ground by the bank and began her most detailed shaping yet. She first kneaded the yellow earth into a lump, gently rolling it into a round ball, as if making herself a little “bread.” Then, she dripped clear water onto the earth ball, which instantly sprouted tiny water beads, like a baby just waking up. Nüwa pressed her fingers lightly into the lump, slowly pressing it into a rough head, with a few drops of water occasionally bubbling out of the mouth, as if saying “glug glug.” She stuck her fingers into the mud, as if picking eyebrows for the little person; the eyebrows turned out like two thin willow branches, looking a bit comical. Then, she lifted the mud lump and gently tapped it against a stone on the bank; the lump went “thump thump,” like a little person who had just learned to walk. Nüwa placed the lump in her palm and stroked it gently, as if soothing a newborn baby. She gave the lump a light shake, and mud particles flew off like little stars. Nüwa smiled, thinking to herself: This is a mother’s first attempt. It’s a bit clumsy, but it’s the beginning of life.

Nüwa placed the mixed yellow earth in her palm.

For her first attempt at shaping a person, she first molded feet that could stand firmly on the ground, then shaped the body upward. The first arms were too long, and the little figure leaned forward under its own weight as soon as it stood up; she didn’t hide the failure, but gathered the mud back, shortened the arms, and allowed the waist to turn with movement.

The second mud figure stood, but its hands could only stretch in one direction. Nüwa thought that hands needed to hold water and also grasp fruits of different shapes, so she separated the palms and fingers, allowing them to bend, grip, and release.

She then molded a face. The eyes faced forward to easily see companions’ expressions; the ears stayed on the sides to hear calls from different directions; and the mouth was not just for eating, but also for speaking names, asking questions, and laughing.

The clay had dried in places where the wind blew, cracking into fine lines on its surface. Nüwa gently smoothed it with a damp fingertip, then placed the unfinished figure under a tree’s shade. She knew that creating life wasn’t just about speed—materials have their own moods, and she had to find a balance between softness and firmness.

When the final touch was done, the clay figure stood before her, a little taller than the palm of her hand. Its outline resembled Nüwa’s, but it wasn’t a tiny copy: the eyebrows and eyes had their own shape, and its hands would soon choose different tasks once life filled them.

Nüwa first steadied its shoulders, letting its feet touch the earth, then slowly breathed life into the yellow earth. The breath spread from its chest to its limbs, and the dull clay color warmed gradually. The clay figure’s fingers twitched, then its eyes opened.

It came to life.

The newborn looked up at the sky and stared for a long moment; hearing the river, it turned toward the bank. It tried to step forward—its first step was too hasty, its body wobbled, and Nüwa reached out to help, but the little one spread its arms on its own and found its balance.

The second step was steadier. By the third, it could circle around to Nüwa’s other side, watching its creator with curiosity. Then it opened its mouth, and the first sound it made wasn’t a word—just a mix of bird chirps and wind.

Nüwa pointed to herself and slowly said her name: “Nüwa.”

The clay figure tried a few times, then finally called it out clearly. It pointed at itself, eyes full of a question.

“You are a human,” Nüwa said.

“Human,” it repeated.

That single word fell onto the once‑silent plain, and for the first time another voice truly understood and answered Nüwa. She laughed, and the newborn tried to laugh too—first just copying the curve of her mouth, then, as if feeling genuine joy, the giggle grew more natural.

Questions poured in quickly. Why does water flow? Why are trees taller than me? Can clouds be plucked down? Nüwa answered the first two; for the third she said they’d have to wait until the little one learned how to figure it out.

“Can you pick one for me?” the human asked.

“I could,” Nüwa replied, “but if you wait for me for everything, making two hands would be a waste.”

The newborn looked down at its hands and immediately cupped river water. The water slipped through its fingers; surprised, it tried again, and finally managed to bring its palms together. Nüwa watched the self‑correction, confirming that what she’d made wasn’t just a moving lump of clay but a being that could observe, fail, adjust, and act again.

The first clay person had truly experienced being molded, corrected, and breathed into, and now stood among humans. Nüwa’s loneliness received its first reply, but a new responsibility sprang up at once: a person needs companions and a way to live together.

She gathered more yellow earth. The next round didn’t have to start from total failure, but each new face, voice, and temperament should not be exactly the same. Nüwa prepared to shape more people by hand, turning that first answer into a chatter of many.

When the second person came to life, the first one rushed over to greet them.

It spun around its new partner and asked, “Are you a human too?”

It spun around its new partner and asked, “Are you a human too?” The newborn had just learned to speak, so it could only answer with a simple “Yes.” The first person nodded approvingly, acting as if it were a seasoned elder, completely forgetting that just that morning it had been asking whether clouds could be plucked. He lifted his hand and gave a gentle tap; tiny specks of mud on the new figure’s head puffed up like a little cloud, then rolled back into a small clump, and he laughed awkwardly, “Isn’t that a cloud?” Nearby, Nüwa looked up, smiling warmly, but she didn’t stop working with the yellow earth. She plunged her hand into the mud and shaped a slightly larger figure with thick eyebrows, then another with a tiny hand and sparse eyebrows. She brushed each forehead lightly, as if giving them a nod, then placed the two figures together, giving them a gentle shake. They bumped into each other, making a “clack‑clack” sound, as if greeting one another. “I want you to grow as tall as I am,” Nüwa said, her voice soft like a mother’s. The clay people seemed to understand; one raised an arm, another bowed its head, teasing each other. Nüwa kept shaping more figures from the yellow earth. She didn’t make them all identical: some taller, some shorter; some with booming voices, others better at quiet observation; hands of different sizes for different tasks. The variety came from life’s richness and didn’t decide who was noble or lowly.

One by one, the clay people came to life, and the riverbank was no longer silent. Some chased butterflies, some crouched by the water studying reflections, and one picked up a lump of unused mud and asked when it would learn to speak.

“Wait until I finish molding,” Nüwa answered.

The newcomer earnestly told the mud lump, “Hang on a little longer.”

Nüwa couldn’t help but laugh. The already‑alive ones gathered around, introducing the river, the trees, and the sky to the mud lump as if it could already understand. When the new companions took shape, they all rushed to teach it the word “human,” and seven or eight voices shouted at once, leaving the newborn unsure whom to copy.

Aman arrived at the riverbank and was almost swamped by a flood of questions. One little figure asked whether the green bamboo slips were edible, another wondered why an old brush had lost its bristles, and someone else wanted to know if their name could be written longer than anyone else’s.

Aman hugged the green bamboo slips and said, “Slips aren’t for eating; a brush loses bristles when it’s overworked. If a name gets too long, the one who writes it gets the trouble.”

Nüwa told everyone to ask one question at a time. Only then could Aman squat down and write each name in the voice each child preferred. He didn’t rank anyone, nor did he place anyone above another just because they were molded first.

At first the crowd needed shelter. Clouds gathered on the horizon, and everyone looked up, unsure what to do. Nüwa didn’t wave a hand to conjure houses; she broke off a long branch, planted it in the ground, and draped leaves over its sides, showing them how to copy it.

The loud‑voiced one was in charge of telling distant friends to fetch branches, the observant one noticed that leaves faced downward drained water better, and the strong‑handed one brought a sturdier support. The first person, eager to prove experience, grabbed a branch longer than himself, and when he turned around he swept three companions off their feet.

He quickly apologized. The knocked‑down ones got up, lifted the long branch higher together, and placed it atop the shelter. No one was forever blamed for the mishap, nor did the strongest claim command over everyone.

When the rain fell, the first shelter was already up. It wasn’t very wide, so the crowd squeezed inside, shoulder to shoulder. Someone who felt cramped saw a friend outside getting drenched and voluntarily gave up a spot.

Aman recorded the scene on a green bamboo slip: “The newborns’ first shelter was cramped; the roof hadn’t learned to be spacious yet, so they learned to shift aside.”

After the rain stopped, the group realized one shelter wasn’t enough and split into teams to build a few more. Nüwa only reminded them when a support was about to topple, never rebuilding for them. By dusk, several rain‑proof shelters dotted the riverbank.

Each hand‑molded clay person had a unique face and talent, yet all shared the same spark of life. Watching them ask questions, make mistakes, apologize, and cooperate filled Nüwa with a joy even more concrete than the first spoken word.

Yet she looked down and saw her own hands still caked in yellow earth. Beyond the vast mountains and rivers lay countless silent places, and even molding people nonstop day and night wouldn’t quickly fill the world with voices. She needed a way to create many more at once without erasing the differences that made each life special.

More and more people gathered along the river, but the distant lands still heard no human voices.

Every day Nüwa molded people from yellow earth, her hands busy from sunrise to starfall. She’d mastered how to make the clay stand and let varied faces appear naturally, but each new figure added only a pair of feet to the earth. The world was so vast that at this pace, countless places would remain silent for a long time.

A bigger problem came from the weather. Dark clouds gathered upstream, and a sudden downpour was imminent. If the stored yellow earth on the bank were washed away, even the already‑living folk would find food scarce because they’d all be huddled together.

Nüwa first had the crowd move the dry earth to higher ground. Everyone carried a basket, but it was still not enough. She noticed a vine rope that had been dragged through the wet mud, with many uniform mud spots on it. The vine was flexible, and when swung, it could send the mud flying to a distance without breaking like a hard wooden stick.

She dipped the vine rope into the prepared mud mixture and gently swung it towards the open area.

The mud spots fell, some too small, only forming a moving mud ball; others stuck together, merging into one when they stood up. Nüwa didn't directly increase her divine power; instead, she changed the amplitude of the swing, allowing the mud spots to have enough distance in the air before giving them life.

The second batch of mud spots fell, one by one, and took on the same basic shape as those made by hand, but each had a different face. When they opened their eyes, some laughed first, some looked at the trees, and some squatted down to touch the mud they had just left.

Aman was originally standing downwind, recording, and the first batch of mud spots almost filled his green bamboo slips. He held the slips to the other side and said, "If I get any closer, I'll be recorded as one of the newly created too." He raised his hand and gently swung it, and the mud spots rotated in the air like small clouds before falling onto the slips, leaving fine patterns. At the moment the mud spots fell, the air was filled with the fresh scent of mud. Aman's eyebrows furrowed, and he thought to himself, "This isn't an ordinary record; I have to be careful not to get caught by the mud people." Suddenly, a mud person emerged from the mud spots, holding a broken stone, like a child who had just struggled out of the mud, blinking big eyes and making a "gurgling" sound, as if asking for directions. Aman quickly pushed the slips aside, afraid of being trampled by the mud person, but the mud person grabbed the edge of the slips with a wet hand and pulled them, like a flag blown by the wind. He hurriedly raised his hand, wanting to record the scene, but found the mud person was using mud to shape his fingers into a small bird, with wings still flapping, looking very childish. Aman couldn't help but laugh out loud, and the laughter echoed in the empty sky. The mud person also laughed, making a "gurgling" sound, as if saying, "I want to laugh too!" At this moment, Nüwa appeared, holding a lump of yellow earth, and gently shook it, causing the mud people's bodies to slowly become complete, with eyes shining with the light of life. Aman's pen danced on the slips, recording every move of the mud people, silently praying: "May these new lives find their own light in this quiet world."

A newborn person asked, "Aren't you one?"

Aman was stunned for a moment: "I'm here to record you."

"Then who records you?"

He looked down at the blank corner of the slips and found this question difficult to answer. Nüwa laughed and said, "When they learn to record, they can record you too."

Aman quickly wrote down this sentence and retreated further, so as not to dodge the mud while mistakenly recording the colors. He didn't take the vine rope and didn't swing it for Nüwa; he just witnessed how the method changed from failure to success.

Nüwa swung the mud for the third time, and the vine rope drew a larger arc. The mud spots fell towards the grassland, gentle slopes, and river bays, and groups of people stood up from the ground. She controlled the direction, avoiding rapids and rocky areas, and made sure there were enough people in each place to take care of each other, rather than being alone in the wilderness.

Some people saw that the hand-shaped ones had more delicate features and worried that those born from the swung mud might be inferior. Nüwa had people from both sides work together to lift a fallen tree and then told them to say what they saw first. Some were good at listening, some were strong, and some were good at remembering the way; different strengths had no fixed relationship with their birth.

"You are all shaped from yellow earth, and you all have the same breath of life," Nüwa said. "The order and method of creation only explain how I created you, not how you should look at each other."

After understanding this, the people exchanged names with each other. The hand-shaped ones taught their new companions how to build shelters, while those born from the swung mud discovered a distant forest with edible fruits and led everyone to find the way. The initial differences were quickly replaced by their shared life.

The rain finally came down. Nüwa swung the vine rope one last time, sending the remaining mud to the high places that were not threatened by the flood. More people stood up from the rain, learning from their predecessors to support each other.

The vine rope's mud-swinging method greatly increased the speed of creation and allowed humans to spread beyond the riverbanks. When Nüwa put away the vine rope, calls and responses were already coming from different directions of the earth. The next thing to solve was not to continue pursuing quantity, but to enable these newly created people to truly take care of themselves and their companions.

The newborn crowd could walk and talk but didn't know how to arrange their lives.

On the first night after the rain, many people still gathered around Nüwa. Some ate all the fruits at once, some found clear water but refused to tell their distant companions, and some were afraid of the dark and clung to the people they first met, unwilling to go and add branches to the shelters.

If Nüwa were to distribute food, build shelters, and do everything for them, the same chaos would still appear the next day. She decided to demonstrate only once and then let the people complete the tasks themselves.

She divided a basket of fruits into three parts: for immediate consumption, for the next day, and for burial in the ground to wait for growth. Someone didn't understand why good fruits had to be buried, so Nüwa let him observe the young seedlings under the trees. The person discovered that there were already tender shoots next to the fruit cores and was willing to put some aside.

Water sources required even more cooperation. The riverbank was slippery, and the crowd easily fell into the water. Nüwa had the people who were good at observing the terrain find gentle slopes first, then had the stronger ones move the stumbling stones, and the others tried to make water-holding vessels with leaves and clay.

The first mud bowl was too thin and softened as soon as it was filled with water. The creator was about to throw it away, but someone suggested letting it dry in the sun first. The second bowl was made thicker and finally brought water to the elderly and children.

Language also expanded from individual names. Someone shouted "over there," and people in different directions turned their heads; someone said "come quickly," but didn't explain whether it was food or danger. Nüwa had them specify the location, things, and what needed to be said, and the listeners would repeat it to confirm.

A young boy ran over and shouted, "There's something big over there!"

Everyone tensely picked up branches.

Nüwa asked, "Is it moving?"

"No."

"Is it dangerous?"

"No, it's a tree full of fruits."

Everyone put down their branches, and someone sighed, "If you had said that last sentence earlier, we would have walked to the tree by now."

The laughter made the boy remember that expression wasn't just about being loud, but about making companions understand. From then on, people agreed on names for common plants, water sources, and directions, and distant groups could also convey messages through shouting.

When the night came again, not everyone crowded around Nüwa. They divided into groups to collect dry branches, used natural fire starters to light small fires, and cleared the surrounding area to prevent the fire from spreading. Those who didn't know how to build shelters followed the skilled ones, and those who had stored water shared their excess with more distant settlements.

Some people argued over who should live in the sturdiest shelter first. Nüwa didn't appoint an owner but asked who couldn't withstand the night rain. When they saw that there were young and slower-moving people among them, they let those people enter first, and the rest continued to reinforce the nearby shelters.

This kind of choice wasn't innate. Humans had the ability to think and might only consider themselves; therefore, caring and cooperation had to be learned through shared needs.

A few days later, the riverbank crowd could independently distribute food, fetch water, and keep watch. Another group of people went to the fruit forest along the mountain path, leaving clear markers along the way; the highland crowd shared their experience of avoiding rain with their newly arrived companions.

Nüwa walked from one settlement to another, only reminding them in urgent situations and no longer doing everything herself. She saw people imitate, correct mistakes, and teach each other, and she also saw them argue and then renegotiate.

Humans were no longer just mud people waiting for the creator's instructions. They could understand intentions with language, change their environment with their hands, care for and protect the weak, and settle in different mountains and rivers through cooperation.

Nüwa was pleased with this independence, but soon noticed a more long-term issue: the earliest people were growing up and would eventually grow old with the passing years. If life in the world only came from her yellow earth and hands, everyone would eventually disappear.

Nüwa saw white hair for the first time on one of the earliest people.

Time grows children up and slows the steps of the elderly. When someone passes away, friends and family stay by their side, puzzled as to why life cannot stay exactly the same forever. Nüwa tells them that plants flourish and wither, birds and beasts are born and age, and new lives too will eventually walk their own course.

People fear the earth falling silent again. They beg Nüwa to keep molding humans, to permanently replace every one who leaves.

Nüwa could take up the yellow earth again, but she knows this is no long-term solution. If humans rely solely on her creation, they can never truly own their own future; and she cannot stand guard by every river and mountain, adding members to each settlement one by one.

She observes that people have already learned to draw close to one another. Some are willing to work together long-term, some care for each other through illness, and some, when facing parting, think first of the same companion. This choice was not commanded by Nüwa, but formed naturally through living together.

Nüwa gathers people from all lands and explains that humans can bring forth the next generation through the union of men and women. Newborn children will no longer be formed directly from yellow earth; they will grow slowly under family care, inheriting some of their parents’ features while developing their own temperaments.

Someone asks if Nüwa will assign each person their partner. She shakes her head. “Living together requires mutual willingness. If I decide for you, family becomes just another kind of command.” Behind that gentle shake of her head, Nüwa’s brows hide a trace of compassion and faint helplessness. Her fingers lightly stroke the soft yellow earth, as if reminding herself that the process of creation should not be bound. Beside her, Aman mutters quietly, holding a lump of mud and trying to mold it into a little figure. The mud is slippery in his hands, slipping away several times, so Aman can only laugh and say, “This mud man loves to dance too much; I can’t shape him.” Hearing this, Nüwa smiles softly, a gentle light appearing in the corners of her eyes. She slowly kneads the yellow earth in her hands into a small body, then gently strokes it, as if soothing a newborn life. Then, she takes out a thick vine rope, flicks it lightly into the air, and the mud spins in the sky, like clouds blown by the wind. The mud figure falls from the air, landing softly on the ground, instantly gaining the rhythm of breath. Aman watches this miracle in surprise, exclaiming, “Wow, even mud can dance!” Nüwa merely nods gently, her eyes full of maternal tenderness. The first pair of partners forms a family under the witness of the crowd. They do not do so because their faces look most alike, nor because they were both molded by Nüwa’s hands or splashed from the vine rope, but because they have already drawn water and built shelters together for a long time and are willing to continue sharing their days.

Others gradually understand that marriage is not about randomly pairing two people. Some quickly find a companion willing to walk with them, some choose to wait a little longer, and others are better suited to live with relatives and friends without rushing to start a family. Nüwa does not rush them.

After the new generation of children is born, the entire settlement gathers at a distance to wait for news. When the first cry is heard, people first fall quiet, then burst into cheers. The elderly see faces that resemble their own yet are entirely fresh, and for the first time, they understand that life can continue forward beyond an individual’s end.

Parents initially do not know how to soothe their children. When a child cries, five or six people simultaneously offer water, fruit, and cloth, crowding the small hut so no one can turn around. Nüwa reminds them to first observe what is truly needed, while others handle food and rest; not every hand needs to reach for the same spot.

Families learn to raise children through practice. Parents teach children to speak, walk, and recognize danger, while others in the settlement help look after them when they are busy. When the children grow up, they pass on what they have learned to the next generation.

As the number of newborns increases, death no longer means a voice is lost forever. Names, stories, and ways of living are remembered by family members, and human forms and temperaments leave new variations in their descendants.

Nüwa stops molding new people day after day. She keeps the yellow earth and the vine rope as witnesses to the origin of life, but hands the power of continuation over to humans themselves.

Humans begin to reproduce, and families become the primary unit of care. Life still has an end, but the world no longer loses all its future when one person departs. What Nüwa creates is not just the first generation, but also the possibility for them to continue through generations, constantly producing new stories.

Many years later, the sounds of human voices have spread beyond the original riverbank.

Settlements appear at the foot of mountains, on plains, and in forests. Children follow the elderly to learn how to draw water, identify fruit, and build shelters, and when they grow up, they teach their experience to younger people. No one remembers the exact spot where every ancestor first stood up, but everyone knows that life comes from the yellow earth in Nüwa’s palms.

With more people come new problems. Two settlements both need a spring. The upstream people build a fence first, and soon the downstream people have no water to draw. Both sides come to ask Nüwa to judge, hoping she will simply declare who owns the spring.

Nüwa does not assign an owner for them. She lets both sides explain their needs, then they jointly inspect the spring’s daily water flow. The upstream people discover that their fence causes water to spill and waste in low-lying areas, and the downstream people admit that if they draw water without any limits, everyone will fall short during the dry season.

They agree to first clear the wasted water channels, then take turns drawing water, prioritizing the elderly, children, or the sick. This agreement is not perfectly free of dispute, but when the next disagreement arises, people already know to first clarify the facts, then find a way for both sides to survive.

Another settlement argues over the boundary of a fruit forest. Nüwa asks only one question: “Who planted the trees?”

Both sides say they were left behind by ancestors, planted together.

“Then why are the fruits enough for only one side?”

People see many fruit pits carelessly discarded in the forest, so they decide to expand planting together, rather than just guarding the existing fruit trees and pushing each other. Nüwa steps aside, letting them distribute the care tasks themselves.

Aman walks through various places carrying the green bamboo slips. He first goes to the original riverbank, recording Nüwa watching the water, taking yellow earth, and personally molding humans; he also draws a vine rope on the slips, marking how mud splashes toward different mountains and rivers, showing that everyone possesses the same life.

A child comes over and asks, “Am I also made from a mud splash?”

Aman says, “You were born and raised by your parents.”

“Then do I still have a connection to the yellow earth?”

Nüwa answers from nearby: “Your ancestors stood up from the land, and the grains you eat also grow from the land. The connection does not disappear just because many generations have passed.”

The child squats down and touches the ground, seriously asking if the earth remembers everyone’s name. Aman holds the green bamboo slips a bit further away, lest they be treated as a place to write down the names of all humanity: “I don’t know if the earth remembers, but this slip certainly can’t hold them all.”

Everyone laughs. Then, the child finds a small piece of wood and crookedly carves the titles of his family members. The young historian does not write for him, nor does he treat this as another act of creating characters; he simply records that humans have already learned to actively preserve each other’s memories.

New generations are constantly born, and old generations gradually grow old. Families connect with each other into larger groups; when floods come, they move to high ground together, and during harvests, they exchange food. Humans still make mistakes, argue, and feel fear, but they no longer wait for Nüwa to descend from the sky to solve every problem.

Nüwa returns to the place where she first watched the water. The river surface can still reflect her face, but the bank is now covered with different footprints. The first hand-molded ones, the crowds splashed from the vine rope, and the children later born from families have all drunk water here; no one is ranked below others because of their different origins.

At the end of the green bamboo slips, Aman writes only the complete cause and effect: the heaven and earth were silent, Nüwa watched the water to determine form, molded mud figures from yellow earth with her hands and gave them life; later she swung the vine rope to splash mud, sending more people to different mountains and rivers; finally, she taught people to establish families and reproduce on their own.

At dusk, cooking smoke rises simultaneously from all directions. Some call their children home, some sing songs learned during work, and others sit in a circle telling the story of how the first sound of “human” rang out by the riverbank.

Nüwa no longer sits alone, waiting for no one to answer her calls. She isn’t the judge of every tiny dispute either; she watches from afar and, when needed, nudges living things to cherish one another. Meanwhile, humans spread out across mountains and rivers, filling the once‑silent world with countless new voices.
