When Zhu Yingtai first asked her father to let her go to school, he assumed she was just complaining that the family library was too small.

"I’ll have someone buy a few more scrolls," her father said.

"Buying more books just means more books. I want to go to the academy, listen to the master teach, and debate with others."

He cited the long distance, the dangers of the road, and the fact that the academy only accepted male students. Though her mother knew her daughter was bright, she also worried that such a journey would invite gossip.

When he questioned her on classical texts, she answered point by point; when he asked about mathematics, she used the family’s grain accounts to demonstrate; and when he asked how she would care for herself on a long trip, she listed her clothing, travel funds, lodging plans, and expected return date.

Zhu Yingtai brought out a set of men’s clothes she had prepared long ago. Wearing men’s clothes didn’t make her a different person, nor did her identity limit her desire for knowledge. As she stepped out of the house, she turned back to bow to her parents, then continued along the road to the academy.

She did not yet know who she would meet on the road, nor did she know that this pursuit of learning would lead to a bond that would span several years, eventually colliding with a marriage contract and social laws that could not be easily changed.

On the third day of the journey to the academy, mountain rain soaked the path.

Zhu Yingtai took shelter from the rain in an old pavilion and found that the bottom of her book bundle had already gotten wet. As she was untying it to rewrap it, a young scholar ran in, clutching his own scrolls. He pointed to the driest spot and offered it to her.

"I am Liang Shanbo, also heading to the academy," the scholar said.

Zhu Yingtai gave her name, pronouncing "Zhu Yingtai" as if it were an ordinary man’s name. Seeing her bundle was loose, Liang Shanbo helped find some dry straw to prop up the books. Together, they protected the books and waited for the rain to ease before continuing.

Ahead, a wooden bridge had been washed askew by the water, leaving only a narrow section passable. Liang Shanbo went first to test the bridge, but halfway across, he turned back. Zhu Yingtai split their luggage, and they each carried half, crossing one by one. When it was Liang Shanbo’s turn, a plank under his foot wobbled. Zhu Yingtai reached out and grabbed his bundle. The books didn’t fall, but Liang Shanbo ended up hugging the bridge pillar for a moment.

When Liang Shanbo asked how many brothers she had, she carefully answered that she was one of the older children and had sisters at home, then steered the conversation back to the master’s writings, keeping the question from probing too close to her identity.

Aman happened to be resting by the roadside. Seeing the two scholars debating as they walked, he took out his green bamboo slips to record the scene. Inside the academy, Zhu Yingtai faced a new challenge: she had to be careful with her living arrangements, bathing, and daily interactions. Zhu Yingtai suppressed a smile, and changed the direction of the wood, then applied some wax to the hinge. The door finally opened and closed quietly, and could be securely locked from the inside. Liang Shanbo didn't take the opportunity to peek inside her still-unpacked trunk, but instead asked if the bookshelf needed to be raised to prevent water damage.

The door latch was locked from the inside, and Zhu Yingtai answered the knock, then opened the door, her clothes neatly arranged. She sat on the bed, changing her shoes, and Liang Shanbo, outside, heard the noise and asked if he could help.

Liang Shanbo replied seriously, "If you can't agree, you could mark the direction with a note on the shoe tip." Zhu Yingtai took the fabric, feeling both that he was being genuinely helpful and that it was a rare kind of help that didn't pry into her secrets. She didn't tell him why she had put her shoes on the wrong feet the night before, but sewed one piece into her shoe and kept the other in her book bag.

As time passed, the two formed a routine: Liang Shanbo would block unnecessary visitors, and Zhu Yingtai would take care of morning water duties and tidy up the shared study area. Neither of them turned their help into a way to pry into each other's secrets. Her true identity remained safe, but she gained the freedom to continue studying thanks to the specific details of the door latch, their schedules, and their mutual respect.

The first night, a neighbor came to borrow a lamp and tried to push open the door. Liang Shanbo blocked the door, saying the new student was still unpacking and didn't need to check on them. He also moved his own bed to the outside, so that anyone entering or leaving would have to pass by him first. Zhu Yingtai thus avoided many hasty cover-ups, and simply appreciated his consideration.

Liang Shanbo noticed that she never wanted to join the others for a bath by the river, and asked if she was afraid of water. Zhu Yingtai looked at him, saying, "Liang brother asks this question, the bridge probably has different opinions." Liang Shanbo thought for a moment, then decided not to press the issue.

In the first exam, the teacher asked the new students to explain a passage. Zhu Yingtai gave a precise explanation, and Liang Shanbo provided simple examples to support her points. Their answers complemented each other, and the teacher assigned them to sit together. From then on, they studied together.

After class, Liang Shanbo said that they had been walking together and studying together, like fate. Zhu Yingtai replied, "If Liang brother doesn't put his ink in his sleeve next time, this fate might not have to wash one more piece of clothing."

Liang Shanbo looked down, and saw that his sleeve was already stained with ink. He apologized sincerely, as if Zhu Yingtai had saved a valuable manuscript.

From then on, the two became like brothers, officially becoming study partners. Liang Shanbo admired Zhu Yingtai's learning, thinking of her as a young, refined, and careful scholar, without suspecting her true identity. Their bond began with protecting the same book, walking across the same bridge, and sitting together at the same desk.

Their days together weren't just spent at the desk.

When the teacher explained a passage, Liang Shanbo was good at applying the principles to everyday life, while Zhu Yingtai could find three layers of meaning in a single sentence. They often argued over the meaning of a single character, but eventually combined their ideas to form a more complete answer.

The next day, Zhu Yingtai’s father told her that the Ma family had already sent a betrothal gift.

She finally understood the meaning of “different arrangement.” Her father had admired the Ma family’s status, and the elders of both families had already agreed on a marriage; they were just waiting for her return to set a date. Zhu Yingtai firmly declined, saying she had already pledged her heart at the academy.

Her father pressed for the name. She took off her male disguise, confessed her years of study, and revealed Liang Shanbo’s name. The father was furious—not only because his daughter broke the betrothal, but also because she had hidden a male classmate from the family. He believed the Liang family’s status was insufficient, that Liang Shanbo had no idea of his true identity, and that this love was merely a one‑sided longing.

“He’ll come soon,” Zhu Yingtai said. “I asked Jiù mèi to invite him when I sent my farewell at the eighteenth gathering.”

“Even if he comes quickly, it’s still later than the Ma family’s betrothal.”

The betrothal letter, the gifts, the reputations of both families, and the father’s orders were all intertwined. Zhu Yingtai couldn’t just refuse a gift and gain freedom. She asked to return the gift, but her father refused; she was willing to explain to the Ma family herself, but the father feared the news would spread and bring shame to the Zhu family.

Liang Shanbo did indeed come.

He brought modest gifts and, as agreed, sought an audience with Jiù mèi. The family initially wanted to block him at the door, but Zhu Yingtai insisted on meeting him. She changed back into her female attire and entered the hall; Liang Shanbo rose to greet her, waited a moment, and then looked outside the door.

“Where is Jiù mèi?” he asked.

“Brother Liang has known me for years—why would I need to look elsewhere?”

Zhu Yingtai recounted the time they sheltered from rain in the pavilion, the books they protected on the bridge, the shared table at the academy, and the eighteenth gathering. Liang Shanbo finally pieced together all the clues. The woman before him spoke with the same tone and gaze as Zhu Yingtai, and he was first shocked, then silent with the realization of his long‑standing misunderstanding.

“Yingtai,” he said, calling her name for the first time.

That single word gave their years of friendship a new, undeniable reality. Zhu Yingtai explained why she had disguised herself as a man and confessed her feelings. Liang Shanbo did not blame her for the deception; he simply asked if she would marry him.

“I will,” Zhu Yingtai replied. “But the Ma family’s betrothal is already set.”

Liang Shanbo immediately asked Zhu’s father for a marriage proposal. He explained that the two of them had known each other as classmates, that his feelings were not based on a fleeting appearance, and that he was ready to formally marry her. Zhu’s father still rejected, citing the prior betrothal and the difference in status, even suggesting that if they had acted earlier, they might have negotiated.

That statement carried more weight than a simple refusal. Liang Shanbo had indeed arrived late—late in not understanding the hints, and late in sealing the Zhu family’s fate before the betrothal.

He and Zhu Yingtai had a final conversation in the courtyard. They confirmed their love but found no way to dissolve the betrothal that day. Zhu Yingtai could not run away with him, because that would involve her mother and family and turn their love into a flight; Liang Shanbo also did not want to harm anyone with force.

When the mother saw no one around, she persuaded her husband to think about it for another night. The father stared at the already registered betrothal gifts and said that the Ma family had many relatives; if the Zhu family broke the promise now, not only would his daughter suffer rumors, but future marriages of younger relatives would also be affected. The more he spoke as if planning for the whole family, the clearer it became to Zhu Yingtai that everyone could find a reason in the betrothal, but no one had asked her if she was willing.

When they left, Liang Shanbo turned back to look at Zhu Yingtai. At the academy’s farewell, he had thought there was still a celebration ahead; now that the truth was revealed, the celebration belonged to the Ma family. Zhu Yingtai stood inside the door, no longer speaking in riddles, and simply wished him well.

Liang Shanbo finally realized that Jiù mèi was Zhu Yingtai, and the two of them were clearly in love; the Ma family’s betrothal did not disappear because of the confession. From that day on, all laughter stopped.

After returning, Liang Shanbo formally invited a matchmaker to the Zhu family.

He prepared all the proper rituals his family could offer, and, unlike the last time he was rejected, he did not sigh on his sickbed. The matchmaker brought a recommendation from the academy’s professor, detailing Liang Shanbo’s character and talent. Zhu’s father listened but still did not relent: the Ma family’s betrothal was public, and withdrawing it would bring the shame of a broken promise.

Liang Shanbo came again, only to be blocked at the door. Zhu Yingtai could only hear his voice from the inner courtyard. She begged her father to let them meet, but he refused, only allowing her to write a letter of renunciation.

Zhu Yingtai did not write a renunciation. In the letter she said that her feelings would not change, but the betrothal could not be dissolved by her alone; if Liang Shanbo were harmed because of it, she would be even more burdened. She also made it clear that she did not want him to hold a grudge against the Ma family, because what truly kept them apart was not a single person to defeat, but an entire system of orders, status, and rituals.

The letter reached Liang Shanbo, who read it for a long time. His reply simply said he would still wait and asked her to take care of herself. After that he kept pleading, but his health grew weaker each day. At first he had trouble sleeping and eating, and later he could not even finish the walk to the Zhu family’s house.

The family brought a doctor; the medicine eased the pain for a while but could not lift the heaviness in his heart. Liang Shanbo still kept Zhu Yingtai’s letter beside his pillow, occasionally reading the old academy texts. The words they had once debated together remained unchanged, but the writer was now separated by the walls of two families.

When Zhu Yingtai learned of his illness, she begged to visit him. Her father, worried about a pre‑marriage scandal, forbade her from leaving. She asked a trusted friend to bring herbs and an old handkerchief, and she wrote, “When we were classmates I could not speak plainly; at the eighteenth gathering you did not understand me. Now every sentence is clear, yet we still cannot meet.”

When Liang Shanbo received the letter, he could barely get up. He had his family help him sit, and he wrote his final reply. His strokes were shaky, but the meaning was clear: he did not blame her, nor did he regret knowing her; if the laws of this life could not allow them to stay together, he only wished she remembered that the academy had once held a love that did not depend on status.

While writing to the end, he had wanted to ask one more question, “If I had understood earlier, would things have been different?” The pen hovered for a long time, and finally he did not write it. That question would have pushed all the blame back to the eighteenth gathering, but it could not make the Ma family withdraw the betrothal, nor could it open the Zhu family’s door for her. He simply drew a tiny butterfly in the corner of the letter, like the one that once fell on the window in the academy’s spring.

The letter had not yet been sent when Liang Shanbo’s condition worsened dramatically. The family stayed by his bed; he looked toward the Zhu family’s direction and softly called her name. After his breath grew weaker, he never woke again.

Liang Shanbo died in the presence of his family’s watchful care.

The news of his death reached the Zhu family; the needle and thread in Zhu Yingtai’s hand fell to the floor. She unfolded the letter Liang Shanbo had not yet sent, read it word by word, and did not cry. Her father still insisted on keeping the marriage date, because canceling now would only invite more gossip.

She saw the tiny butterfly in the corner of the letter and gently stroked it with her fingertip. Her mother stayed beside her, trying several times to persuade her, but found that any comfort felt like adding a lock to a door already closed; in the end she only kept the letter safe for her daughter.

Zhu Yingtai agreed to wear the wedding dress, but she set one condition: on the day of the wedding, the procession must detour by Liang Shanbo’s grave so she could pay her respects to her classmate. Her father thought that after the ceremony she could break her heart, and finally agreed.

Liang Shanbo had already been buried, and the Ma family’s sedan chair was ready. Zhu Yingtai would pass his grave as a bride, making her final choice between the set betrothal and the dead man’s eternal rest.

On the day of the wedding, Zhu Yingtai wore her wedding dress.

The flower carriage set off from the Zhu family, with drums and gongs echoing along the road. Inside, the bride sat with the curtains still closed, clutching only Liang Shanbo’s last letter. The Ma family sent a messenger to urge the procession forward, but the Zhu family, honoring their prior agreement, insisted on detouring around Liang Shanbo’s grave.

As they approached the grave, the sky grew darker. Wind whipped up fallen leaves, scattering the drumbeats. The carriage driver, worried about an impending downpour, tried to quicken his pace, but Zhu Yingtai in the carriage called out, “Stop.”

She disembarked and walked to Liang Shanbo’s tomb. The name on the headstone was surrounded by fresh earth, as if the schoolmates, the “Eighteen Farewells,” and the late‑arriving marriage proposal were all being buried beneath that stone. Zhu Yingtai took out her offerings and spoke each unfinished thought aloud to the dead.

The Ma family clerk stepped forward, saying that detouring for a brief incense offering was already a generous concession, and that they could only burn incense for a moment. Zhu Yingtai turned and asked, “When Liang Shanbo was alive and you said the marriage was sealed, you didn’t need a good time. Now that he’s dead, do I have to wait for a lucky hour to finish my words?” The clerk was taken aback and stepped back, yet he still urged the drums to keep going. The drummer lifted his mallet, but no one was willing to strike the first beat.

The Zhu relatives spread out the altar. Zhu Yingtai did not recite the short eulogy written by others; instead she pulled out a page they had copied together years ago. The top half of the paper contained Liang Shanbo’s annotations, the bottom half her replies, the ink already varying in depth. After reading that passage, she added, “You said we had to unite to complete the answer. Now that half is gone, who do you want me to ask?”

She said that back then she didn’t dare speak plainly, not because of lack of affection; she said that after returning home the Ma family had already sealed the marriage, and every act of defiance was blocked by her father’s orders; and she said that when she heard of Liang Shanbo’s illness and death she realized that the living are not always better able to choose their path than the dead.

The bride‑gathering pressed from behind. The Ma family clerk reminded her that the lucky hour was passing, and the Zhu relatives urged that after the ceremony she should return to the carriage to complete the marriage. Zhu Yingtai did not look back.

Her father’s men lifted the carriage curtain again, signaling her to board. Zhu Yingtai saw the red carpet inside, but in the center lay a crease from Liang Shanbo’s letter. The living’s ceremony was meticulously arranged, yet no space was left for her own will. She slid the letter back into her sleeve and poured all the sacrificial wine onto the grave.

“Brother Liang,” she said to the headstone, “I couldn’t walk beside you in life; can I enter now in death?”

The wind suddenly intensified, thunder rolled from the clouds. The ground before the grave trembled, and a crack opened in the new earth, as if the darkness below was replying. The crowd recoiled, but only Zhu Yingtai stepped forward.

Relatives reached out to stop her. Zhu Yingtai removed the outer layer of her bridal gown, breaking the barrier. She refused to let the Ma family’s wedding dress become her final identity, pasted Liang Shanbo’s letter to her chest, and looked toward the split grave door.

“Yingtai, no!” her father’s men shouted.

She paused. If she returned to the carriage, she would become the Ma family’s new bride as the contract dictated; if she walked toward the tomb, her life would end. The rites offered no path for her to stay alive with Liang Shanbo.

Zhu Yingtai did not look back again; she leapt into the grave.

She crossed the edge of the crack, her silhouette disappearing into the earth. The wind and rain fell, the split grave closed again, and soil and stone sealed the entrance tightly.

The bride‑gathering froze in the rain. The carriage still sat by the road, but the Ma family’s marriage ceremony could never resume. The Zhu relatives shouted, dug at the earth, yet the grave door never opened again.

Zhu Yingtai, by giving her life, rejected the imposed marriage and never returned to the roadside carriage.

When the rain eased, a tiny fissure appeared in the closed grave soil. No voices came from the crack, but two faint wingbeats fluttered. Everyone stopped their motions and stared at the new earth.

From the fissure, a butterfly emerged.

It hovered for a moment in the post‑rain air, its wings reflecting the spring light of the academy’s flowers. Then another butterfly sprang from the same spot, circling the first. The two flapped in tandem, then soon flew side by side.

No one in the bride‑gathering spoke. The bride who had been urged by drums had already fallen into the grave, Liang Shanbo had long since died; only the two butterflies lingered together after the rain.

The butterflies flew past the headstone, looping around the carriage that had stopped by the road. The Ma family clerk tried to wave them away, but in the eyes of all present he lowered his hand. The marriage contract could dictate the carriage’s direction but could not command where the wings would land.

The Zhu relatives chased the butterflies a few steps, then finally stopped at the field’s edge. Her father’s men bowed, took back the marriage document that was to be read at the Ma family gate, and no one pressed for the lost lucky hour. The drummer laid his mallet flat on the drum, the red silk for the bride was dampened by rain, and the once rigid ceremony suddenly lost its intended guests.

The butterflies flew along their path toward the fields. They skimmed over a small bridge, a pond, and a blooming meadow, as if retracing every unheeded hint from the “Eighteen Farewells.” This time no one mistook the pair for a mere race, and no one again asked who “Jiu Mei” truly was.

Aman arrived at the grave as the rain had stopped. He listened to Zhu Yingtai’s funeral rites, the grave’s split, and her leap into the earth, then saw the two butterflies together in the distance. The green bamboo slip in his hand was open, but the carving knife had not yet touched the paper.

He closed the bamboo slip for a moment.

In the academy’s early days he had mistaken their quarrels for mutual study encouragement and had joked that “the classmates were full of respect.” After the Ma family’s marriage contract, all those misunderstandings lost their humor. Liang Shanbo understood too late, Zhu Yingtai clarified too late, and the two living paths were cut off by ritual and death.

Aman opened the slip again, writing only the facts: Zhu Yingtai, disguised as a man, studied with Liang Shanbo; the “Eighteen Farewells” hinted at affection; after returning home she was forced into the Ma family’s marriage; Liang Shanbo, knowing the truth, failed to propose and died ill; Zhu Yingtai, on her way to the bride‑gathering, performed the funeral, the grave split, and she leapt in; the two became butterflies and departed together.

He folded the bamboo slip against his chest, stepped back behind the crowd at the grave. The knowledge, delayed realization, and resoluteness belonged only to Liang Shanbo and Zhu Yingtai.

The butterflies flew farther and farther, still chasing each other in the same wind. Workers in the field looked up, and later they told more people the story of Liang and Zhu’s butterflies. Some were moved by true love; others asked for the first time: if the living had fewer class distinctions and coercion, would they not have to wait until death to be together?

The carriage was carried back, leaving the rain‑wet offerings at the grave. The butterflies did not erase Liang Shanbo’s death or Zhu Yingtai’s leap; they simply let the love that refused to be rewritten by marriage continue on.

Later, every spring, neighbors who saw the butterflies together would remember the empty carriage that had gone back. Young people spoke of Liang and Zhu’s acquaintance; elders sighed that neither family had ever stepped back. The story spread farther, and the “ducks,” the butterflies, and “Jiu Mei” from the “Eighteen Farewells” were finally understood by later generations.

Sunset light fell on the butterflies’ wings as they crossed the last field’s edge. Aman watched them disappear and then wrote “Butterfly Transformation” at the end of the bamboo slip. After finishing, he paused for a long time.

The academy’s old laughter had faded; all that remained were the accompanying butterflies and the tragic aftermath that living people must face.
