The great mountains Taihang and Wangwu loomed right in front of Yu Gong’s house.

On a clear day the peaks piled upon each other like a wall of stone, as if the northern sky had been bricked up. Yu Gong lived north of the mountains and wanted to go south toward Yunan or the Han River, but he couldn’t head straight for his destination; he had to wind far around the foothills. The smoke from a kitchen just beyond the ridge was visible, yet half a day’s walk still wouldn’t get him there.

One morning the family needed to deliver a cartload of fresh grain to relatives on the southern side of the mountains. Before dawn they hitched the wooden cart, and even though Yu Gong was nearly ninety, he walked alongside it. The narrow path first slipped through a field of loose stones, then hugged the mountain base and turned east. After only a short distance the wheel jammed between two boulders.

Several grandchildren pushed the cart, and the wheel only squeaked. Yu Gong ordered the grain sacks off, had each bag carried over the stone slope, then lifted the empty cart over the obstacle. By the time the grain was re‑loaded, the sun had risen to the summit and the way beyond the mountains was still hidden behind Taihang.

Aman, who had just arrived from Kunlun, happened to see them hauling the cart a second time. He held a green bamboo slip and asked, “If we’re heading south, why does the road turn east first?”

Yu Gong’s grandson pointed ahead and said, “Because the mountain won’t let us.”

The junior chronicler looked up for a moment, then down at his slip: “If those two peaks moved any closer together, we’d probably have to pay to go around.”

He kept talking while his feet kept moving. One side of the foothill was a sheer cliff, the other a tangled ditch; the bamboo slip would scrape a rock edge if he didn’t hold it tight. Aman had intended to note “one grain delivery,” but by afternoon he realized that one trip also included hauling the cart, carrying grain, finding a path, and re‑loading—so he added many lines to his slip, while the family only managed to skirt half a mountain.

At dusk they finally reached the southern side. The relatives took the grain and let the group stay the night, but Yu Gong, thinking of the livestock back home, rested briefly and set out for the return. As darkness fell on the way back, the grandchildren lit pine twigs; the firelight only illuminated a short stretch of road beneath their feet. Their own houses occasionally peeked out from mountain gaps on the opposite side, as if deliberately saying, “We’re not far,” while the mountain road stretched that promise into a long, lingering trek.

The youngest grandson, exhausted, sat on a stone and asked, “We live right over there—why does it always feel like we have to go so far?”

No one answered at once. Everyone knew the answer was right there, massive enough that it needed no explanation.

When they got home, night had deepened. The cart’s wooden wheel rim had split open, two corners of the grain sacks were torn, and the family didn’t even wait for a hot meal. Delivering grain was supposed to be a routine chore, but the Taihang and Wangwu mountains turned it into an all‑day, all‑night ordeal.

Yu Gong didn’t go straight inside. He carried a pine twig back to the doorway, walked a short way along the foothill, and let the firelight reveal the pine‑covered soil, tree roots, and exposed stone layers below the slope. He crouched, scooped up a handful of earth, let the fine soil slip through his fingers, and tapped a stone with his wooden staff.

Aman asked, “Are you looking for a shorter bypass?”

“I’ve been looking for years,” Yu Gong said, “the more we wind around, the more familiar the path becomes, but the mountain never shrinks.”

“Then what can we do?”

Yu Gong stared at the mountain swallowed by night and said, “The mountain blocks me, and it will block my descendants. If everyone only learns to go around, it will stay right here forever.”

The junior chronicler had been about to note that the two great mountains didn’t seem too nervous after hearing this, recalled his own record of Jingwei picking up stones one by one for the Eastern Sea, and clipped a joke in half. Tiny things aren’t automatically funny; at least you have to see how long the person is willing to try.

Yu Gong drew a short line on the pine‑covered soil with his staff. It was tiny compared to the whole mountain, but it pointed straight from his front door toward the southern side.

“Tomorrow we’ll gather the family,” he said. “This time we won’t discuss how to go around; we’ll discuss how to make the mountain give way.”

The next day the whole family sat together in the courtyard.

Yu Gong placed a stone he’d taken from the line he’d drawn yesterday on the mat and explained his plan: “We’ll level Taihang and Wangwu together, carving a road straight to Yunan and Han Yin. Starting at the doorway, we’ll dig basket by basket.”

The grandchildren were stunned at first, then all nodded. They’d already gone around the mountain more than once, and everyone knew how painful the blockage was. The youngest grandson even ran to fetch a hoe, as if moving fast enough would outpace the mountain’s resistance.

Yu Gong’s wife, however, did not join the cheers.

“I don’t oppose your intention,” she said softly, the wrinkles at the corners of her eyes spreading like mountain ridges, yet carrying a faint, hard‑to‑spot worry. “But you’re old; you can’t even pull up a single weed on the mountain, let alone shift two huge peaks. How will you move them?” She set the bamboo basket she was holding gently on the threshold; inside were only a few broken stones, a reminder—“Where will the earth and rock you dig up go?”

Yu Gong lifted his hoe; its wooden handle caught a dim glint in the morning light. He laughed and hammered the hoe into the ground, the “dong‑dong” sound like a drumbeat of resolve. His wife shook her head but couldn’t help laughing, “Don’t move the obstacle in front of our house onto the neighbor’s doorstep, or they’ll end up moving with us.”

In the yard, the neighbor’s old ox was lazily chewing grass. It looked up at Yu Gong, snorted, and seemed to say, “That old man wants to move a mountain—how many baskets of rice can we still carry?” The ox’s low moo made several children burst into giggles, their laughter echoing through the valley.

Yu Gong put the hoe away, turned to his wife, and nodded, his eyes flashing determination. “If I can’t finish it, my descendants will keep digging while the mountain stays the same; why stop now?” His wife patted his shoulder gently, her voice soft yet strong: “Fine, I’ll help you pile the soil into a little hill for the next generation to take over.”

The courtyard fell silent, only the rustle of pine branches in the wind. Her words weren’t defeatist; they were a reminder of the challenges that would appear the moment work began. If they couldn’t answer them, moving the mountain would be nothing but a burst of enthusiasm. So Yu Gong and his wife exchanged a smile, the hoe struck the ground again with a thud, soil flew up, laughter mingled with the chopping sound, and the new mountain‑moving adventure quietly unfolded in the peaceful yard.

The courtyard fell quiet. Her question wasn’t a discouraging remark but a prompt about the immediate hurdles that would appear once they started. If they couldn’t answer, moving the mountain would be just a flash of excitement.

Yu Gong first answered with manpower: “I’ll dig as much as I can. After I’m gone, my son will dig, his son will dig, and the descendants will keep at it. It’s not for me alone to finish in my remaining years.”

His wife pointed at the stones: “What about these?”

“We’ll send them to the far end of the Bohai Sea, to the hidden north of the earth. There the ground is low and far away, a place where the earth and rock can be put down without blocking anyone.”

“One trip will take a long time.”

“So we can’t just have one basket and shovel; we can’t all leave the mountain at once either. Some will dig, some will load, some will transport, and we still need people at home to tend the fields.”

Hearing this, the grandchildren stopped swinging their hoes wildly. They fetched old baskets, checked the bottoms and the rope knots; some repaired wooden handles, others sharpened dull iron hoes, and some cleared the thorns along the short line drawn yesterday. The first concrete division of labor for moving the mountain was set.

Aman sat in the corner of the yard taking notes; the bamboo slip first read “Whole family agrees,” but after hearing the wife’s follow‑up question he crossed out the word “immediately.”

Yu Gong asked why he had altered the character.

“Just now everyone answered as if the mountain had already shrunk a little,” the junior chronicler said, “When the lady asked those two questions, I realized the mountain hadn’t shrunk, and the talk steadied.”

Hearing that, his wife picked up a leaky basket and handed it to him: “Since you’ll be recording, note that this one can’t be used.”

Aman quickly took it but didn’t dare treat it as a tool; he only added “first fix the basket bottom” on the slip. He was a witness, not a decision‑maker, and he couldn’t use the green bamboo slip to scoop soil; but looking at that broken basket, he finally understood that the first grand step in moving a mountain was simply finding a suitable vine.

The whole family spent the entire afternoon preparing. The wife sorted dry provisions by distance, reminding those heading for Bohai not to rush the journey; the grandchildren chose the first slope at the foothill with the most pine‑covered soil, to avoid breaking their hoes right away. Yu Gong walked back and forth from the front door to the slope, making sure the future opening would line up with the southern road.

Before sunset he gathered the family again at the line he’d drawn.

“We can still change our minds today,” Yu Gong said. “The mountain is huge, the road is long; this won’t be a one‑day effort.”

The eldest son drove the repaired hoe into the earth: “Exactly because it won’t block us for just one day, we should start today.”

The grandchildren placed the repaired basket beside it. The wife didn’t promise the mountain could be moved, but she handed a sack of dry food to the person who would haul soil tomorrow: “If you’ve asked about the difficulties and still go ahead, don’t starve yourself trying to be brave.”

Yu Gong nodded. He didn’t announce a finish date, nor did he try to sweep away the long road and high peaks with a boastful line. After the whole family examined the labor, the stone and soil destinations, and the living arrangements, they all chose together to move the mountain.

At night the tools were left in the hallway, their wooden handles casting shadows across the floor. Taihang and Wangwu were still as massive as ever, and the mountain wind still pressed down from the heights. But for the first time, no one stood at the doorway debating which path to take; they had already decided where to start digging.

At first light, the very first iron hoe struck the foot of Taihang Mountain.

The family began to dig according to the assignments they made the day before.

The blade cut into the loose earth with a dull thud. Yu Gong shovelled the loosened soil to the side, and his children and grandchildren quickly swept it into the baskets. The first basket filled fast, and everyone’s faces brightened; but when they lifted it, the old seams at the bottom leaked a trail of fine soil, and by the time they reached the pile only a little more than half remained.

His wife patched the seams with vines, and Yu Gong moved to a new spot to dig. The second swing of the blade struck a buried tree root, and the wooden handle vibrated so hard his arm went numb. The younger men rushed to chop, but he first had everyone loosen the soil around the root so they wouldn’t keep hammering the same hard spot.

After the loose soil was cleared away, a gray‑white stone layer was exposed. The iron hoe could only scrape off a few fragments, and the slope that had seemed easy at first suddenly turned stubborn. The crew switched to iron picks to pry at stone cracks, used wooden levers to lever out smaller rocks, and separated the broken stone from the mud. Those who loosened soil couldn’t jump ahead to fill baskets, and those hauling stones had to wait for a clear spot to set down. The work gradually fell into a rhythm.

Aman cradled a green bamboo slip at the slope’s edge, carefully recording the first day’s progress. He first drew the original base line of the mountain foot, then sketched the new notch—two lines almost touching.

“How much have we taken away?” Yu Gong’s grandson asked.

The junior scribe lifted the slip to look from afar, then leaned in: “Just a polite little bit, as if we’re afraid to wake the mountain.”

Everyone laughed for a moment, but the work kept going. Aman stood by the pile of stones, looking up at the shallow trench cut by the hoe, and silently noted how each clod of earth turned into a mud ball after rain, then hardened into a crust under the sun. When old Yu Gong swung his iron shovel, the blade often got stuck in the roots, making a muffled “creak‑creak.” Beside him, his grandson Little Yu quickly handed over the shovel handle, grinning, “Grandpa, the shovel’s like a little snake has wrapped around it!” Yu Gong patted his grandson’s shoulder and laughed, “As long as we’re not scared of snakes, we can move the mountain.”

At that moment, an old wise man standing at the mountain’s foot shook his head, twirling a bamboo staff, and scoffed, “You old fellows, moving a mountain is like moving a house—why waste all that screw‑tightening effort?” Aman raised an eyebrow, and in a low voice he rewrote on the slip, “The notch may be shallow, but it’s no longer the same.” He lightly dotted the ink, the tip picking up a bit of mud, but he dared not write “useless,” lest he disappoint the sweating elders.

The hoe kept sinking into the earth, only to bounce back when a rock was too big. Yu Gong’s palms grew calloused, while his granddaughter Little Mei giggled, smearing mud from her hands onto his forehead and teasing, “Grandpa, your forehead is tougher than the mountain!” Yu Gong looked toward the distant Taihang and Wangwu and replied softly, “Our descendants may be endless, but the mountain never grows. If we stop, the mountain stays.”

While everyone labored, a divine light from the Heavenly Emperor slipped down onto the summit, a gentle breeze stirring up dust as if listening to the endless clatter of hoes and laughter. The crew kept digging at their steady pace, their chuckles, the thud of the hoe, and the distant wise man’s jeers weaving together into a song of perseverance.

In the afternoon a widowed neighbor came with her little child to watch. The child saw peers hauling stones and picked up a small rock to put in his own basket. His mother didn’t let him touch the iron hoe; she only let him carry the little stone across the flat ground to the temporary stash. He ran back and forth quickly, each trip bringing a few stones, and soon the basket bottom was a mosaic of pebbles.

“Your house isn’t even right at the mountain’s mouth, why help?” someone asked.

The child answered, “I have to go around it to get to school, too.”

That simple reply prompted the neighbors to bring vines, dried food, and old wooden levers. No one claimed they could actually move the mountain; they just saw the path blocking their own lives and pitched in where they could.

By late afternoon a deeply embedded stone still wouldn’t budge. Several grandchildren took turns prying, the wooden lever slipping each time. Yu Gong didn’t force a flashy first‑day result by brute‑shoving; instead he cleared the soil beside the stone, wedged a small wooden shim into the crack, and applied pressure from the opposite side simultaneously.

The stone finally popped out of its place and rolled into a shallow pit they’d prepared. The group didn’t let it roll further down; they looped a rope around it, pulled it together, and slowly hauled it onto level ground. It would become one of the first rocks shipped to the Bohai Sea.

When the sun slipped behind Wangwu Mountain, everyone paused to count. A shallow opening just wide enough for two people to stand side by side had appeared at the slope’s foot, with a dozen baskets of earth and a few stones piled nearby. Compared with the two great mountains, the change was almost invisible; compared with the sunrise, the hoe now had a definite place to rest.

Aman finished counting the baskets, his brush hovering, “If I record only this little bit each day, this bundle of slips will grow into a third mountain before I’m done.”

Yu Gong chuckled, “Then we’ll write one slip after another, and the mountain will shrink basket by basket.”

The junior scribe wrote down the words without exaggerating the progress. The first day of moving the mountain had no miracles, no trembling peaks—just patched baskets, uprooted roots, pried‑out hard stones, and neighbors who had only watched now bending to pick up rocks.

Before leaving, Yu Gong drove a wooden stake into the edge of the notch to mark how far they’d come today. They wouldn’t pick a new spot at the mountain’s foot tomorrow, nor announce another “start”; the first hoe of the next day would begin right after that stake.

After the first batch of earth and stone settled at the mountain’s foot, the long‑distance transport began.

Yu Gong selected the family members who could handle the trek, loaded earth into the baskets, and tied stones onto wooden frames. The rest stayed home to tend the fields and kept loosening the next section of mountain soil. If the whole family left, the mountain‑moving would cut off their livelihood.

They set out toward the far end of Bohai and the hidden lands to the north. In the first few days the road was still smooth, but the baskets on their shoulders grew heavier by the step. The edges of the baskets constantly rubbed against the ropes, so each day they stopped to check the knots. An old rope snapped while crossing a ditch, sending a stone tumbling to the slope’s edge; everyone rushed to block it, replaced it with a spare vine rope, and from then on they inspected every rest stop twice.

Aman trailed at the back of the convoy, his green bamboo slip wrapped in two layers of cloth. He had thought the hardest part to write would be the digging, but after a few miles he realized “carrying the mountain soil away” could fill another whole bundle of slips.

“If we just dumped it back at the mountain’s foot,” he said as they walked, “you’d probably have finished many rounds already.”

Yu Gong’s son replied, “That’s not moving a mountain; that’s just giving the mountain a new foot.”

The junior scribe carved that line down. True mountain‑moving isn’t measured by how many times the hoe hits the ground, but by where each basket of earth finally ends up.

Along the way a long rain fell. The muddy road stuck to their straw shoes, and each step of the wooden frame had to be pulled out of the muck first. They couldn’t rush, so they temporarily set large stones on a dry high spot, sent the lighter baskets of earth over the low ground, then went back for the stones. A route that should have been a single trek often required three round‑trips.

After night fell they sheltered under trees. The dried provisions had hardened from the damp, so they soaked them in hot water to soften and share. Someone sighed looking at the wooden frame, “That load of earth is already far from Taihang, yet the mountain still looms behind us.” Yu Gong didn’t say, “If you can’t see it, it’s done,” but reminded them, “If a stone never returns to its place, the mountain truly loses that stone.”

When the rain stopped, the grass beside the road deepened from tender green to richer hues. The convoy pressed on, and the seasons changed beneath their steps. Reaching the far end of Bohai, the sea breeze hit them, and the mud on the baskets had dried into light‑colored hard clods. As agreed, they unloaded earth and stone onto the lowlands north of the hidden lands, basket by basket, never dumping obstacles in anyone else’s doorway.

No one cheered when the first empty basket was set down. After a day of bearing weight, the lightness on their shoulders already spoke enough that a small piece of the task was truly done.

The return trip carried no earth or stone, but it wasn’t easy either. They had to retrace the path to retrieve repaired ropes, the spare wooden levers, and the markers they’d left. When someone’s foot wore a blister, the group took turns supporting them; the empty wooden frames made perfect carriers for those who couldn’t walk far. By the time they reached home, the leaves had already changed color.

The family who stayed behind didn’t stop working. The shallow notch had been pushed a little farther inward, and new piles of earth and stone gathered beside it. When the travelers returned and saw the new piles, they first laughed, then sighed: the first batch had just been delivered, and the second batch was already waiting.

Aman unfolded the bamboo slip he’d written before setting out and noticed the ink on it looked older than the fresh leaves. He said, “You’ve hauled a load, and even the seasons have changed clothes. If the mountain could count the days, it’d probably pretend it didn’t notice.”

Yu Gong leaned the empty basket against the wall and said, “It doesn’t count; we do. After we keep going one trip, we’ll know where the rope will snap and where we’ll need to duck for rain. The next trip won’t start from cluelessness.”

That day the group didn’t climb the mountain again. They first dried the ropes, patched the bottom of the baskets, and told the others what troubles they’d found along the way. The experience of long‑haul transport was written into the next round of duties: the elders guarded the home and repaired tools, the strong ones carried earth, the young handled the stone frames, and a spare vine rope had to be kept on hand.

The next day Yu Gong returned to the wooden stake and kept loosening the soil, moving forward along the existing notch. The first batch of earth and stone had truly left Taihang and Wangwu. The long back‑and‑forth proved that moving a mountain slowly isn’t just about digging; as long as each trip reaches the agreed spot, the slow pace isn’t just spinning in place.

When another batch of earth and stone was being loaded, He Qu Zhi‑sou came down to the foot of the mountain.

He first looked at the freshly dug notch, then at Yu Gong’s white‑haired face, shook his head and laughed, “You’re not very clever. With the little strength you’ve left, you can’t even move a single blade of grass on the mountain, yet you still want to move Taihang and Wangwu?”

A few young men stopped their iron hoes. A neighbor’s child, holding a small basket, stood aside with only a few pebbles inside. Zhi‑sou pointed at them and said, “You dig away a little each day, but that’s just a fine hair on the mountain. When the elders are gone, who will remember what you said today?”

Aman sat beside the wooden stake, having just finished writing the day’s progress. He glanced at the tiny numbers on the slip, then at the peak that seemed unreachable, and whispered, “If it’s only compared to today, that number really feels like it was made just to scratch the mountain’s itch.”

Yu Gong heard this and didn’t scold. He steadied his iron hoe, turned to face Zhi‑sou, and said, “You see my age, but you don’t see how far my children are from the widow’s house.”

Zhi‑sou replied, “Could a few more stones make the mountain fear you?”

“The mountain won’t fear me, and I’m not comparing courage with it,” Yu Gong answered. “I’ll die, but I’ll have a son; the son will have a grandson; the grandson will have more descendants. The line of descendants goes on forever. The mountain, however, won’t grow taller or add a third peak. The descendants are endless, the mountain is not. Why should I be upset about that?”

Zhi‑sou was about to laugh, but his expression stopped.

He weighed the situation, looking only at how many years the old man had left. Yu Gong weighed every generation continuing to dig the notch left by the previous one. If everyone pushed forward from the same wooden stake, the basket of today wouldn’t have to return to the mountain tomorrow. Even after Yu Gong’s death, the part already dug would not revert.

“How can you guarantee that all the descendants will want to do it?” Zhi‑sou asked again.

“I can’t guarantee it for those not yet born,” Yu Gong said. “So I let today’s descendants see me working. If I just sat at the gate and asked later people to move the mountain, they would only learn to say; if I start the hoe, they’ll have a place to take over.”

“Even so, many generations might never see the mountain level.”

“Then let many generations do it. The road is meant for many generations to walk. Why should the road be finished by just one generation?”

Those words didn’t make Taihang shrink immediately, but they shifted Zhi‑sou’s questions in every direction. Old age doesn’t mean action can only last one life, and slow progress doesn’t mean every day resets to zero. What truly causes a mountain‑moving effort to fail is when each generation, unable to see the end, refuses to leave the first hoe.

Aman didn’t continue the debate for Yu Gong. He crossed out the phrase “scratch the mountain’s itch” at the corner of the slip, and with the tip of his pen drew a thin arc, carving in the middle “the descendants are endless, the mountain is not.” When the old pen reached “endless,” the tip suddenly fell off, like a fine white hair dropping into the ink pot. Aman lowered his head, picked it up, frowned, muttered, “You really know what it means to get less and less.” He then slipped that hair into the pocket of his cloth bag, as if he were storing a tiny gem.

A grandson beside him just threw the hoe back over his shoulder; a chunk of earth slipped from his hand into the nearby basket with a “plop,” startling the neighbor’s child who shouted, “Wow, the mountain really drops pies!” Everyone laughed together, but only for a moment; the laughter was short. Yu Gong already picked up the heavy iron hoe again, his back slightly hunched, yet he still stood firmly at the foot of the mountain. The descendants stopped their play and returned to their digging spots, the clatter of hoes producing a crisp metallic sound.

The neighbor’s child tipped the small stones from the basket into the scoop; as the stones rolled, they made a “dong‑dong” sound, like little drumbeats. Then he hopped away to pick up another basket, filled with soft mud, wiping it on his knees as he walked, turning himself gray‑yellow, which made the old woman nearby laugh out loud, “This kid really moved the mountain into his own yard!” The laughter echoed through the valley, but it was swallowed by Yu Gong’s hoe‑clatter, leaving only the echo of persistence.

The tense crowd laughed, but the laughter was brief. Yu Gong had already picked up the iron hoe again, and the descendants had returned to their positions. The neighbor’s child tipped the small stones from the basket into the scoop, then went to pick up another basket.

Zhi‑sou stood for a long time. He saw that the stake beside the notch wasn’t just a single stick; behind it were old marks left a few days earlier. He saw that the empty basket returned from long‑haul transport had been patched. He saw that the family didn’t wait for Yu Gong’s command to start. This thought truly began with the old man, but it was no longer just his hands.

He finally had nothing to say, turned and left. Yu Gong didn’t chase after him to demand an apology, nor did he treat the debate’s outcome as new progress. The mountain still lay ahead, and the next hard stone would not automatically loosen just because Zhi‑sou stayed silent.

The sound of the iron hoe resumed. The notch that had been mocked today was still small, but the question of “who can finish it” had a different answer: it’s not about how much one person can dig, but whether the descendants are willing to let the work that has begun continue forward.

As the notch deepened, the soil became less, and the hard rock increased.

The ground that could still be turned with an iron hoe at first, later became only a series of tight stone layers. When the hoe blade struck, a spark flashed, leaving only shallow marks on the stone. Someone kept hammering, the wooden handle cracked first; another hoe soon dulled, its edge could no longer bite the stone cracks.

Yu Gong didn’t just make the people swing the hoe faster. They took all the broken tools back to the yard, reinforced the wooden handles that could be repaired, and alternated sharpening the iron blades. The young people searched the stone layers for natural fissures, first cleared fine soil from the cracks, then drove iron spikes in, using a wooden lever to pry slowly from both sides. Large stones couldn’t be lifted whole, so they were split along cracks into manageable pieces.

After the first frost, the soil layer froze tight. In the morning, swinging the hoe only shook the handle, so everyone incorporated timing into the division of labor: when the day was warm, loosen soil and pry rock; when the ground was hard in the morning, move the stone blocks already packed from yesterday; when snow blocked the road, stay home to weave baskets, tighten ropes, and sharpen hoes.

Persistence was no longer repeating the same action regardless of circumstances. It meant remembering when to dig, when to repair, which stone crack had already loosened, and which long road could not be taken after rain. Every adjustment was to let the next labor build on the existing result, not to exhaust tools with brute force.

Seasons turned, and the stake at the notch moved deeper into the mountain. The old stake stayed behind, marking where they had dug. Spring grass once grew beside the stake, summer was trampled flat; autumn wind blew over the empty basket, winter snow fell into the stone cracks. The peak remained high, but a deep man‑made cleft appeared at the foot of the mountain.

Yu Gong grew even more aged than when he started. He no longer always swung the hoe first; instead he taught his descendants the stone layers he’d tried: which sound meant there was a cavity below, which stone needed to be dug from the side first, how to tie a knot so it wouldn’t slip on the slope.

The eldest son took the heaviest lever, the grandchildren learned to split earth and stone, the neighbor’s child grew taller and began carrying farther loads. The wife still checked the dry food and the basket ropes, and would point out in person when someone tied a knot that only “scared a timid stone.” Everyone laughed, then tightened again, not treating the reminder as a blow to morale.

One year, the rain fell especially hard, and a freshly loosened slope was washed away by a mudflow, covering half the notch. The family looked at the mud and initially thought all the days of work had been buried. Yu Gong poked with a wooden stick and found that the old stone layer hadn’t returned to its original place, only new mud covered it.

They first dug a small drainage ditch, letting the accumulated water flow out through the gaps on both sides, and then cleared away the newly fallen debris. Thanks to their past experience, this time they were able to repair the damage much faster. Despite the increased rainfall, they didn't send everyone back to square one.

The relay teams gradually took shape. Before the previous team returned, the ones left behind would prepare the next batch; those who had returned would rest and recover, then hand over the damaged tools to the repair team. Each round built on the previous one, leaving behind the road, ropes, stakes, and lessons learned.

Sometimes, while sitting at the foot of the mountain to catch his breath, the old man would watch the young people carrying stones past him. He knew he might not live to see the two mountains disappear, but he no longer thought of this as a failure. The action had shifted from a single old man's decision to a family affair, and his own age was decreasing, while the number of participants and their experience were increasing.

The Taihang and Wangwu mountains, of course, didn't bow their heads after a few storms. They still cast a massive shadow over the house, and from a distance, it seemed as though nothing had changed. But if you walked into the gap, you could see the layer upon layer of old scars stretching deep into the mountains.

The old man handed the first wooden stake to his grandson, letting him move it to its new position. This wasn't just a meaningless phrase passed down; it was clearly handing over the question of "where to continue" to the next generation. The grandson received not only the iron shovel but also the reduced mountain stones and the unbroken direction.

In the Taihang Mountains, there was a snake god who had long guarded the two mountains.

At first, when the old man and his family started digging, the mountain god didn't pay much attention. Human iron shovels clattered at the foot of the mountain, making a faint sound, and a gust of wind could easily cover up the marks of the floating soil. But after many seasons, he noticed that the wooden stakes kept pushing deeper into the mountain, and the soil and stones were carried away and didn't return; the old man retreated to the back, and the young people took over at the front.

The snake god was unsettled not by the old man's words, "My descendants are endless, but the mountain won't grow," or by the old man's determination to continue, but by the fact that the old man's words were true: people would come and go, but the action could continue; the mountain wouldn't get any taller, and if a piece was removed, it would be one piece less.

The mountain god didn't send a giant stone rolling down to hurt anyone or transform into a human to argue with the old man. Instead, he left the Taihang Mountains and went to heaven to report the situation to the Emperor of Heaven: the old man, now nearly ninety, was still digging away, and his family and neighbors had taken turns, generation after generation. If this continued, the Taihang and Wangwu mountains would eventually be worn down.

The Emperor of Heaven didn't make a decision based solely on the fact that "an old man wanted to move a mountain." He saw the old wooden stakes at the foot of the mountain, the repaired baskets and tools, and the tracks leading to the Bohai Sea, where the soil was being transported. He also saw that the old man had already handed over the most forward position to his descendants. Although human strength was small, the old man's determination was not a fleeting emotion but a steadfast resolve that had endured through ridicule, long journeys, hard rocks, and the changing seasons.

"They're not asking to move the mountain to someone else's doorstep," the Emperor of Heaven said, "but to open up a path for future generations. If divine power were to intervene at the first shovel, it would only make the promise untested; now that they've proven they won't stop, there's no need to let countless generations waste their time on the same obstacle."

The Emperor of Heaven then ordered the two sons of the goddess Doumu to descend to the mortal realm. The two brothers were incredibly powerful and arrived in the southern part of Hebei Province without driving away the old man and his family. Instead, they first examined the roots of the two mountains and the areas where they could be safely relocated. One brother stood to the side of the Taihang Mountains, and the other stood behind the Wangwu Mountains, each supporting the mountain body.

The old man and his family heard the low rumble coming from deep within the mountains and immediately retreated from the gap they had dug. The ground shook, but it wasn't a collapse; the Taihang Mountains first rose from their roots, and the old wooden stakes and the gap they had dug were clearly visible; on the other side, the Wangwu Mountains were also lifted up, and the cloud-covered peak moved accordingly.

The two sons of the goddess Doumu each relocated one of the mountains to a new location: one to the eastern part of Shanxi Province, and the other to the southern part of Shaanxi Province. The massive mountains crossed over the original path, not pressing down on the villages or falling into the Bohai Sea. They were placed in a location where they no longer blocked the old man's door or the southern road.

After the Taihang and Wangwu mountains were lifted, the sky that had been hidden behind the two mountain shadows opened up. The sun shone directly on the old man's doorstep for the first time, and its rays followed the gap they had dug for many years, illuminating the south. The family stood in the open space, and no one spoke for a moment.

The old man didn't attribute the divine assistance to his own strength. He bowed his head to the sky and looked at his descendants behind him. If they hadn't dug the mountain for so long, the mountain god wouldn't have been afraid, and the Emperor of Heaven wouldn't have seen their determination; if the Emperor of Heaven hadn't ordered the divine assistance, the mortals would have had to continue for many generations.

The snake god looked at the empty space where the mountains used to be and finally understood that it wasn't the iron shovels that he had feared but the word "unrelenting." The great mountains could overcome a person's strength, but they couldn't rely on not growing to exhaust the determination of generation after generation.

The clouds in the sky closed in, and the two sons of the goddess Doumu completed their mission and left. After the Emperor of Heaven ordered the divine assistance to be removed, the southern part of Hebei Province, all the way to the Yin River, was no longer blocked by the original high mountains.

The old man's family didn't continue to swing their shovels to prove anything. They put down their tools and walked forward along the newly revealed path. The ground still had the fragments of stone and soil left from their many years of labor, but the path ahead was now straight.

On the morning after the mountains were moved, the old man pushed open the door of his house and stood there for a long time.

In the past, opening the door would mean walking into the shadows of the Taihang and Wangwu mountains. Now, the house, fields, and trees to the south were visible in a single line of sight. The youngest grandson ran a few steps along the old path, then turned back to the straight road, still getting used to not having to detour.

The whole family walked along the new road, no longer needing to lift carts or navigate through the mountain gaps. They used the small stones they had collected to fill in the shallow holes and smoothed out the path for walking and carts.

Aman held the green bamboo slips tightly, following closely behind the old man. His footsteps left shallow impressions on the soft mountain slope. The wind rustled through the bamboo slips, causing the pages to flutter, as though a bird was afraid of being blown away. Aman looked down carefully, placing the bamboo slips on a stone next to the first wooden stake, gently tracing the edges of the pages, which had been worn smooth by the rain. Then he looked up at the empty space ahead, his eyes darting back and forth between the marks on the bamboo slips and the depressions in the ground, as though playing a game of hide-and-seek, afraid of missing any subtle differences.

"This stone is harder than the potatoes in our fields," Aman muttered, but he carefully pressed the corners of the bamboo slips flat to prevent the wind from blowing away the characters.

He flipped to the next page of the bamboo slips, carefully checking each stroke and line, even sticking his fingers into the depressions in the ground to feel the cool soil, to make sure he hadn't taken a wrong turn on the mountain path. Just then, a neighbor, Old Sage, hobbled over, leaning on his cane, muttering, "Ah, old man, why do you and your family insist on digging away at the mountain? The mountain won't move, and life will go on as usual."

Aman looked up, his eyes shining, and replied to the old sage, "My descendants are endless, but the mountain won't grow. If we don't move the two mountains, future generations will still be blocked by them." His words echoed through the valley, as though the characters on the bamboo slips had become a real promise.

The old sage laughed, his voice like rustling leaves, shaking his head and saying, "Your family's determination, passed down through generations, seems like you're treating the mountain like a toy."

Aman didn't hurry, carefully putting away the bamboo slips, but a hint of a smile played on his lips, as though he was saying, "Toys also need to be moved so that children can run farther."

He continued to pace back and forth beside the wooden stake, occasionally looking up at the distant Taihang and Wangwu mountains, mentally noting the distance of each step, waiting to pass on this record to future generations. At that moment, the wind picked up again, causing the bamboo slips to flutter, as though the Emperor of Heaven was listening from afar, moved by this unyielding determination.

"And what about the Taihang Mountains?" a neighbor's child asked, pretending to be curious.

Little Official pointed to the east, "They've been moved. The Wangwu Mountains have been moved too. You two are faster at writing than I am, but if you had told us earlier that you wanted to move them, I could have saved on those bundles of bamboo slips."

The old man smiled and said, "Those bundles couldn't be saved."

The bamboo slips recorded the broken baskets, hard stones, the long journey to the Bohai Sea, the broken ropes after the rain, and the old sage's ridicule. If it had only been the Emperor's order to move the mountains, people would have thought that the old man had said a single sentence, and the two mountains would have immediately given way.

He flipped to the new bamboo slips of the "Twenty Bamboo Slips of Mountains and Seas," writing "Taihang and Wangwu blocked the road" and "The old man led his family and neighbors to dig away at the mountain, never stopping." Those small, almost imperceptible daily progressions, combined, became the evidence that moved the Emperor of Heaven.

Aman remembered Jingwei carrying a stone and a piece of wood to the East Sea. One was meant to fill the sea, the other to move a mountain, but it wasn't because the first stone was big enough; it was because it would come back after being put down. He scribbled a note in the corner: "Next time you see something small, ask if it will keep growing, don't laugh at the big mountain just yet."

At the end of the new road, the southern relatives of the mountain people could see them from far away. The cries they used to hear through the peaks were now carried clearly on the flat land. The wooden cart went straight south for the first time, its wheels not getting stuck in the rocky mountain base; the wife of the foolish old man sat beside it, reminding everyone not to forget to tie up their food bags just because they were close to their destination.

Someone asked the foolish old man, "If you had known the Heavenly Emperor would help you, why did you dig for so many years?"

The foolish old man replied, "I didn't know the Heavenly Emperor would come. I only knew the mountain was in my way, and if I didn't dig today, I'd have to go around it again tomorrow. If I just waited for the gods to notice, there would be nothing for them to notice."

The little historian added at the end: what moved the Heavenly Emperor was not a bold idea, but the foolish old man turning it into a long-term effort; the two mountains were carried away by divine power, but the sincerity of a human heart made the divine power respond.

The wise old man came back to the new road later and stood for a moment next to the first old wooden post. The foolish old man didn't ask him if he had given up, and the road that let everyone pass was more useful than winning an argument.

Aman wrote "children are endless, but the mountain doesn't grow" in the most visible place on the whole text. The old pen was finished, but there were still a few hairs left; he couldn't help but praise, "You've managed to stick with it until the mountain was moved."

The mountain wind blew in from the open area, almost blowing away the green bamboo slips. The little historian frantically protected them, and the foolish old man's grandson helped him press down the outermost piece. He immediately clarified, "Protecting the slips is okay, but I'm not going to sneak in the credit for moving the mountain."

Laughter echoed along the new road. The foolish old man looked at his children, neighbors, and the carriages that no longer had to go around the mountain. Years of hard work had made something that would outlast his lifetime possible, and it was finally seen by heaven and earth.

The Taihang and Wangwu mountains were no longer in their original place, and the road was truly connected. What the foolish old man left behind wasn't just the new location of the two mountains, but a scale that wouldn't give up because of slow progress: one person's efforts are limited, but children are endless; the mountain may be high and big, but it doesn't grow. As long as each generation continues from the wooden posts left behind by their ancestors, the distant result will eventually arrive one day.
